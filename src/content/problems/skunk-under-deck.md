## What's really going on under there

A skunk doesn't dig itself a den from scratch when it can move into one you've already built. A deck, a shed on skids, a porch, a set of concrete steps with a hollow underneath — each is a dark, dry, roofed cavity with a soft dirt floor, sheltered from wind and coyotes, with an easy spot to dig in along the edge. To a **striped skunk** looking for a maternal den in the cold end of winter, that's prime real estate. Very often it takes over an existing woodchuck or rabbit burrow that already runs under the structure, saving itself the work.

So the animal under your deck isn't lost or unusual. It found exactly the kind of den skunks seek, on a property that happens to offer it. Understanding that reframes the fix: the goal isn't to punish one skunk, it's to close the den so this one leaves and the next one can't move in.

## The season that drives the whole problem

Skunk timing runs on a tight, predictable schedule in Wisconsin, and it dictates everything about how — and when — you evict one. Skunks **breed in late February and March**. You'll notice it: the roadkill count climbs and the musk turns up on cold nights as males roam. Gestation runs about two months, so **kits are born around May**, in litters of four to seven, and they stay dependent in the den for weeks before they're mobile enough to follow their mother out to forage.

That May-to-midsummer window is the one that turns a straightforward eviction into a trap. Seal a den with a nursing female excluded and her kits stranded, and they die under your deck — which means weeks of odor seeping up through the floor and a cleanup that involves getting under the structure to remove them. This is precisely why a real skunk job starts by determining whether young are present. Late winter, before kits arrive, and late summer, once they're mobile, are the clean windows; the middle stretch is handled with care around the litter, not against it.

## Identifying an active den

The burrow entrance is the surest sign — a **hole four to eight inches across**, usually snugged against the deck skirt, shed foundation, or step, often with a spray of fresh dirt fanned out below it. Around an active den you'll notice a **worn path** at the entrance and a **low, persistent musk** in the soil even without a full spray event. Out in the yard, look for the skunk's foraging signature: **shallow, cone-shaped divots** punched into the lawn where it's been digging for grubs and earthworms overnight, and small pits in mulch beds.

The clincher is watching at dusk. Skunks are crepuscular to nocturnal, so a resident will emerge around sundown and return before dawn along the same route. If you see that come-and-go against the deck, you've got a tenant, not a passer-through.

## Why this is common in our area

This is grub country. Skunks forage heavily on **white grubs and earthworms** in lawn and pasture, and the well-watered lawns, old orchard ground, and pasture edges around Clintonville and the lake communities produce them in quantity — which supports a healthy skunk population pressed right up against yards and outbuildings. Combine that food base with the local building stock — decks and porches on older homes, sheds set on blocks or skids, pole barns and outbuildings with dig-in gaps, and the woodchuck burrows that riddle rural ground — and you have both the food and the den sites skunks need in close proximity. Lake cabins add the twist of sitting empty in late winter, letting a female den and raise kits under a deck without anyone noticing until the season opens.

## The real fix: eviction plus a dig-proof barrier

There are two halves to solving this, and skipping either one guarantees a repeat.

The first is getting the animal out **without a spray and without stranding young**. The tool is a **one-way door** fitted over the burrow entrance: the skunk pushes out on its normal dusk trip and can't reopen it to get back in. It's calm, it happens at night on the animal's own schedule, and because nothing is chasing or cornering the skunk, it rarely triggers the defensive spray that clumsy trapping does. Before the door goes on, we confirm there's no dependent litter; if it's kit season, we plan around the young rather than force it.

The second half is what makes the fix permanent: a **buried, dig-proof barrier**. An empty burrow under a deck is still an open invitation, so after the skunk is out we run **hardware cloth around the base of the structure and bury the lower edge in an outward L-shape** — down into the soil, then bent out horizontally. When any animal tries to dig at the base, it meets buried mesh and quits. That L-footer is the difference between "the skunk left" and "nothing re-digs this spot," and it stops the next skunk, woodchuck, or rabbit just as well.

Trapping-and-relocation, by contrast, leaves the open burrow behind and risks the spray it's meant to avoid — which is why it's not our first move.

## Keeping skunks from moving back in

The buried barrier is the backbone of prevention, but a few habits keep your yard from advertising itself. **Close the gaps** under decks, porches, sheds, and steps with buried hardware cloth before an animal claims them. **Cut off the food** where you can: a lawn with a heavy grub load is a skunk magnet, so grub management reduces both the foraging divots and the draw. **Secure the buffet** — bring in pet food at night, secure garbage and compost, and pick up fallen fruit under trees. And **don't leave inviting cover**: woodpiles, brush, and junk against a foundation give a skunk a staging spot. Walk the perimeter of decks and outbuildings each spring, since a fresh dig at the base in March is the earliest warning that a female is prospecting for a maternal den.
