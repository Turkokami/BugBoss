## Rats are a different animal than mice — treat them like one

When a farmer around here tells me they've got rats, the first thing I want to confirm is that it's actually rats, because the word gets used loosely. But when it's real — and on farms it often is — it's a bigger deal than a mouse problem, and it calls for a different, more aggressive approach. In Wisconsin, a rat in a farm building means one thing: the **Norway rat**, *Rattus norvegicus*, the heavy brown burrowing rat that has followed grain and livestock across the Midwest for two centuries. The climbing roof rat that plagues warmer states doesn't establish here in the cold, so we don't have to guess about the species — and knowing it's a Norway rat tells us exactly how it lives: burrowing low, along foundations and under slabs, not nesting up in the rafters.

## Rats or big mice? Confirm it first

The difference is easy to see once you know the marks:

A **Norway rat** is a heavy-bodied animal — seven to ten inches in the body, plus a shorter, thicker tail, and it can weigh a half-pound or more. A mouse is a few inches and a fraction of the weight. **Rat droppings** are big, three-quarters of an inch, capsule-shaped with blunt ends, and you'll find relatively few of them; mouse droppings are quarter-inch, rice-grain shaped, and scattered by the dozen. The tell that clinches it on a farm is **burrows**: two-to-four-inch holes along a foundation, at the edge of a slab, under a feed bunk, or in a junk pile, often with a smooth run of packed dirt at the entrance. Mice don't dig burrows like that. You'll also see heavier gnawing and thicker, greasier rub marks where rats travel the same routes.

## Why they showed up — and why they stay

Rats need more than mice do. A mouse can get by on crumbs and condensation; a Norway rat needs a **steady food supply, a water source, and cover to burrow in**, and it goes where all three line up. A farm outbuilding often supplies exactly that: stored feed and spilled grain, livestock feed and water, and burrowing cover in the form of brush, junk, old lumber, unused equipment, and slabs to tunnel beneath. Give a colony that combination and it settles in and grows.

And it grows fast. Norway rats breed year-round when they're fed and sheltered, several litters a year, so a couple of rats and a good feed supply become a colony in a matter of months. This is why a rat sighting is worth acting on quickly — the difference between a few rats caught early and an established colony with burrows all down the foundation is the difference between a small job and a big one.

## Why they cost you more than mice

A rat colony hits a farm three ways at once. **Feed:** rats eat and, worse, contaminate far more than mice, fouling stored grain and feed with urine and droppings and rendering it unfit. **Structure:** their burrowing undermines slabs, foundations, and floors, causing settling and cracks, and they gnaw wood, insulation, and stored equipment. **Fire and disease:** they chew wiring, a genuine ignition risk in a building full of dry feed and fuel, and they carry disease and parasites that matter around livestock. Add it up and rats are a real cost, not a nuisance to shrug at.

## Why farms out here are the setting for it

Rats are less widespread in rural Wisconsin than mice — you don't find Norway rats in every woodlot the way you find deer mice — but where they do turn up, it's on farms, and the reason is concentration. A rat colony needs a dense, reliable resource base to establish, and a working livestock or grain operation supplies it in one place: bunks of feed, spilled grain, waterers running year-round, manure, and warm animal housing. That's a far richer, steadier setup than the scattered living a mouse gets by on, and it's what lets a rat population take hold and grow to real numbers.

The building stock cooperates too. Older farmsteads accumulate outbuildings over generations — the original barn, a machine shed, a granary, a milkhouse, add-on lean-tos — many on aging slabs and foundations with plenty of low gaps and undermined edges to burrow beneath. Add the junk and equipment that collect around a long-running farm, and you've got harborage everywhere. A tidy, well-fed dairy can get rats as readily as a run-down place; it comes down to whether the feed is contained and the harborage is cleared, not to how clean the yard looks.

## Why casual trapping fails on rats

Here's the thing that surprises people: rats are hard to trap, much harder than mice, and it's because of a trait called **neophobia** — a deep suspicion of anything new in their environment. Set a fresh baited trap squarely in a rat runway and the rats will route around it for days rather than investigate. The homeowner sets a couple of traps, catches nothing, and concludes the rats are gone or the traps don't work. Neither is true; the rats just haven't accepted the traps yet.

Effective rat trapping is a technique. You place the right heavy traps in the right spots along established runways and burrow openings, often set them unset and baited first so the colony gets comfortable feeding at them, and only then arm them — and you do it consistently over more than one night. That patience is exactly what casual DIY skips.

## What actually works: the whole program

Rat control on a farm is never one thing; it's several done together.

**Cut the food and water.** Get feed into rat-proof metal containers, clean up spills, fix leaks and standing water, and don't leave livestock feed accessible overnight where it can be avoided. A colony without a reliable food source is a colony you can actually beat down.

**Clear the harborage.** Remove the junk, brush, lumber, and unused-equipment piles that shelter burrows near the buildings. Cover matters as much as food; take it away and the rats have nowhere safe to den.

**Trap and bait as a managed program.** Aggressive, properly placed trapping along runways, backed by bait in tamper-resistant stations where appropriate — placed and managed carefully to protect barn cats, dogs, and raptors from secondary poisoning, and to avoid rats dying in inaccessible spots.

**Treat the burrows.** Work the burrows directly, then collapse them, and watch for re-digging, because the burrows are where the colony lives and breeds.

**Seal the buildings.** Close the low entry points — under doors, wall-to-slab joints, penetrations — with materials that stand up to gnawing and digging, since a rat will widen a gap a mouse would merely slip through.
