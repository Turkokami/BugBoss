faqs:
  - q: "How do I know it's flying squirrels and not regular squirrels?"
    a: "Timing settles it, and you can do this yourself tonight. Grey and red squirrels are daytime animals — noisy at dawn, active through the day, silent after dark. Flying squirrels are strictly nocturnal, so the noise starts within minutes of sunset, carries on through the night, and stops around dawn. That is the exact opposite pattern. The sound is different too: flying squirrels are small and light, so people describe fast scampering and soft thumps rather than the heavy rolling and loud gnawing of a grey squirrel."
  - q: "How many are up there?"
    a: "Far more than you would guess from the noise. Flying squirrels den communally through the winter because huddling together conserves heat, and colonies of twenty to thirty in one attic are routine in this part of Wisconsin. This is the single most important thing to understand about the problem: it is not one animal that got in. It is also why people trap three or four, enjoy a quiet week, and then find the noise is back — because twenty-odd were left."
  - q: "How did they get onto my roof? There are no branches touching it."
    a: "They glided. A flying squirrel has a membrane of skin between its wrists and ankles, and launching from a tall tree it can cover well over a hundred feet in a controlled glide, steering with a flattened tail. It never touches the ground and never climbs your wall. This is why the standard advice to trim branches back — genuinely useful against grey squirrels — helps far less here. You cannot stop a flying squirrel reaching your roof. You can only stop it getting through it."
  - q: "How small a gap do they need?"
    a: "About an inch and a half — roughly a golf ball — and they will enlarge something smaller by chewing. Being much smaller than grey squirrels, they exploit defects other squirrels simply cannot fit through: the corner where a soffit meets a fascia return, the gap behind a sagging gutter, a lifted shingle at a valley, damaged screening on a gable vent, the junction where a dormer meets the main roof. Plenty of houses that have never had a grey squirrel problem host a flying squirrel colony for years."
  - q: "Will they leave on their own in spring?"
    a: "No. This gets confused with bats, which are seasonal. Flying squirrels are resident year-round. The winter group loosens as the weather warms and females separate to raise litters, but the site stays in use and they come back — and there are two breeding periods here, spring and late summer, so an occupied attic tends to become permanent rather than resolving on its own."
  - q: "Can't I just set traps?"
    a: "Trapping alone almost never resolves it, for two reasons. The numbers — removing five from a colony of twenty-five leaves twenty and the noise continues. And more importantly, trapping does nothing about the hole, so the site stays available and gets reoccupied, often the same season, because a warm dry insulated attic beside good habitat is prime real estate. Exclusion is what ends it: get every animal out, then seal the roofline properly."
  - q: "Is there a time of year you shouldn't evict them?"
    a: "Yes — when there are dependent young in the attic. Flying squirrels breed in spring and again in late summer, and sealing the adults out while non-mobile juveniles are still in the nest leaves them to die in your ceiling, which is inhumane and creates an odour and insect problem. Working out whether young are present is part of the inspection. Autumn is generally the best window: the late-summer young are mobile and the winter colony has not fully formed."
  - q: "What damage do they do?"
    a: "Mostly insulation, and it is worse than people expect because of the numbers. Colonies establish latrine areas rather than scattering waste, so you get heavily saturated patches of insulation with urine soaked through to the ceiling drywall below. Two winters of that ruins a substantial area. They are rodents with continuously growing incisors, so attic wiring is at risk too. And they carry the fleas and mites you would expect of any wild rodent, which disperse into living space when the colony is removed."
  - q: "Do they bite or carry disease?"
    a: "They are small, shy, strictly nocturnal animals with no interest in confrontation, and nobody is being attacked. The realistic concerns are the parasites they bring, which can disperse down into the house after the animals leave and cause unexplained biting, and the air-quality issue of accumulated droppings and urine-soaked insulation in an attic connected to your ventilation. Both are handled by cleaning up the site properly rather than stopping at animal removal."
  - q: "How does BugBoss get them all out?"
    a: "We confirm the species by standing outside at dusk and watching the roofline — the emergence is so tightly synchronised that the animals show us the exit themselves within minutes of sunset. Then every other opening on the building is sealed with heavy-gauge hardware cloth, a one-way door goes over the remaining active entry, and the colony pushes out over several nights and cannot return. We monitor to confirm the attic is empty, seal the last opening permanently, and clean up the contaminated insulation and residual scent — which otherwise advertises the site to the next glider."

## Why this happens

Northeast Wisconsin has a lot more flying squirrels than almost anyone realises, for the simple reason that they are strictly nocturnal and virtually nobody ever sees one. They are out there in the woods regardless of whether you know it. You find out when a colony picks your attic.

Two features of the animal explain the whole problem.

**They arrive by air.** A flying squirrel glides — not flies — from a tall tree, covering well over a hundred feet in a controlled descent and landing directly on your roof. No branch has to touch the house. No wall has to be climbed. Trimming trees back, which is sound advice against grey squirrels, does very little here.

**They are small and social.** An adult needs a gap about the size of a golf ball, which means they use roofline defects that grey squirrels physically cannot. And they overwinter in **colonies** — twenty to thirty in one attic is an ordinary finding, because huddling together is how a small animal survives a Wisconsin January.

An insulated attic, above a heated house, near mature woods, is the best overwintering site available for a hundred yards in any direction. From the squirrel's point of view your house is simply the best tree on the property.

The buildings that get them are the ones near mature trees: farmsteads with old windbreaks, homes backing onto hardwood stands, cabins in timber around Iola and the Chain O' Lakes, and village-edge properties in Clintonville, Marion and Tigerton where the woods come close. Seasonal properties are especially vulnerable — a cabin sitting empty from October to May is quiet, undisturbed, well insulated, and nobody is there to hear the first winter.

## What to do now

**Note the timing tonight.** Stand outside at sunset and watch the roofline. If animals come out within a few minutes of dusk, you have flying squirrels and you have also just found the exit. That one observation is worth more than an hour in the attic.

**Do not seal anything yet.** Sealing a roofline with animals inside is the most damaging thing you can do — trapped squirrels chew hard to get out, doing real damage, and any that fail die in the structure.

**Do not rely on repellents or ultrasonics.** Nothing on a shelf competes with the value of a warm dry attic in January, and animals habituate within days.

**Look at the ceiling below.** Staining on drywall means a latrine area above it and tells you roughly where they are living.

**Note whether it is breeding season.** Spring or late summer means dependent young may be present, which changes what can responsibly be done and when.

## When to call

Call when the attic **comes alive at dusk and goes quiet at dawn** — that timing alone is worth the phone call, because it points at a colony rather than a stray animal and the two need completely different work.

Call if you have already **trapped a few and the noise came back**.

Call if you find **soiled insulation or ceiling staining**.

Call if **biting starts** in the rooms below after the animals leave — that is parasites dispersing, and it means the site needs treating.

And call **before sealing anything**, because the order of operations is the difference between a resolved job and animals dying in your ceiling.
