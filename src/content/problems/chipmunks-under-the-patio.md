faqs:
  - q: "Can chipmunks really damage a patio or wall?"
    a: "Yes, and it is the reason to take a small population seriously. Chipmunk burrow systems are extensive — many feet of tunnel with multiple entrances and chambers — and when that network runs under a slab, a stoop, a patio or behind a retaining wall, it removes the compacted material those structures bear on. Over a few seasons you get settling, cracking, tilting pavers and, behind retaining walls, bulging where the backfill has been undermined and water now channels through. A single animal rarely matters. An established colony does."
  - q: "How do I know it's chipmunks and not something else?"
    a: "The entrance is the tell. Chipmunk holes are clean, round, about two inches across, and — crucially — have no mound of excavated soil around them, because they carry the spoil away in their cheek pouches to keep the entrance inconspicuous. Ground squirrel and rat burrows usually leave a spoil pile. Mole and vole work is different again: moles push up ridges and conical mounds, voles run at grass level with no digging. A clean two-inch hole with no dirt around it, next to a hard structure, is a chipmunk."
  - q: "How many are down there?"
    a: "Usually more than you see at once. Chipmunks are territorial and largely solitary, so a burrow system typically holds one adult, but a favorable site supports several adjacent systems — and a patio, retaining wall or rock landscaping offers a lot of adjacent real estate. People commonly report seeing one and then, once they start watching, realize there are five or six working different entrances. Counting entrance holes gives a better estimate than counting sightings."
  - q: "Why my yard?"
    a: "Cover, food and easy digging, in that combination. Rock landscaping, retaining walls, stacked stone, woodpiles and dense foundation plantings all give protected cover with ready-made voids. Bird feeders are a major draw — spilled seed under a feeder will support a chipmunk population indefinitely, and it is by far the most common attractant we find. Add loose backfill behind a wall or under a patio, which is far easier to tunnel than undisturbed ground, and the site is close to ideal."
  - q: "Will they get into my house?"
    a: "Occasionally, and usually by accident rather than intent — chipmunks are not looking to live indoors the way mice are. Where they do cause building problems is by burrowing along foundations, getting into crawlspaces through gaps, and occasionally into basements or garages. The more common indoor consequence is a chipmunk that has got in and cannot get out, which is a different and more frantic problem. Sealing ground-level gaps handles both."
  - q: "Do they hibernate? Will the problem stop in winter?"
    a: "They go dormant rather than truly hibernating — chipmunks spend the winter in the burrow living off cached food, waking periodically to feed. So the visible activity stops and people assume the problem has resolved, but the animals and the burrow network are still there and both return in spring. Winter is actually a reasonable time to do barrier work on a structure, since you are not disturbing active young, though the ground being frozen has practical limits."
  - q: "Can I just flood the burrow or use a repellent?"
    a: "Flooding a burrow under a patio or behind a retaining wall is a genuinely bad idea — you are introducing water into exactly the backfill whose stability you are trying to preserve, and washing out fines makes the settling worse. Repellents perform poorly against an animal with an established burrow and a food source, and they need constant reapplication. Neither addresses the tunnel network, which remains as a void whether or not the animal is in it."
  - q: "What actually stops them coming back?"
    a: "A physical barrier plus removing what drew them. Hardware cloth buried along the edge of the structure and turned outward at the base stops re-tunnelling at the vulnerable line. Then take away the food — move or remove bird feeders, or fit catch trays and clean up spilled seed — and reduce the cover by clearing woodpiles and thinning dense plantings against the wall. Removal without the barrier and the food changes means a new chipmunk takes the vacant system within a season."
  - q: "Do I need to worry about the existing tunnels?"
    a: "Sometimes. If there is visible settling, cracking or a wall beginning to bulge, the voids themselves need addressing as well as the animal — that is a landscaping or masonry job rather than a pest one, and we will say so plainly. Where it is caught early and the structure is sound, sealing off re-entry and letting the ground consolidate is usually enough. The point of dealing with it early is precisely to stay in the second category."
  - q: "When should I call?"
    a: "When you count more than a couple of entrance holes near a hard structure, or when you see any settling, tilting pavers or movement in a retaining wall. Also worth calling before you build — if you are putting in a patio, stoop or wall on a property that already has chipmunks, incorporating a barrier during construction costs very little and saves the whole problem. Spring and autumn are the practical windows for the work."

## Why this happens

A chipmunk in the yard is genuinely charming and most people are pleased to see one. The problem is not the animal, it is the **burrow system**, and it is invisible.

Chipmunk burrows are far more extensive than their size suggests — many feet of tunnel, several entrances, storage chambers and a nest. And they are dug to be inconspicuous: chipmunks carry excavated soil away in their cheek pouches rather than leaving a spoil heap, which is why the entrance is a clean two-inch hole with no dirt around it and why people walk past them for months.

That network becomes a structural problem when it runs under something that bears on the ground:

**Patios, stoops and slabs.** The compacted base is what the slab sits on. Tunnel through it and you get settling, cracking, and tilting pavers.

**Retaining walls.** The backfill behind a wall is loose and easy to dig, which makes it ideal chipmunk habitat and terrible news for the wall. Undermined backfill leads to bulging, and water starts channelling through the voids, which accelerates everything.

**Foundations, steps and rock landscaping.** Same mechanism, smaller scale.

Yards get colonised because they offer three things at once. **Cover** — rock landscaping, stacked stone, walls, woodpiles, dense foundation plantings. **Easy digging** — loose backfill and base material are far softer than undisturbed ground. And **food**, which is very often a bird feeder. Spilled seed under a feeder will sustain a chipmunk population indefinitely, and it is the most common attractant we find on these calls.

Chipmunks are territorial, so a burrow usually holds one adult — but a good site supports several adjacent systems, which is why people who start watching realize there are five or six rather than the one they had noticed.

## What to do now

**Count the entrance holes** rather than the animals. Clean, round, roughly two inches, no soil mound. That gives you a far better sense of scale, and it maps the network.

**Look for movement in the structure.** Settling at a patio edge, a paver that has started to tilt, a crack in a stoop, a bulge or lean in a retaining wall. That tells you whether this is early or late.

**Deal with the bird feeder.** Move it well away from the structure, fit a catch tray, or take it down for a season. This is the single highest-value change on most properties and people are always reluctant to make it.

**Clear the cover** — woodpiles away from the wall, dense plantings thinned back from the structure.

**Do not flood the burrows.** You would be putting water into the exact backfill you are trying to keep stable, and washing out fines makes settling worse.

**Do not wait for it to resolve.** It does not. The animals go dormant in winter and come back in spring, and the tunnel network persists either way.

## When to call

Call when you count **more than a couple of entrance holes** around a patio, stoop, wall or foundation — that is a colony rather than a visitor.

Call at the **first sign of settling**, tilting pavers or movement in a retaining wall, because early is much cheaper than late.

Call **before you build**, if you are putting in a patio, stoop or wall on a property that already has chipmunks. A barrier incorporated during construction costs very little and prevents the whole problem.
