  - q: "Is a bird nest in a dryer vent actually dangerous?"
    a: "Yes, and this is the part people underestimate. A dryer vent blocked by nesting material stops exhausting properly, so heat and lint build up in the duct — and dryer lint is extremely flammable. Blocked dryer venting is a recognised cause of house fires. On top of that, a dryer that cannot exhaust runs longer, costs more, and can push moist air back into the house.     a: "Listen and look. Chirping or scratching from the wall behind the dryer, especially early morning, is the giveaway once the chicks hatch. Inside, the dryer takes far longer than usual and the vent flap no longer opens when it runs. A vent hood whose flap is propped permanently open is nearly always holding a nest."

## Why this happens

Every spring, starlings and house sparrows go looking for a cavity to nest in. A dryer vent is close to ideal from a bird's point of view: sheltered, elevated, out of the weather, warm, and defended from predators on all sides. The hood flap that is supposed to keep things out swings open easily, and once it is propped by the first few sticks it never closes again.

They build fast — a packed mass of grass, twigs, feathers and litter, wedged into the duct — and they build a lot. A single season's nest can fill a substantial length of the run.

Two things make this different from other bird problems.

**The fire risk is real.** A dryer vent blocked with nesting material stops exhausting. Heat and lint accumulate in the duct, and lint is highly flammable. Blocked dryer venting is a recognised cause of house fires, and a nest is one of the most complete blockages a duct can get.

**They come back.** Starlings and sparrows return to a site that worked, year after year. Clearing the nest without guarding the opening buys you about six weeks.

Out here the pressure is higher than in town. Farmsteads and village properties sit close to open ground and outbuildings that support large starling and sparrow populations, and older houses tend to have more exterior vents in worse repair.

## What to do now

**Stop using the dryer** until the vent is cleared, if the vent is a dryer line and you have any reason to think it is blocked. This is the one urgent step on this page.

**Check the other vents while you are at it.** Bathroom exhausts, stove and range hood vents, and any unused capped vent. If one is occupied, others usually have history.

**Look, do not reach.** Identify the species and work out whether there are live chicks before anything is pulled. Pulling a nest with nestlings in it leaves you with a dead-animal problem inside a duct you cannot easily reach.

**Do not screen it with insect mesh.** It clogs with lint and recreates the blockage.

**Note the date.** If biting starts in the house a few days after a nest is cleared, that is mites dispersing — it is a known sequence and it needs the site treating, not the bedroom.

## When to call

Call as soon as you suspect a nest in a **dryer** vent — that one is a safety issue and it should not wait for a convenient moment.

Call if you can hear **chirping or scratching** in a wall or ceiling near a vent, or if a dryer has suddenly started needing multiple cycles.

Call if this happened **last year too**, because that means the opening has never been guarded and it will keep happening.

And call **before** pulling a nest out yourself if you are not certain what species it is or whether there are chicks in it.
