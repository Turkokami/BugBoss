## A chimney is a hollow tree to a raccoon — and in spring it means babies

When someone calls about scratching and thumping in the chimney in April or May, I already have a strong idea what's down there before I arrive: a **mother raccoon and a litter of kits.** It's worth understanding why, because it changes everything about how the problem gets handled. A raccoon doesn't fall into your chimney by accident. A female looking for somewhere to raise her young in the spring is searching for a large, hollow, vertical cavity — in nature, a big hollow tree — and to her, your chimney is the best hollow tree for miles: tall, dark, dry, sheltered from weather and predators, with a flue she can climb like bark. She chose it on purpose, and she brought a family.

## Why you must not light a fire

It's the instinct everyone has and it's exactly wrong. The kits down there can't climb out — they're too young, that's the whole reason the mother chose a place she has to carry them in and out of. Light a fire or set off smoke and you don't evict anyone; you kill the young, frequently the mother too, and now you have **dead raccoons decomposing in your flue** — a severe odor problem, a real cleanup and disassembly job, and a smoke-logged house on top of it. Every year somebody learns this the hard way. Please don't be that call in June asking us to remove what a March fire left behind.

## The timing: why it's almost always spring

Wisconsin raccoons **mate in late winter — roughly January through March — and give birth to kits in April and May.** From birth through early summer, the mother needs a secure den to nurse and raise them, and that's the stretch when chimney calls flood in. So if it's spring or early summer and there's a raccoon in your chimney, the safe and usually correct assumption is that there are babies present, even if you've only seen or heard the adult. Handling it as a mother-and-kits situation from the start is what keeps the job humane and keeps you from stranding a litter you didn't know was there.

## Is it really a raccoon?

Weight and sound are the tells. Raccoons are big animals, and they sound like it — **substantial thumping, heavy scratching, and climbing**, not the light skittering of a squirrel or the flutter of birds. The giveaway in denning season is the **kits**: a chittering, crying, almost bird-like chorus coming from deep down in the flue, distinct from the lighter chittering of actual chimney birds like swifts up near the top. You'll also notice the mother's rhythm — leaving at dusk to forage, returning before dawn. Put that together and it's a raccoon den, not a stray.

## How the removal actually works

With a mother and kits, the humane and durable approach usually isn't trapping — it's **getting the mother to relocate her young herself.** A female raccoon keeps several backup dens and is powerfully motivated to move her kits somewhere she deems safer. So we make the chimney feel unsafe using a humane eviction deterrent placed at the top of the flue, and over a day or two the mother carries her kits, one at a time, to another den. It works with her instincts instead of against them, and it keeps the family together.

Where that isn't workable — kits too young to be carried, or lodged on the smoke shelf — we **remove the kits by hand** from the cleanout or firebox and position them so the mother recovers them, or reunite them appropriately. What we don't do is trap and remove the mother alone, because that strands the kits to starve in your chimney, which is inhumane and just creates the dead-animal problem by another route.

Once everyone is out, the permanent step: **cap the chimney.**

## The cap is the actual fix

Removal solves this spring. The **chimney cap** solves every spring after. A cap is a sturdy metal cover with mesh sides that lets smoke and gases out while keeping raccoons, squirrels, birds, rain, and debris out. An uncapped flue is an open invitation, and raccoons return to good den sites — if not the same animal, then next year's female scouting the same great hollow tree. Cap it properly and the chimney stops being available. Skipping the cap is why people deal with this two and three springs running; installing one is why they deal with it once.

A cap earns its keep beyond raccoons, too. The same cover keeps out squirrels, chimney swifts and other birds, and the debris and rain that shorten a flue's life and cause draft and moisture problems. So while the raccoon is what prompts the call, the cap is a permanent upgrade to the chimney regardless — one of those repairs that quietly pays for itself the first time it turns away an animal you never even knew came sniffing around the roof.

## Cleanup matters too

Raccoons leave a mess, and it's not a benign one. Droppings in the smoke shelf and flue are a genuine health hazard — raccoons can carry **rabies and raccoon roundworm**, whose eggs are hardy and dangerous — so the den debris gets cleaned and disinfected carefully, not just scraped out. We also check the damper and flue for damage a raccoon can do working its way in and out, and address it before capping.

## Why it happens where it does around here

Chimney raccoons track the housing and the landscape. The older homes across Clintonville and the surrounding towns tend to have **masonry chimneys with open, unlined or uncapped flues** — the exact vertical cavity a denning female wants — and a lot of them have never had a cap installed. Meanwhile the region's woodlots, river corridors, and farm country carry a healthy raccoon population; the Pigeon, Wolf, and Little Wolf river bottoms and the wooded edges everywhere are prime raccoon habitat, so there's no shortage of females each spring hunting for a den site.

Development pressure nudges them toward chimneys, too. As natural den trees get cut and woods get cleared, the big hollow trees a raccoon would naturally use grow scarcer, and a warm dry chimney becomes the best available substitute. That's why a house that never had a chimney raccoon can suddenly get one, and why an uncapped flue near woods or water is essentially advertising a vacancy every spring. None of it reflects on the house being dirty or neglected — it's location, an open flue, and a raccoon doing what raccoons do.
