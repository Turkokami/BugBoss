## The big gray nest in the tree

That gray, papery, football-shaped nest hanging from a tree branch, tucked in a tall shrub, or stuck to the side of your house under a gable is the work of the **bald-faced hornet** (*Dolichovespula maculata*) — and it's probably the most recognizable insect nest in Wisconsin. It's also one of the more intimidating things to find on your property, because a mature one can be the size of a basketball and hums with hundreds of aggressive workers.

First, a bit of name-clearing, because it changes how you think about the problem. The bald-faced hornet isn't really a hornet at all. It's a large, black-and-**ivory-white** relative of the yellowjackets — an aerial-nesting *Dolichovespula* — and it earned the "hornet" label mostly from its size and its big conspicuous nest. Wisconsin has no established population of true hornets native to it, so when someone around Clintonville reports a "hornet nest," this is almost always what they've got. Our [wasps and hornets](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) library page lays out how it fits alongside the paper wasps and ground-nesting yellowjackets.

## How the nest is built and why it looks the way it does

The nest is a marvel of insect engineering, and understanding it explains why the wrong treatment fails. Bald-faced hornets make their gray paper by **chewing weathered wood and plant fiber and mixing it with saliva** into a pulp — you'll sometimes catch a worker rasping fibers off a gray fence board or deck rail on a summer afternoon. They build the comb inside and then wrap the whole thing in a multi-layered **papery envelope**, leaving a single entrance near the bottom. The comb, the brood, and the queen all live protected inside that envelope; the workers you see flying at the entrance are a fraction of the colony.

It starts small. In spring, an overwintered queen founds the nest alone, building the first cells and raising the first batch of workers herself. Once those workers mature, they take over construction and foraging, the queen switches to full-time egg-laying, and the nest grows layer by layer through the summer. By **August and September the nest reaches its peak** — commonly football- to basketball-sized, holding several hundred workers — which is exactly when most people notice it and when the colony is at its most defensive. The reason "spray the outside" doesn't work is that same envelope: coating the exterior of a big paper nest does almost nothing to the hundreds of hornets, the brood, and the queen sealed inside. Effective treatment has to **penetrate the envelope and reach the interior**.

## Identification and the danger

Telling a bald-faced hornet nest from other wasp nests is easy once you know the tell: it's about **architecture**. A large, fully enclosed gray nest with a smooth papery envelope, hanging from a branch or a building, is a bald-faced hornet. A small, open, downward-facing umbrella comb with the individual cells exposed, tucked under an eave or railing, is a **paper wasp** — a much smaller, milder colony. A hidden enclosed nest betrayed only by traffic at a hole in the ground or a wall is a **yellowjacket**. Nest shape and location alone will usually tell you what you're dealing with.

As for danger, bald-faced hornets take defense of the nest seriously. They react strongly to anything coming within a few feet, they'll pursue a perceived threat some distance, and like all yellowjacket relatives they **sting repeatedly** rather than dying after one sting like a honeybee. A mature colony's workers can swarm out fast when the nest is disturbed. For someone with a stinging-insect allergy, a single sting can be a medical emergency; for anyone, a mass response from a disturbed nest can deliver enough stings to be serious. The hazard scales entirely with location: a nest hanging high in a woodlot tree that nobody approaches is a low risk and a useful predator of flies and caterpillars, while a nest by the back door, over the deck, at head height in a foundation shrub, or on the corner of the house is a problem that needs dealing with.

## Why these nests turn up around here

The rural Clintonville landscape gives bald-faced hornets exactly what they want. They favor **tree lines, woodlot edges, tall shrubs, and building corners**, and a property ringed by woods, fencerows, and mature yard trees offers no shortage of nest sites. They also readily build on structures — under gable peaks, on eaves and fascia, on the sides of barns and outbuildings — so it's common to find a nest stuck to the house itself, not just in the trees.

Like all our social wasps, the colony is **annual and self-terminating**. It dies completely at hard frost, and only the newly mated queens survive winter, dispersing to found brand-new nests elsewhere the next spring. The nest is never reused — that old gray nest still hanging in a bare tree in December is just empty paper, and you can take it down in winter without any risk because the colony has been dead for weeks. But a property that suited a colony one year tends to attract a new queen the next, so seeing a fresh nest in roughly the same area season after season is normal, even though it's a different colony each time.

## Why DIY is a bad bet on these

Bald-faced hornet nests are among the worst wasp jobs to attempt yourself, for a few compounding reasons. The nests are often **high** — well up a tree or on a second-story gable — which means a ladder, and a ladder under a nest full of defending hornets is how serious falls and mass stings happen. Store-bought aerosols reach only a few feet and, as covered above, **can't penetrate the envelope** to reach the colony, so they mostly just provoke a swarm while you're standing close. And the defensive behavior of a mature colony means the margin for error is small. A high, mature bald-faced hornet nest is a textbook call-a-professional situation.

## Prevention

There's less you can do to prevent aerial hornet nests than ground or soffit nests, because bald-faced hornets build out in the open on branches and building faces rather than in cavities you can seal. The most useful habit is a **springtime check**: in late spring, before the workers arrive, look over the eaves, gable peaks, porch ceilings, and low shrubs near the house for the small starter nests a founding queen builds alone, and knock those down while it's still just a queen and a handful of cells — by far the easiest point to stop a colony. Beyond that, keeping an eye on the tree lines and building corners through the summer lets you catch a growing nest and deal with it before it reaches its August peak and its full defensive strength.
