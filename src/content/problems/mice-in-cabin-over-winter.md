## An empty cabin is exactly what a mouse is looking for

The Waupaca Chain O' Lakes and the woodlots and river bottoms around it are full of seasonal cabins — places that hum with people all summer and then go dark and quiet from October to May. That dark, quiet stretch is the problem. A cabin closed for winter isn't a home with a mouse problem; it's a purpose-built mouse habitat that happens to be yours. No people, no pets, no lights, no disturbance — just warm cavities, soft things to nest in, and often a pantry that got left in a hurry. Out in lake and woods country there's never a shortage of deer mice hunting for winter shelter, and your cabin is the best offer around.

So they move in during the fall cold-down, and with nobody there to interrupt, they settle, breed, and multiply through the winter. You don't find out until you open the door in spring to that unmistakable musky smell and a season's worth of droppings in the silverware drawer.

The good news: a cabin is a smaller, simpler shell than a farmhouse, and it can be genuinely mouse-proofed. ## The two problems: keeping them out, and cleaning up after

Cabin mouse work splits into two jobs at two ends of the season, and it helps to think of them separately.

**At closing, in the fall, the job is exclusion and prep** — seal the shell and make the inside not worth moving into. **At reopening, in spring, the job is safe cleanup** — because whatever got in has to be dealt with as a health matter, not just a mess. Most cabin owners only think about the second, after the damage is done. Do the first and the second gets a lot smaller.

## Why deer mice make cabin cleanup a health job

The mouse you'll most often find in a lake cabin is the **deer mouse** — bicolored, brown on the back with a clean white belly and feet, a furred two-toned tail, prominent eyes and ears. It's a woods-and-field animal, and it's the classic occupant of a building left empty.

It's also the primary carrier of **hantavirus**, which changes how you handle the spring cleanup. The virus lives in the mouse's urine, droppings, and saliva, and people get exposed by breathing dust when dried contamination is stirred into the air. Opening a cabin and grabbing a broom to sweep out the droppings is the textbook wrong move — that's precisely how the dust goes airborne. The right way, every time: **open it up and ventilate for at least thirty minutes before you touch anything**, then wet the droppings and nests thoroughly with a bleach or disinfectant solution, let it sit, and wipe up with rubber gloves and a respirator, bagging waste sealed. No dry sweeping, no vacuuming, no shortcuts. Our [deer mice and hantavirus](/pest-problems/deer-mice-and-hantavirus/) page walks through it in full.

## How mice get into a cabin

Seasonal cabins tend to be older and lightly built, which means plenty of entry points. Many sit on **block, pier, or fieldstone foundations** with open crawlspaces underneath — a wide-open path to the underside of the floor and the wall cavities. Log and plank walls check and gap as they age. Add-on porches, bump-outs, and screened rooms create seams where one section meets another. And the usual suspects are all there: unsealed sill plates, pipe and wire penetrations, worn door thresholds, and soffit and gable vents that let mice in high as well as low.

A mouse needs about a **quarter inch**, so on a building like this the entry points number in the dozens. That's why a spray or a scatter of bait blocks doesn't cut it — the fix is finding and closing the gaps.

## Why cabins here get hit especially hard

The Chain O' Lakes and the river country around Waupaca and Iola are as good as mouse habitat gets, and the cabins sit right in the middle of it. These are twenty-two spring-fed glacial lakes ringed by woods, and the deer mouse is a woodland animal — so every cabin is dropped into a landscape that produces mice by the acre. Then the buildings themselves cooperate. Seasonal cabins tend to be older and more lightly built than year-round homes, often thrown up in eras and budgets that didn't fuss over sealing, and expanded over the years with porches and add-on rooms that each brought new seams.

The seasonal rhythm is the final ingredient. A year-round house is occupied through the fall push — lights, footsteps, a dog, doors opening — and that disturbance alone discourages mice from settling deep. A cabin goes dark in October right as the mice are moving, and stays undisturbed for six or seven months. Nothing interrupts them. That uninterrupted stretch is why a cabin can go from mouse-free at closing to a full-blown nest colony by the spring thaw, and why the buildings out here need to be shut tight, not just shut up.

## What actually keeps them out

Sealing the shell is the heart of it. We close the sill and foundation gaps, the penetrations, the door thresholds, and screen the vents with the right mesh so they still breathe but a mouse can't pass. On a crawlspace cabin that often means addressing the underside seriously, because that's the highway.

Then we take the inside off the menu. Every bit of food goes — not just the obvious pantry items but the forgotten stuff, the crackers in the drawer, the birdseed on the porch. Soft nesting material gets removed or sealed away: cushions, blankets, paper, insulation scraps. Traps go in as a monitor so that if anything does slip past, you know it and it isn't breeding all winter. Done at closing, this heads off the whole problem before it starts. Waiting until spring just means cleaning up instead.

## What to do the day you arrive

If you're pulling up to a cabin you haven't seen since fall, a little discipline at the door saves you grief. Before you carry anything in or start cleaning, prop the doors and windows open and let the place air out — thirty minutes at least — and step back outside while it does. That single habit addresses the biggest risk, the airborne dust from deer mouse droppings, before you've stirred anything up. Only then go in, assess where the activity was, and clean the way described above: wet everything down, wipe rather than sweep, gloves and a respirator on. Check the obvious targets — silverware and towel drawers, the pantry, under the sinks, mattresses and cushions, the backs of closets — because that's where the nests and contamination concentrate. If what you find is heavy, that's the signal to stop and call rather than push through it yourself.
