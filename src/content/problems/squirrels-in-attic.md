## The animal running laps over your bedroom at 6 a.m.

There's a specific rhythm to a squirrel in the attic, and once you've heard it you don't mistake it for anything else. It starts at first light — scampering, the hollow roll of an acorn across the joists, a burst of scratching, then quiet — and it comes back in the last hour before dark. That daytime pattern is the first clue, because squirrels are diurnal. Mice run at night and stay light; a raccoon is a heavy, slow, after-dark tenant. A squirrel sounds like exactly what it is: a small, busy animal the size of a cat's kitten, awake when you are, living directly over your ceiling.

Around Clintonville and the surrounding rural counties, the culprit is almost always the **eastern gray squirrel** or the smaller, feistier **red squirrel**, and in wooded lots it can be the nocturnal **flying squirrel**. All three are looking for the same thing your attic delivers better than any hollow tree: a warm, dry, defensible place to nest and — more to the point — to raise young. That nesting drive is what turns a squirrel on the roof into a squirrel in the house, and it's why this problem has a season.

## Why the attic, and why now

Squirrels den to breed, and in Wisconsin they breed twice a year. The first litters arrive in roughly **February and March**, conceived in the late-winter mating chase you hear tearing across the yard in January; the second come in **July and August**. In both stretches a female is looking for the safest cavity she can find, and a heated attic beats a windblown tree nest every time. That's why the classic squirrel-in-the-attic call spikes in late winter and again in midsummer — a pregnant or nursing female has moved in to raise a litter.

This matters enormously for how the problem gets solved, because for several weeks those pups can't leave on their own. Rush an eviction while a litter is still in the nest and you trap the young inside to die — which trades a noise problem for a dead-animal smell, a fly hatch, and a wall you have to open up. Getting the timing and the litter check right is the difference between a clean job and a mess, and it's the first thing a real exclusion accounts for.

## Reading the signs

Sound is the headline, but the evidence is on the building. Walk the perimeter and look up at the roofline. The entry points that let squirrels in give themselves away:

- **Soffit-and-fascia gaps.** The junction under the eaves is the number-one entry. Squirrels pry and chew where the soffit meets the fascia board, especially at corners and returns.
- **Gable and ridge vents.** Unscreened or damaged vents are an open door. You'll often see the screen torn out or the louvers chewed.
- **Chewed holes in fascia or trim.** A fresh gnaw hole shows pale, splintered wood and is usually round and about two inches across.
- **Roof-to-wall junctions.** Where a porch or lower roofline meets an upper wall, squirrels exploit the flashing gap.

Other tells include a **greasy rub mark** around a heavily used hole, scattered **droppings** in the attic (larger than mouse pellets), **shredded insulation** pulled into a nest, and stashed nuts or gnawed nutshells. On a red-squirrel job you'll sometimes find middens — piles of stripped pine cone scales — near the entry.

## Why rural Wisconsin homes get it worse

This is oak, maple, and pine country with woodlots stitched between farm fields, which means a heavy resident squirrel population pressing against every building at the wood's edge. Add the local housing stock — older farmhouses and village homes with decades of settlement, wood soffits and fascia rather than sealed modern panels, and complex rooflines with dormers and porch junctions — and you have abundant animals meeting abundant entry points. A mature oak overhanging the roof is a launch pad; a squirrel doesn't even need to climb the siding when a branch drops it straight onto the eaves.

Seasonal cabins around Iola and the lakes are a special case. They sit empty through the very months squirrels are house-hunting, so a colony can move in, breed, and settle without anyone there to hear it — the owner discovers it on the next visit, along with the chewed wiring and fouled insulation of a nest that's been running for months.

## The real fix: exclusion, not extermination

Here's the core of it. You do not solve a squirrel-in-the-attic problem by killing squirrels. Poison is a genuine mistake — it risks an animal dying inside a wall, and it does nothing about the hole that let it in, so the next squirrel simply moves into the vacancy. Trapping one animal ignores the rest and any litter. The problem isn't the individual squirrel; it's the **open building**.

The method that actually works is **one-way exclusion followed by permanent sealing**. After confirming there's no dependent litter (or waiting until the young are mobile), we install a **one-way door** over the main entry — a device the squirrels can push open to leave and feed but can't reopen to get back in. Every other opening is sealed first, so there's exactly one exit. Over a few days the animals let themselves out. Once a follow-up confirms the attic is empty, that last opening is closed too, and the whole roofline is sealed in **steel** — galvanized metal, hardware cloth, and flashing that a rodent's incisors can't chew through. Foam and caulk alone are worthless here; a squirrel will gnaw straight back through them.

## Keeping them out for good

Sealing in steel is most of prevention, but a few things keep the roofline from becoming an invitation again. **Trim back branches** that overhang or reach within jumping distance of the roof — six to eight feet is the rule of thumb — so squirrels can't launch onto the eaves. **Screen gable, ridge, and soffit vents** with metal mesh. **Cap the chimney**, which does double duty against squirrels and raccoons. And walk the roofline once a year, especially on older homes, because settlement and weather constantly open new gaps at the eaves and fascia; closing a small gap before an animal finds it is far cheaper than another exclusion.
