## When one wall of the house turns red and black

It's a sight that stops people on their own driveway. A warm afternoon in late September, and the **south wall of the house is crawling** — hundreds of flat, half-inch bugs, black with sharp red or orange lines, stacked in the corners of the siding, packed around the window trim, basking in the sun and slowly working into every seam. The shaded north side is bare. By a few weeks later some of them are inside, and come a warm day in January they're back at the windows.

These are **boxelder bugs**, and while the sheer numbers look alarming, the good news is what they *don't* do. They don't bite, they don't sting, they don't chew your house, and they don't get into your food. What they do is gather, stain, smell when crushed, and keep reappearing indoors all winter — a nuisance measured in mess and persistence, not damage. The fix is the same well-timed approach that handles the whole fall-invader crowd.

## Identifying them

Boxelder bugs are easy to know once you've seen them. The **adult** is about a half-inch long, flat and elongated, **charcoal-black with three red-orange stripes** on the thorax and red lines edging the wings, so a resting group looks like a scatter of dark shields marked in red. The **nymphs** are smaller, brighter red, and wingless, and you'll often see all sizes mixed together in a fall cluster. On the wall they sit in tight, sun-facing groups; disturbed, they move deliberately rather than darting. Crushed, they leave a **reddish stain and a sharp odor** — which is the main reason not to swat them against light siding or a curtain.

Don't confuse the massing on the siding with a feeding problem. The bugs aren't eating your house or the plants against it. They're just using the warm wall as a staging ground before winter.

## Why they're on your wall, and where they came from

Boxelder bugs spend the growing season out on **boxelder, maple, and ash trees**, feeding mainly on the developing seeds. The classic source is a **female, seed-bearing boxelder tree** near the house, but they range well beyond a single yard — a fencerow, a woodlot edge, or a neighbor's maples can feed the population that ends up on your siding. This is common around Clintonville because boxelder and box elder's maple relatives are everywhere in the fencerows, riverbanks, and yard edges of this landscape.

Come fall, the trees stop mattering and the **building** takes over. As nights cool and days shorten, the bugs quit feeding and go looking for a place to overwinter. They orient to **large, sun-warmed vertical surfaces** — precisely your south and west walls, which on a clear fall afternoon run far warmer than the surrounding air. They pile onto that warmth, bask, and spend the afternoon probing every crack for a way inside. It's the same behavior, driven by the same trigger, that brings the [cluster flies](/pest-problems/cluster-flies-in-windows/) and lady beetles at the same time of year.

## The overwintering cycle and the winter comebacks

The bugs that find a gap slip into the **wall voids and attic** and go dormant for the winter. They don't eat or breed in there — they just wait. The trouble, again, is that a false warm spell fools them. When a sunny winter day heats the wall cavities, they rouse, take the warmth for spring, and crawl toward light, emerging at the interior windows and light fixtures. A cold snap the next day sends the survivors back down. So the January bugs at your window aren't a new arrival; they're last fall's siding crowd, still in the walls, waking in waves.

How they got into the wall is the ordinary story of any building envelope: the **soffit-and-fascia junction**, gaps around **window and door frames**, spaces behind aging trim, **utility penetrations**, and the cracks where **siding meets the foundation**. Because the bugs mass right on the siding for days, they have ample time to find every reachable seam. And in the older, balloon-framed homes common here, a bug that enters low on the wall can travel the uninterrupted cavity straight up to the attic and turn up at an upstairs window.

## The real fix: exterior barrier plus sealing, not indoor spray

Spraying the bugs at the window is the wrong tool for the same reason it is with cluster flies: the ones you see indoors are a fraction of the population dormant in the wall voids, and no indoor aerosol reaches those cavities. You'd be putting pesticide in your living space all winter to kill a harmless nuisance bug, without touching the reservoir behind the drywall.

What works is two coordinated moves. The centerpiece is an **exterior treatment of the sunny walls in early fall**, before the bugs mass — a residual barrier on the south and west siding, eaves, soffits, and around windows and doors, so the bugs are knocked down on the outside as they arrive. This only works while they're still outside, which makes the timing non-negotiable: **late August into September** catches the wave; October is largely too late; winter is a lost cause until next fall. The second move is **sealing the entry points** — the soffit junctions, window and door frames, penetrations, and siding-to-foundation cracks — which keeps the bugs out this fall and every fall after. For the ones already on the wall or inside, **soapy water outside and a vacuum inside** handle the cleanup without the staining that crushing causes.

## Keeping the siding clear next year

Prevention comes down to hitting the timing and keeping the envelope tight. **Schedule the exterior treatment for late August**, because waiting until the bugs are inside means the window has already closed. **Keep sealing** the soffits, frames, and foundation line as the building ages. **Vacuum the indoor bugs and hose the outdoor ones with soapy water** rather than crushing them, to avoid the stains. If a **female boxelder tree is right up against the house**, removing it is worth weighing — it can cut the local numbers — but don't expect it to solve the problem alone, since the bugs travel from trees well beyond your lot. Pale siding with a big south exposure on an older rural home is a magnet, which is exactly the kind of house the annual barrier is built for.
