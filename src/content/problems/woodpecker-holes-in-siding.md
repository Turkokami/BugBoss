## Why this happens

A woodpecker hammering the same patch of your siding every morning is not being random. It is doing one of three things, and the fix depends entirely on which.

**Feeding.** It can hear insect larvae inside the wood and is excavating to reach them. In Northeast Wisconsin the usual target is **carpenter bee larvae** — the bees drill neat round galleries in bare softwood, the grubs develop inside, and the birds hear them. Feeding damage looks irregular and follows the tunnels, often opening a tidy half-inch hole into a ragged trench several inches long. This is the version that does real structural harm, and on most carpenter bee jobs the woodpecker damage costs more to repair than the bees caused.

**Drumming.** A fast, rhythmic, surprisingly loud tapping on something resonant — metal flashing, a gutter, a downspout, a hollow spot in siding. This is territorial and mating display, peaks in spring, and does little or no damage. It is a noise problem, usually at six in the morning.

**Nesting.** A large neat round cavity excavated into soft or rotting wood, leading to a hollowed chamber. Less common on houses but a much bigger repair, and once it is active the legal position narrows sharply.

Cedar and other soft natural siding takes the worst of it — easy to excavate, hosts insects, and resonant. Stained-but-not-painted surfaces do worse than painted ones. Wood that is already damaged or beginning to rot is more attractive again, because it is softer and more likely to hold larvae.

## What to do now

**Look at the wood, not the bird.** Are there perfectly round half-inch holes on the underside of nearby fascia or trim? Coarse sawdust? Yellowish staining? That is carpenter bees, and it is your answer.

**Note the pattern.** Damage concentrated on one board while the rest of the wall is untouched means something is in that board. Damage spread thinly over a resonant surface is more likely drumming.

**Do not fill the holes yet.** Repairing while the larvae are still in there means the bird reopens them.

**Do not attempt anything targeting the bird.** Native woodpeckers are protected and the options that involve harming them are not available.

**Try deterrents only as a stopgap**, and deploy them the day damage starts rather than weeks in. Move them frequently. Expect days, not weeks, of effect.

## When to call

Call when a woodpecker is **repeatedly working the same spot** — that is the pattern that means something is in the wood, and it will not resolve by itself.

Call if you can see **round half-inch holes** in nearby trim, fascia or rails, which points at carpenter bees.

Call before **repairing**, so the cause is dealt with first and you only pay for the repair once.

Call if you think a bird has started **excavating a nest cavity**, because the options narrow once that is active and earlier is much better.
