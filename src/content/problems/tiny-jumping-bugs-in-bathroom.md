faqs:
  - q: "Are these fleas?"
    a: "Almost certainly not, and this is the single most common misidentification we correct. Check two things. Bites: fleas bite and leave itchy welts, usually around ankles and lower legs — springtails cannot bite at all, their mouthparts scrape mould. And the jump: a flea launches with direction and purpose toward a host, while a springtail's jump is a panic reflex that flings it into an uncontrolled tumble with no idea where it will land. If nobody in the house has bites and the specks are on damp surfaces rather than on the pets, you have springtails."
  - q: "Can springtails hurt me or my pets?"
    a: "No, in every sense. Springtails do not bite, sting, spread disease, live on people or animals, damage the house, chew anything, or infest food. They eat mould, algae and decaying plant matter. Some people report an itching or crawling sensation when large numbers are present, which is a real experience but is a response to tiny animals on skin, or to the same damp mouldy conditions the springtails like, rather than to any bite. If someone genuinely has bites, something else is causing them."
  - q: "Where are they coming from?"
    a: "Water. Springtails live in enormous numbers in soil, mulch and lawn thatch — tens of thousands per square metre is normal and nobody notices, because they stay out there. They come indoors when conditions change: heavy rain floods their habitat, or a dry spell drives them to seek damp. Indoors they can only persist where it stays wet, so a bathroom concentration usually means a leak behind a fixture, failing grout, or a wall cavity that has been damp long enough to grow mould."
  - q: "Why the bathroom specifically?"
    a: "Because bathrooms combine standing humidity with the specific wet spots springtails need — the gap behind a toilet base, under a leaking trap, shower grout, the edge of a bath surround, and any framing kept wet by a slow leak. Finding springtails in a bathroom is often the first symptom of a leak nobody knew about, and I have traced genuine plumbing failures that way. It is worth taking the hint and looking behind the fixtures rather than reaching for a spray."
  - q: "Does this mean I have mould?"
    a: "Often, and this is the most useful thing to take from finding them. Springtails eat fungal growth, so a thriving indoor population means there is enough mould or mildew present to feed them — which means a surface has been wet long enough for fungus to establish. That might be visible on a basement wall or hidden inside a wall cavity or under flooring. Treating springtails as an early warning system is far more productive than treating them as an insect problem."
  - q: "How do I get rid of them?"
    a: "Dry the source, which is not a hedge — moisture is the entire dependency and springtails cannot survive anywhere that stays dry. Indoors that means finding and fixing the leak, ventilating properly, and dehumidifying a damp basement or crawlspace. Outdoors it means extending downspouts, regrading so water falls away from the house, pulling mulch back off the foundation, and dethatching lawn that runs to the wall. Treatment knocks numbers down for a few weeks; drying ends it."
  - q: "Should I spray?"
    a: "Not as the main approach, and not much indoors. The springtails inside are dying anyway in dry air, spraying interior surfaces puts product where you live for little gain, and the outdoor reservoir is effectively unlimited so any residual just fades and they return. A perimeter treatment has a place in knocking down heavy outdoor pressure while the moisture corrections are being made, but anyone selling you a quarterly spray for springtails is selling you a subscription to a plumbing problem."
  - q: "Why are they in my houseplants?"
    a: "Overwatering. Consistently wet potting soil grows the fungal and organic material springtails eat, and a well-established pot can maintain a population indefinitely and seed the rest of the room. This is the main winter source, when everything outdoors is frozen and people cannot work out where the specks are coming from. Let the top of the soil dry between waterings, empty the saucers, and repot anything with soil that has gone sour. Fungus gnats usually turn up in the same pots for the same reason."
  - q: "Will they go away on their own?"
    a: "A one-off wave after a storm often does — springtails that wander indoors dry out and die within a day or two if the interior is genuinely dry, so a vacuum and a few days handles it. But that only holds where the inside is dry. If you have a damp basement, a wet crawlspace, a leak, or a chronically humid bathroom, they find those conditions comfortable, they stay, and they breed. Recurring means resident, and resident means there is water to find."

## Why this happens

Somebody notices a scattering of tiny dark specks around the base of the toilet, or across a damp basement floor, or floating in a sink. Then they touch one and it vanishes — flicked into the air faster than the eye can follow.

That disappearing act is what turns a curiosity into a phone call, because the immediate assumption is nearly always fleas.

They are **springtails**, and they are one of the most abundant and least harmful animals on the planet. A millimetre or two long, soft-bodied, grey or white or bluish, living by the hundreds of thousands in soil, mulch and lawn thatch, eating mould and decaying plant matter. Underneath the abdomen they carry a forked appendage held under tension; release it and it snaps against the ground and flings the animal several inches in an uncontrolled tumble. That is the jump, and it is a panic reflex rather than a way of getting anywhere.

They come indoors for exactly one reason: **water**.

Springtails breathe partly through their body surface and have almost no ability to retain moisture. Dry air kills them within a day. That hard physiological limit is the whole story — it explains why they cluster where they do, and it is also the lever that solves the problem, because a genuinely dry space cannot support them no matter how many arrive.

So a persistent indoor population is not really an insect problem. It is a **moisture report**, delivered by insects.

## What to do now

**Check for bites before anything else.** If nobody has any, you can stop worrying about fleas and stop spending money on them.

**Follow the concentration.** Springtails cluster where the water is. Where they are thickest is where to look — behind the toilet, under the vanity, along a basement wall, around the sump, at the base of a shower surround.

**Look behind the fixtures.** A slow leak keeping framing wet is the classic bathroom source and it is invisible until someone looks.

**Vacuum rather than spray.** They die on their own in dry air; a vacuum handles the ones you can see today.

**Check the houseplants**, especially in winter. Overwatered soil is the commonest indoor reservoir and the hardest to guess.

**Walk the outside.** Downspouts discharging at the foundation, ground sloping toward the house, a dripping hose bib behind a shrub, mulch piled against the siding. Any of those keeps a wet strip exactly where you least want one.
