faqs:
  - q: "Why would an animal chew plywood and tool handles?"
    a: "Salt. Porcupines have a strong physiological craving for sodium, and their winter diet of bark and conifer needles supplies almost none, so they actively hunt for it. That puts a strange range of things on the menu: plywood, because many adhesives contain sodium compounds; tool handles and axe shafts soaked in years of hand sweat; deck rails and stair treads people have gripped; road-salted vehicle undersides, tires and brake lines; leather saddles, boat seats and boots. The chewing looks random until you realize it is tracking the salt precisely."
  - q: "Can a porcupine throw its quills at me?"
    a: "No — that is the most persistent myth about them and it is entirely false. Quills cannot be thrown, launched or shot. What creates the impression is how easily they detach: they are loosely rooted, and a threatened porcupine raises them, turns its back and lashes with its tail, so anything that makes contact comes away with quills embedded. It happens fast enough that a dog can take a faceful without anyone seeing the moment. Keep a few feet of distance and a porcupine cannot quill you."
  - q: "My dog got quilled. What do I do?"
    a: "Go to a vet, today, and do not try to pull them yourself. Quills carry microscopic backward-facing barbs that make them very hard to withdraw cleanly, and they migrate deeper into tissue with time and movement — a quill left in place can travel and reach a joint, the chest cavity or an eye. Removal generally requires sedation, both for the pain and because a struggling dog drives them further in. Pulling at them at home commonly snaps them, leaving barbed fragments buried where you cannot see them."
  - q: "Are they dangerous to people?"
    a: "Not unless you touch one. Porcupines are slow, placid, largely nocturnal animals with no interest in confrontation and no ability to chase anything down. They do not attack and they cannot throw quills. A person keeping a sensible distance is in no danger at all. The realistic risks are to curious dogs, to livestock that investigate, and to anyone who decides to move one. Give it room and it will amble away — slowly, because porcupines are in no hurry about anything."
  - q: "How do I know it's a porcupine and not a beaver or a raccoon?"
    a: "Look at what was chewed and where. Porcupine chewing is heavy and coarse with broad tooth grooves, and it tracks salt — plywood edges, tool handles, deck rails, vehicle parts — rather than food. Then find the den: a well-used entrance under a shed, barn or porch with a distinctive mound of pellet scat outside it, and shed quills caught around the opening. In winter, worn trails through snow leading to and from a building settle it. Beavers work near water on whole trees; raccoons make a mess but do not gnaw structural wood for salt."
  - q: "It's stripping bark off my trees. Will they die?"
    a: "They can. A porcupine feeding on the upper trunk sometimes strips bark all the way around, which severs the tissue carrying nutrients between canopy and roots, and everything above that point dies. Partial stripping usually only weakens the tree and opens it to disease and insects, but complete girdling is fatal. They favor hemlock, pine and other conifers in winter and return to the same trees repeatedly, so one animal using your woodlot can kill several trees over a few seasons."
  - q: "Where is it living?"
    a: "Naturally, in rock crevices, hollow logs, brush piles and the base of large trees — but around a rural property, the void under a shed, barn, porch, deck or outbuilding is better than anything nature offers, being dry, sheltered and inaccessible to fishers. Look for the scat mound at the entrance; porcupines defecate at the den rather than away from it, and over a season the accumulation becomes substantial and very obvious. They are strongly attached to these sites and reuse them year after year."
  - q: "If I close the den, will it stay away?"
    a: "Only if the closure is built properly. Porcupines have strong site fidelity and will try hard to get back into a den they have used for years, and they are heavy-jawed rodents and capable diggers. Lattice, thin plywood or chicken wire will not hold — expect a new hole beside your patch within days. Hardware cloth, well fastened, with the base buried or skirted outward against digging, is what holds. Done properly the animal moves on rather than persisting."
  - q: "Do I need to do anything about the salt?"
    a: "Yes, or you will get a repeat with a different animal. Secure livestock salt and mineral blocks, store tools and leather inside rather than leaning in the barn, park vehicles under cover in winter where you can, and protect exposed plywood edges on sheds and outbuildings. Removing the animal while leaving everything that attracted it means the property is just as appealing to the next porcupine that comes through."
  - q: "Is it legal to remove one?"
    a: "Porcupines are native Wisconsin wildlife and their handling, trapping, relocation and dispatch are governed by state wildlife regulation, with specific provisions that can differ for animals causing property damage. The rules are worth getting right rather than assuming, and they change. Source: Wisconsin Department of Natural Resources wildlife regulations. ## Why this happens

Porcupine problems rarely announce themselves as wildlife problems. They start as something inexplicable.

The plywood on the shed has been chewed away along one edge. The handle of a good axe left leaning in the barn is ruined. A section of deck rail looks like it was worked over with a rasp. Someone finds the brake lines on a truck chewed through, or the seat of a boat destroyed, or a saddle wrecked. None of it makes sense as animal behavior, because none of it is food.

Then you notice what the damaged things have in common: **salt**. Sweat-soaked wood, sodium-containing glues, road salt on a vehicle underside, salt-cured leather. Once you see the pattern the whole thing resolves at once.

Porcupines need sodium and their winter diet supplies almost none, so they hunt for it — and they are remarkably good at finding it. They are large, slow, nocturnal rodents, excellent climbers, and completely unhurried. They cannot chase anything and have no interest in you.

**Winter is when this peaks**, and everything converges: the diet shifts to bark and needles so tree girdling starts, the sodium deficit becomes acute so the structural chewing starts, deep snow keeps them close to the den so damage concentrates in one area, and road salt is everywhere.

They den in the void under sheds, barns, porches and outbuildings — dry, sheltered, safe from fishers — and they come back to the same den year after year.

## What to do now

**Deal with a quilled dog first.** If that has happened, it is a same-day veterinary visit and it outranks everything else on this page. Do not attempt removal at home.

**Follow the salt.** Look at what has been chewed and you will usually be able to predict what gets hit next. Move it.

**Find the den.** Under the shed, barn, deck or porch, marked by a mound of pellet scat at the entrance and shed quills nearby. In snow, follow the worn trails.

**Check your trees.** Bark stripped in patches high on the trunk and out on branches, with coarse tooth grooves and nipped twigs on the ground below. If a trunk is stripped all the way round, that tree is finished.

**Check vehicle brake lines and hoses** if anything has been parked outside. This one is a genuine safety issue, not an inconvenience.

**Keep dogs leashed at dusk** on rural property in winter. That is when most quillings happen, and dogs that have been quilled once frequently do it again.

## When to call

Call when you find **chewing you cannot explain** on plywood, tool handles, deck rails or vehicle components.

Call when you find a **den with a scat mound** under a shed, barn or deck — that is a resident animal, not a passing one.

Call when **trees are being stripped high on the trunk**, especially if you have conifers you care about, because girdling is not reversible.

Call **before winter** if you had a porcupine last year. Autumn is the right window to close dens and protect trees, before the animal is established for the cold season.
