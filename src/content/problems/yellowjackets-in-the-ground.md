## Why there's a hole full of wasps in your lawn

Most of the yellowjackets in this part of Wisconsin nest **underground**, and the way you usually discover it is unpleasant: a lawnmower, a string trimmer, or a footstep passes over an unremarkable hole in the yard and a furious cloud of wasps comes boiling out. Until that moment there's often no sign at all, because the nest is hidden below the surface and only the entrance is visible.

Ground-nesting yellowjackets — chiefly the eastern yellowjacket and its relatives (*Vespula*) — don't dig their nests from scratch. A founding queen in spring looks for an existing cavity, and the most common one is an **abandoned rodent burrow**: a mouse, vole, or chipmunk tunnel that gives her a ready-made starter chamber. She enlarges it, builds the first paper cells, and raises the first batch of workers herself. From there the workers take over, and through the summer they excavate the cavity larger and larger to hold a growing paper nest. The colony that started as one queen in a mouse hole in May can be a **nest of several thousand workers** by late summer, filling a cavity the size of a basketball or larger underground. Banks, the base of retaining walls, the edges of walkways, spots under stumps, sheds, and rock piles, and the soft ground of field edges and fencerows all offer the kind of cavity they want. Our [wasps and hornets](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) page covers the full annual cycle these colonies run on.

## Spotting the nest — and ruling out bees

The reliable way to find a ground nest is to **watch the traffic**. On a warm, sunny afternoon, look for wasps flying a steady, repeated line to and from one point on the ground. That consistent two-way stream at a single hole is the entrance. Random wasps drifting around the yard are just foraging; a busy, dedicated highway in and out of one spot is a colony.

Before you treat anything, though, make sure it's actually yellowjackets. In spring you may see a scatter of small individual holes in a patch of bare soil, each with a single gentle insect coming and going — those are almost certainly **ground-nesting solitary bees**, like mining bees. They're harmless, they rarely sting, they're excellent pollinators, and each little hole is one bee minding its own business. That's not what this page is about, and those you leave alone. A yellowjacket nest is the opposite picture: **one heavily trafficked entrance**, shared by the whole colony, with defensive wasps that will come out in force if disturbed.

## Why a ground nest is the dangerous kind

Yellowjacket ground nests cause most of the serious multiple-sting incidents we get called about, and the reason is simple: **they're invisible until you're on top of them.** A nest hanging in a tree or under an eave you can see and avoid. A nest in the lawn gives no warning, so people blunder into it doing ordinary yard work — mowing, trimming, gardening, digging — and the colony responds to the vibration and disturbance with a mass defense.

Two things make that response serious. First, unlike a honeybee, a **yellowjacket can sting repeatedly**, so a single wasp does real damage and a swarm does a lot. A large disturbed ground colony can deliver dozens of stings in seconds. Second, for the roughly three percent of people with a stinging-insect allergy, even a single sting can trigger anaphylaxis, a medical emergency — and a swarm from a ground nest is a worst-case exposure. Even for someone with no allergy, enough stings at once can be medically dangerous. A ground nest near the house, the mowed lawn, the garden, a walkway, or anywhere kids and pets play is a genuine hazard, not just a nuisance.

## Why they turn up where they do

Rural yards out here practically invite ground nests, for two reasons that feed each other. First, **cavities are everywhere**. Properties bordered by fields, fencerows, woodlots, and ditches are full of the old rodent burrows yellowjackets colonize, plus gaps under sheds, stumps, walkways, retaining walls, and outbuilding slabs. A founding queen shopping for a nest site in spring has endless options. Second, there's **food**. From midsummer on, yellowjackets scavenge sugars and protein — garbage, compost, fallen fruit from a yard tree, pet food left outside, spilled feed near a barn, sugary drinks at a cookout. A yard that offers both nest cavities and an easy food supply supports big colonies, and rural properties tend to offer plenty of both.

The seasonal shape matters too. The colony builds quietly through June and July and reaches **peak size and peak aggression in August and September** — right when the late-summer calendar of graduation parties, weddings, and cookouts brings people out into the yard. That overlap is why late summer is peak call season for ground nests.

## Why the DIY home remedies are a bad idea

The internet is full of folk cures for ground nests, and the popular ones range from useless to genuinely hazardous. **Gasoline poured down the hole** is dangerous, illegal to use as a pesticide, and contaminates your soil and groundwater — and it doesn't reliably kill a colony spread through a deep cavity. **Boiling water** rarely penetrates far enough to reach the whole nest and mostly just enrages the workers. Both put you standing directly over the entrance of an angry colony, often in the dark, which is how people end up badly stung. Store-bought aerosols aimed at the hole tend to hit the entrance without reaching the brood and queen deep below, agitating an intact colony rather than killing it.

The method that actually works is straightforward but has to be done right: **treat the entrance so departing and returning workers track the product down into the nest**, ideally after dark or in the cool of early morning when nearly the whole colony is home and the wasps are sluggish. Done that way, the colony collapses within a day or two.

## Keeping the yard clear

You can't prevent every ground nest on a rural property, but you can cut the odds. After any nest is treated, **fill in the abandoned rodent burrows and cavities** near the house, lawn, garden, and walkways where ground yellowjackets like to set up — controlling the rodents that dig those burrows in the first place helps too. From midsummer on, **keep garbage covered, clean up fallen fruit and pet food, and cover food and drinks at outdoor gatherings** so you're not feeding a colony. And early in the season, a quick scan of the yard on warm afternoons can catch a new nest's traffic while the colony is still small and easy to deal with, long before it becomes an August hazard.
