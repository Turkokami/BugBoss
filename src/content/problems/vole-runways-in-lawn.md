faqs:
  - q: "Is it voles or moles?"
    a: "Look at whether soil was moved. Voles run at grass level, so you get shallow winding surface trails of flattened, chewed grass about two inches wide, with small clean holes and no soil pushed up. Moles tunnel through soil, so you get raised ridges you can feel underfoot and conical mounds of excavated earth. No mounds means it is not a mole. This matters because the two animals want completely different things — moles are after grubs and earthworms in the soil, voles eat plants — so treating for the wrong one wastes a season."
  - q: "Why did it all appear at once in spring?"
    a: "Because it happened under the snow, where you could not see it. Voles stay active all winter, running beneath the snowpack in the insulated space at grass level, protected from hawks and owls. They graze the grass along those runs for months. When the snow melts, the whole winter's worth of activity is revealed in a single week and it looks like it happened overnight. It did not — you are seeing the record of December through March."
  - q: "Will my lawn recover?"
    a: "Usually yes, and with very little help. The runways are grazed grass rather than dead root systems, so most lawns green over them within a few weeks of spring growth. Raking out the loose debris and overseeding heavily-damaged patches speeds it along. Resist the urge to do anything drastic in March — give it until the grass is properly growing before deciding whether any repair is actually needed. The lawn is almost never the real problem."
  - q: "What's the damage that actually matters?"
    a: "Girdled trees and shrubs. Under snow cover voles will strip bark from the base of young trees, fruit trees, and ornamental shrubs, and if they remove it all the way around the trunk they sever the tissue carrying nutrients to the roots. That kills the tree — sometimes not obviously until it leafs out weakly in spring and then collapses in July. Check the base of every young tree after snowmelt. Gnawed bark with fine tooth grooves at or just above ground level is vole work, and a fully girdled young tree cannot be saved."
  - q: "How do I stop them girdling my trees?"
    a: "Hardware cloth cylinders around the trunks, put on in autumn before snow and taken off in spring. A quarter-inch mesh cylinder held an inch or two off the bark, pushed slightly into the soil and extending above the expected snow line, defeats them completely. Also clear mulch, leaf litter and long grass back from the trunk base — voles need cover to work, and a bare collar of a few inches around a trunk removes the concealment they rely on."
  - q: "What attracts voles to a yard?"
    a: "Cover and food. Thick lawn thatch, deep mulch beds, dense ground-cover plantings, tall grass along fence lines and field edges, brush piles, and anything else that lets them move without being seen from above. Properties bordering fields, pasture, or unmown ground get the heaviest pressure, which describes a great deal of our service area. Voles are prey for almost everything, so they will not cross open ground if they can avoid it — which is precisely what makes habitat management effective."
  - q: "Are voles dangerous?"
    a: "Not to people in any direct way. They do not invade houses the way mice do, they are not aggressive, and they are not a significant disease concern in the way deer mice are. The damage is entirely horticultural — grazed lawn, girdled trees and shrubs, and chewed roots and bulbs in garden beds. They can also occasionally undermine small areas with their surface tunnelling, but the trees are what cost money."
  - q: "How many are out there?"
    a: "More than the runway network suggests, and the population swings hard from year to year. Voles breed prolifically through the growing season, and Wisconsin sees boom years where numbers climb dramatically before predators and winter knock them back. A yard that had almost no damage last spring can be criss-crossed this spring for no reason you did anything about. That cyclical pattern is why habitat management and tree protection beat trying to control the population directly."
  - q: "Will my cat handle it?"
    a: "A hunting cat does suppress voles and is genuinely useful, but it will not protect your trees. Voles work under snow all winter where no cat can reach them, and that is exactly when girdling happens. Treat a cat as helpful background pressure, not as tree protection. The hardware cloth is what saves the apple tree."

Otherwise call at snowmelt if you find bark damage, if the runway network is extensive year after year, or if you want the habitat side looked at properly. ## Why this happens

Voles are small, stocky, short-tailed rodents — often called meadow mice — and they live in the grass rather than in your house. That last point separates them from every other rodent problem on this site: voles are not trying to get indoors.

What makes them a spring surprise is that they work **under the snow**.

Snowpack creates an insulated space at ground level, protected from hawks and owls, warm enough to stay active in, and completely hidden. Voles spend the entire Wisconsin winter running around down there, grazing grass along established routes and gnawing bark off anything woody within reach. Four months of activity accumulates invisibly.

Then the snow melts over a week in March and it all appears at once. People reasonably assume something dramatic just happened to their lawn. Nothing did — you are looking at the record of the whole winter, revealed.

The lawn damage looks worse than it is. The runways are grazed grass, not destroyed root systems, and most lawns green over them within weeks.

**The tree damage is the real problem**, and it is the thing to check first. Under snow cover voles strip bark from the base of young trees and shrubs, and a trunk girdled all the way around is dead — often without looking dead until midsummer.

Out here the pressure is high because so many properties border fields, pasture, or unmown ground. Voles need cover to move safely, and a lawn that runs straight into long grass or a brushy fence line is an open invitation.

## What to do now

**Check the base of every young tree and shrub.** Gnawed bark with fine tooth grooves at or just above ground level is vole work. If the bark is stripped all the way around, that plant is finished — knowing now is better than wondering in July.

**Look for mounds before blaming moles.** No soil pushed up means it is not a mole, and treating for moles will do nothing.

**Rake the loose debris out** and leave the lawn alone otherwise. Give it until the grass is growing properly before deciding whether any repair is needed. Overseed the worst patches then, not now.

**Pull mulch and ground cover back** from tree trunks to leave a bare collar. Voles will not work where they can be seen.

**Note where the runways come from.** They usually lead back to a field edge, a brush pile, or a thick bed. That is the source, and it is where the habitat work goes.

**Plan for autumn now.** Hardware cloth on the trunks before the first snow is the entire solution to the expensive half of this problem.

## When to call

Call in **autumn** if you have young trees, an orchard, a shelterbelt planting, or valuable shrubs and any history of vole activity. That is the call that actually saves them, and almost nobody makes it.

Call at **snowmelt** if you find bark damage, so we can tell you what is salvageable.

Call if the runway network is **extensive and recurring** year after year, which points at habitat you can change.

Call if you are **not sure whether it is voles or moles**, because the two need completely different work and getting it wrong costs a season.
