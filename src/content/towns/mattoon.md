## The Mattoon pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): thaw, swarmers, and the fly build-up

When the snow melts, Mattoon's overwintering pests wake. The **cluster flies and Asian lady beetles** that spent winter in farmhouse wall voids crawl to the windows on warm days, and **mice** that wintered in the house and outbuildings get active and start breeding — a head start that matters, because a farm gives them unlimited food to breed against.

Spring is **carpenter ant** season, and farm properties feed them well: woodlots and tree lines hold the outdoor parent colonies, and older homes and outbuildings with decades of small leaks provide the damp wood satellite nests move into. Winged swarmers at a window in April, or large black ants indoors while it's still cold, mean a nest is living in the heated structure. Our [carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) page covers the difference between foragers and an indoor nest.

Spring is also when **fly pressure** starts building on livestock operations, as house flies and stable flies begin breeding in manure and wet bedding. Getting ahead of them early — at the breeding source — is far easier than fighting a July peak. And late spring brings the first **wasp** queens scouting barn rafters and shed eaves.

### Summer (June–August): flies, stinging insects, and bats

Summer is fly and stinging-insect season, and on a Mattoon farm the two run hard. **House flies and stable flies** hit their peak around livestock, manure, and feed — a genuine nuisance and a real problem for dairy and beef operations, where fly loads cut into animal comfort and productivity. Fly control on a farm is about the **breeding source** — manure management, sanitation, and targeted treatment — not just hanging strips.

**Wasps, yellow jackets, and bald-faced hornets** reach full, defensive size by August in barns, machine sheds, eaves, and equipment, right where people are working, which is when most stings and most of our [wasp and hornet](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) calls come from farms.

Summer is the one time of year we **cannot** seal bats out. Wisconsin keeps bat exits open **June 1 through July 31** for the maternity season, when flightless pups are in the roost. Barns and farmhouse attics are classic bat roosts, so if you find them in July, the move is to plan the exclusion for August — not trap pups inside.

### Fall (September–October): the invasion and the rodent push

Fall is the defining season and the reason a lot of Mattoon homeowners first call. As nights cool, the **fall invaders** — [cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/), [boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/), and [Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/) — mass on warm south and west walls and work into soffit, fascia, and eave gaps to overwinter.

Cluster flies are especially brutal on farmhouses, and it's worth understanding why: their larvae parasitize earthworms in the soil, and a farmhouse surrounded by cropland and pasture sits on top of an enormous earthworm population. That produces a heavy local fly population every single year, no matter how clean the house is — this is a farm-location problem, not a housekeeping problem. The fix is an **early-fall exterior treatment** of the walls they gather on, paired with sealing entry points.

At the same time, **mice and rats** make their big move as the fields are harvested and go cold — out of the stubble and fencerows and into the barns, sheds, and house. Deer mice out of the fields are a specific hantavirus concern. Fall is the highest-value time to seal and treat, because everything you close in September holds off the winter push.

### Winter (November–February): rodents settle in

By winter the outdoor invasion is over and what's indoors is what got in. On a farm, **rodents don't slow down** — a barn full of feed and a heated farmhouse give them everything they need to breed year-round, so a manageable fall population becomes a serious winter infestation if the buildings aren't sealed and a control program isn't running. **Overwintering cluster flies and lady beetles** stir on a warm day and turn up at the farmhouse windows. Winter work in Mattoon is mostly heavy rodent exclusion and control across the house and outbuildings, plus fall-invader cleanup.

## Farm and farmhouse building stock: where pests get in

Mattoon's structures are agricultural, and they fail in predictable, fixable ways.

**Older farmhouses** typically have fieldstone or block foundations, balloon framing, and decades of settlement gaps. Balloon-framed walls are open vertical highways from the basement to the attic — the exact route a mouse takes from the sill plate to the attic and cluster flies take from a soffit gap to an upstairs bedroom. An old farmhouse with open stud channels running cellar to attic rewards a thorough exclusion inspection more than any other building on the place, and our [farmhouse rodent exclusion guide](/guides/farmhouse-rodent-exclusion/) lays out how we seal one.

**Barns, machine sheds, granaries, and pole barns** are the other half. They're not meant to be pest-tight, and they hold feed, grain, bedding, and equipment that draw rodents and give them endless harborage. The goal here isn't to seal them like a house — it's to reduce harborage, protect the feed, and run a control program placed where the rodents actually travel.

Together, the house and the outbuildings are one connected system on a farm, and rodents move freely between them. We treat the whole property as one problem, which is the only way farm rodent control actually holds.

## Farm rodent control: why traps in the kitchen never fix it

The most common mistake we see on Mattoon properties is treating a farm rodent problem like a house rodent problem. A homeowner in the village sets a few traps in the pantry and the problem goes away. On a farm, that same approach fails every time, because the farm is a **connected rodent ecosystem**: the barn holds feed and warmth, the granary holds grain, the machine shed holds harborage and cover, and the farmhouse holds warmth and pantry food — and mice and Norway rats travel freely among all of them along fence lines, foundations, and utility runs. Trap the kitchen and you're removing the few individuals that wandered farthest from a population living in the outbuildings.

Real farm rodent control works the whole system at once. It starts with an **inspection** that follows the runs, burrows, droppings, and rub marks to find where the population actually lives and feeds. It reduces **harborage** — clutter, spilled feed, weeds and debris against foundations, and the gaps that let rodents into feed and buildings. It protects the **feed and grain**, because as long as there's an open food supply, rodents will keep breeding against it faster than you can trap them. And it places **secured bait stations and traps where the rodents run**, not where it's convenient, and keeps a maintenance rhythm through the seasons rather than reacting to a spike. Our [farmhouse rodent exclusion guide](/guides/farmhouse-rodent-exclusion/) covers the house side of that; the mice and rat detail is on the [mice](/pest-library/mice/) page. This is a program, not a product — which is exactly why it works and a bag of snap traps doesn't.

## Stable flies and house flies: the dairy operation's summer problem

For a Mattoon dairy or livestock operation, **flies** are as much a production issue as a nuisance. House flies breed in manure, spilled feed, and wet organic matter, and stable flies — the biting ones that go after cattle's legs — breed in the wet, decomposing edges of bedding, feed, and manure piles. Heavy fly loads stress animals, cut into feed efficiency and milk production, spread pathogens, and draw the attention of inspectors.

Killing adult flies is the least effective part of fly control; by the time they're on the wall, the population is already established. Effective control attacks the **breeding source**: managing manure and moisture, cleaning up feed spillage and wet bedding, and timing larvicide and targeted treatments to the breeding sites and the season. Adult knockdown and baiting have their place as a supplement, but the win is in denying flies a place to reproduce. That's the approach in our [dairy farm pest control guide](/guides/dairy-farm-pest-control/), and it's the difference between a fly program that works all summer and one that just moves the problem around the barn.
