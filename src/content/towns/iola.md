## A village in the high lake country

Iola is a Waupaca County village of about 1,230 people, and it sits higher than most of the towns around it — roughly 1,037 feet of elevation, up in the rolling glacial terrain that also cradles the **Waupaca Chain O' Lakes**, a string of 22 spring-fed lakes that make this some of the most popular cabin country in central Wisconsin. The village takes its name from a **Potawatomi woman** the early settlers called Iola, and the **Tomorrow River** — which flows on to become the Waupaca River — runs through the area. For decades Iola was also a small publishing town, home to **Krause Publications**, the hobby and numismatic publisher that operated here from the early 1970s until it relocated in 2018. But the thing that truly puts Iola on the map is the **Iola Car Show & Swap Meet**, one of the largest old-car events in the country, which fills the grounds every July with something on the order of 2,500 show cars, thousands of swap spaces, and well over a hundred thousand visitors.

All of that shapes the pest work here in ways that make Iola genuinely different from the farm towns nearby. This is **lake and cabin country first** — a place with heavy seasonal occupancy, wooded lots pressed up against water, and a summer that swings from quiet to overwhelmed and back. The pests follow that rhythm, and the single biggest challenge is the **empty building**: a cabin that sits closed for weeks or all winter is an open invitation to mice, cluster flies, and wildlife, with no one there to notice until the season opens. BugBoss The X-Terminator is built for this. We're based southeast in Clintonville, we cover the Chain O' Lakes region every week, and we do **both pest control and wildlife removal**, which matters enormously in wooded lake country where a call about bats or raccoons is as common as one about mice.

## The Iola pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): opening up, and what moved in over winter

Spring in Iola is opening season, and it's when cabin owners find out what shared the building with them all winter. The first warm days heat the wall voids and wake the **overwintering cluster flies and boxelder bugs**, which crawl toward the windows by the dozen or the hundred — not a new invasion, but the population that slipped in last fall. Mice that wintered in a closed cabin have had months to nest, breed, and chew, and the evidence turns up the moment the door opens.

Spring is also **carpenter ant season**, and lake homes are especially prone because they hold moisture. Carpenter ants nest in **moisture-damaged wood** — around a leaky cabin roof, a damp sill plate, or a deck sitting on wet soil — and in April and May winged swarmers appear at the windows while big black ants show up indoors. Seeing them early, before the woods fully green up, usually means a nest is living inside the structure. Our [carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) page covers what to look for during opening.

By late spring, **wasp** queens are scouting nest sites under eaves, decks, and dock structures, and **ticks** turn active along the wooded edges of every lake lot — which in Iola is most of them.

### Summer (June–August): mosquitoes, the car-show surge, and the bat window

Summer is when the lakes and the crowds define the pest picture. The **Chain O' Lakes**, the **Tomorrow River**, and the wet ground between them make Iola a strong mosquito area, and waterfront yards feel it most in the evenings. A seasonal [mosquito](/pest-library/mosquitoes/) program that treats the shaded resting vegetation and knocks back breeding sites is what keeps a lakeside yard usable for the season.

Summer is also the **Iola Car Show**, and it's hard to overstate what that event does to a town this size. For a few days in July the area absorbs a flood of visitors, campers, and food vendors, and that surge concentrates pest pressure — flies, wasps, and rodents drawn to concentrated food and waste — on the campgrounds, lodging, and food service that host it. Stinging insects peak in this stretch too: paper wasps under overhangs, yellowjackets in the ground and in wall voids, colonies at their largest and most defensive by August. Most of our [wasp and hornet](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) calls come in now.

And summer is the one window when we **cannot** seal bats out of an Iola cabin or home. Wisconsin protects its bats, and the DNR requires that exits stay open **June 1 through July 31** during maternity season, when flightless pups are in the roost. Seal a building in July and you trap the pups inside — illegal, and a worse mess than the colony. If you find bats in summer, we schedule the exclusion for the legal window. The details are on the [bats](/pest-library/bats/) page and in our [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/).

### Fall (September–October): closing up before the invaders settle in

Fall is the most important season in Iola, because it's the window that decides whether a cabin comes through winter clean. As the nights cool, the **fall invaders** — [cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/), [boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/), and [Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/) — mass on warm south and west walls and work into every gap, heading for wall voids and attics. At the same time, **mice** push indoors as the fields and woods go cold, and an empty cabin is exactly the warm, undisturbed harborage they want.

Cluster flies deserve special mention in cabin country, because owners so often blame the building. They don't breed indoors; their larvae parasitize **earthworms** in the soil, so any lot surrounded by lawn, woods, or field grows its own population regardless of how the cabin is kept. The fix is an **early-fall exterior treatment** of the walls they gather on, plus sealing the gaps at soffits, windows, and penetrations. Our [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/) lays out the timing.

Fall is when **closing service** earns its keep. Sealing, treating, and inspecting a cabin before it's shut for the winter is the single most effective thing an owner can do, because it stops the problem before four months of empty quiet let it grow. Our [cabin and seasonal pest control guide](/guides/cabin-seasonal-pest-control/) is written for exactly this.

### Winter (November–February): the empty-cabin problem

By winter the outdoor invasion is over, and whatever got in during fall now has the run of a closed building. Mice breed year-round once they're warm and fed, so a handful in November can be an established colony by ice-out if nothing was sealed. A warm winter afternoon heats the wall cavities and stirs the overwintering flies, which gather at the windows of an empty cabin nobody's there to see. The trouble with an Iola winter is that no one is there to see any of it — which is why the exclusion and treatment done in fall matter so much more here than in a year-round home. For owners who want eyes on the place, a mid-season check catches problems while they're still small.

## Iola's housing stock: cabins, lake homes, and village houses

Pests exploit specific weaknesses, and Iola's building stock is dominated by a type that has its own particular vulnerabilities.

The **seasonal cabins and lake homes** around the Chain O' Lakes are the defining stock here. Many are older, built in eras and to standards that leave plenty of gaps: block or pier foundations, unsealed sill plates, log or board construction with settlement gaps, and additions bolted on over the decades. What makes them vulnerable isn't any single flaw — it's the combination of those gaps with **long stretches of vacancy**. A mouse or a cluster fly that gets into a lived-in house meets resistance; in an empty cabin it meets none. Wooded lots make it worse, giving squirrels, raccoons, and bats a direct path to the roofline.

The **year-round village homes** in Iola proper are more conventional but still leak at the usual points: garage-door corners, dryer-vent and utility penetrations, the seam where siding meets foundation, and soffit and fascia junctions that admit squirrels, bats, and fall invaders. And scattered through the surrounding countryside are the **farmhouses and outbuildings** that come with any Waupaca County town, with the rodent pressure that comes from living next to open field.

## Wildlife in Iola: cabins, attics, and the law

In wooded lake country full of older cabins, **wildlife work** is central to what we do, not a sideline. Bats roost in attics and behind cabin fascia; raccoons den in chimneys and torn soffits, especially females raising young in spring; squirrels chew into attics through weak roof edges; and skunks den under cabins, decks, and porches. ## Exclusion is the part that lasts

In cabin country the durable fix is **exclusion**, not repeated spraying — and it matters more here than almost anywhere, because the building spends so much time empty. A treatment clears what's present now; sealing the building keeps the next wave out during the months no one is watching. That's the work that pays off, and it's exactly where the national route model falls short, because sealing an older cabin properly takes time and attention that a quick perimeter spray never delivers.

On an Iola cabin or lake home, exclusion gets specific. We seal around **utility and pipe penetrations**, where the opening is always larger than the pipe. We close the **sill-plate gap** where the frame meets a block or pier foundation, and we look hard at the settlement gaps in log and board construction that give mice and cluster flies their way in. We address **soffit and fascia junctions** and weak roof edges along the wooded roofline, the routes squirrels, bats, and raccoons use. We check **door sweeps** on cabins and boathouses. And we treat **moisture** as part of the job — gutters, grading, crawlspace ventilation, damp sill plates — because moisture is what draws carpenter ants and dampness pests to a waterfront building. Done right before closing, that work is what lets an owner open the cabin in spring to a clean building instead of a season's worth of surprises.
