## A village built on the shore of the biggest lake in the county

Cecil sits on the east shore of **Shawano Lake**, a Shawano County village of roughly 530 people that got its name in 1884 from a railroad man, Cecil Leavitt, when the line reached the shore. That railroad is long gone as the reason people come here; the lake is the reason now. **Shawano Lake covers about 6,178 acres** with 18 miles of shoreline and a maximum depth around 42 feet — a large, shallow-to-moderate warm-water lake fed and drained through the Wolf River system, stocked with walleye, northern pike, largemouth bass, muskie, and panfish, and busy from ice-out through ice-fishing season. That single fact shapes almost everything about pest control in Cecil.

A lake community has a pest profile that a landlocked farm village does not. You've got a ring of **seasonal cottages and year-round lake homes** packed close to the water, a lot of them closed up for months at a stretch. You've got **boathouses, docks, decks, and sheds** — protected, humid structures that insects and wildlife move into. You've got constant **insect hatches off the water** that feed spiders and bats. And you've got the same fall-invader and rodent pressure every home in the region gets, made worse by all those empty buildings sitting dark and quiet through the exact months pests are looking for shelter.

## The Cecil pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): opening the cabin

Spring in Cecil is the "open up the cabin" season, and it's when a winter's worth of hidden problems shows itself all at once. Owners arrive to find **dead cluster flies and Asian lady beetles** piled at the windows and light fixtures — the survivors of the population that overwintered in the wall voids — plus **mouse droppings** in drawers, cushions, and cabinets where rodents nested undisturbed all winter.

This is also the start of **carpenter ant** activity. Lake homes are ideal for them: shoreline trees hold the outdoor parent colonies, and the perpetually damp wood of decks, docks, boathouse sills, and shaded cabin corners gives satellite nests a place to move in. Winged swarmers at a window in April, or large black ants trailing across a deck rail, mean it's time to look before the wood damage compounds. Our [carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) page walks through the difference between a foraging trail and an indoor nest.

By late spring the first **wasp** queens are scouting boathouse rafters and shed eaves, and **ticks** turn active along the brushy, wooded lots common on this shore. If your cottage is one you close and open on a schedule, spring is the smart time for a full inspection and an exterior treatment — everything you fix before the season sets the tone for the summer.

### Summer (June–August): the water works against you

Summer is the season Cecil exists for, and it's the season pests are worst. A large lake means constant **insect hatches** — midges, mayflies, and mosquitoes coming off the water in clouds — and that food supply drives everything up the chain. **Mosquitoes** breed in the ditches, marsh margins, boat covers, tarps, rain barrels, and clogged gutters around waterfront lots, not really in the open lake, so a seasonal [mosquito](/pest-library/mosquitoes/) program that hits resting vegetation and eliminates those small breeding sites is what buys back a summer evening on the dock.

**Spiders** show up in force because the lake feeds them. Webs blanket the lake-facing eaves, soffits, boathouse corners, and any lit wall, because that's where the hatch clusters after dark. **Wasps and yellow jackets** reach full, defensive size by August in the exact structures people use most — dock framing, deck undersides, boathouse rafters, and shed overhangs — which is when most of our [wasp and hornet](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) calls come from the lake.

Summer is also the one window we **cannot** seal bats out. Wisconsin law keeps bat exits open **June 1 through July 31** for the maternity season, when flightless pups are in the roost. Find bats in a Cecil attic or boathouse in July, and the right move is to plan the exclusion for August — not trap pups inside.

### Fall (September–October): close-up season and the invasion

Fall is the pivot of the whole year in Cecil, because two things collide. **Owners close up cottages** for the winter, and at the same moment the **fall invaders and rodents** are looking for exactly the warm, undisturbed shelter a closed cabin offers.

The fall invaders — [cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/), [boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/), and [Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/) — mass on the warm south and west walls, and a wall facing open water bakes in the afternoon sun, so lake homes get hit hard. They work into soffit, fascia, and eave gaps to overwinter. Cluster flies especially, because their larvae live off earthworms in the soil, are guaranteed on any lawn-and-shoreline lot regardless of housekeeping.

At the same time, **mice** push indoors as the nights cool — and a cabin about to be left empty for four months is the prize. The move that actually protects a Cecil property is a **fall exterior treatment plus exclusion done before you close up**: seal the entry points, treat the walls the invaders gather on, and lock the building down before it goes dark for the winter. See our [cabin and seasonal pest control guide](/guides/cabin-seasonal-pest-control/) for the full close-up checklist.

### Winter (November–February): the empty-cabin problem

Winter is when the cost of skipping the fall work comes due. **Mice** that got into a closed cabin have zero pressure on them — no people, no cat, no traps being checked — so they breed and expand through stored bedding, cushions, and pantry goods for months before anyone sees it. **Overwintering flies and lady beetles** stir on the first bright, mild day — warmed through the lake-facing wall they came in by — and gather at the windows of a cottage nobody's watching. And ice-fishing traffic keeps some Cecil properties busy enough that a heated cabin becomes a rodent magnet all its own. Winter is why the pros treat a lake home differently from an occupied year-round house: the fix has to hold on its own while the owner is away.

## Lake housing stock: where the pests actually get in

Cecil's building stock is a lake mix, and each type fails in its own way.

The **older seasonal cottages** — many built in eras when a summer cabin didn't need to be tight — have open eaves, unscreened gable vents, block or pier foundations, and plenty of gaps at the sill plate and around plumbing. They're the easiest buildings on the shore for mice, bats, and fall invaders to enter, and because they sit empty they get exploited hardest.

**Boathouses, docks, and lakeside sheds** are their own category. Humid, shaded, seldom-sealed, and full of protected voids, they're first-choice harborage for wasps, spiders, carpenter ants, and mice, and they act as a staging area right next to the house.

The newer **year-round lake homes** are tighter, but the weak points are still there: soffit and fascia junctions on those tall lake-facing walls, utility and generator penetrations, garage-door corners, and the decks and screen porches that give carpenter ants damp wood to work. We treat each type on its own terms — a 1950s cottage and a 2015 lake house do not get the same plan.

## Opening and closing a Shawano Lake property the right way

The single biggest thing a Cecil owner can do to control pests is treat the **open-up and close-up** as pest-control milestones, not just plumbing chores. When you close in fall, the building is about to sit dark, unheated or barely heated, and unwatched for months — the exact conditions mice, cluster flies, boxelder bugs, and lady beetles are hunting for. Sealing before that happens is worth more than any amount of spraying after.

A proper fall close-up means walking the exterior for the gaps that matter on a lake cottage: the soffit and fascia junctions on the tall lake-facing walls, gable and attic vents that have lost their screen, the sill plate where the framing meets the foundation, utility and water-line penetrations, garage and boathouse door corners, and any spot where an old cottage addition meets the original structure. Add an exterior perimeter treatment timed to the fall invaders, pull food and soft nesting material out of reach, and the place has a real chance of coming through the winter clean. Skip it, and spring open-up becomes a cleanup job. ## Bats and boathouses: the signature Cecil wildlife job

If there's one wildlife job that defines lake country, it's **bats**, and Cecil sees plenty. A large, shallow, weedy lake produces staggering insect hatches at dusk, and bats follow that food supply straight to the shoreline — then roost in whatever's handy, which on this shore means cottage attics, the peaks of open boathouses, and the gaps behind fascia and shutters. A colony can build for years in a seldom-visited attic before anyone notices the guano staining or the smell.

The work has to respect the **DNR maternity window of June 1 through July 31**, when flightless pups are in the roost and exits must stay open. Outside that window, the process is methodical: watch at dusk to find the actual exit points, install one-way devices that let bats leave but not return, wait at least a week to be sure the colony is out, then seal every gap and clean and treat the contaminated area. Done right, it's permanent. Done as a quick trap-and-release, it fails — the bats or their replacements are back next season. The full timing is in our [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/), and the bat biology is on the [bats](/pest-library/bats/) page.
