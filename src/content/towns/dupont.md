## High, wooded ground where the treeline sets the pest agenda

The **Town of Dupont** sits in the northern corner of Waupaca County, up toward Marion and Tigerton, and it stands out from its neighbors in one measurable way: it's **high ground**, roughly 900 feet of elevation, higher than the dairy towns to the south. It's a small township — around 700 people across some 35 square miles — and it's a **wooded one**, a patchwork of farm fields, hardwood woodlots, brushy fencerows, and hunting land. That mix of forest and field is the single fact that shapes pest control here, because in Dupont the woods are never far from the house.

Where a pure dairy town's pest pressure comes off the barn and the feed, Dupont's comes off the **treeline and the forest floor**. This is fall-invader country and tick country and attic-wildlife country, all driven by the same abundance of woodland edge. BugBoss The X-Terminator is set up for exactly this. We're a family-owned pest and wildlife company based in **Clintonville at 600 S. Main St.**, an easy run north to Dupont, and because we do both pest control and wildlife removal, we're one call for the insect problems and the something-in-the-attic problems that a wooded town generates in equal measure. ## The Dupont pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): the woods wake up

When the snow leaves Dupont's higher ground — a little later than down in the valleys — the overwintering insects wake first. Homeowners who thought their fall [cluster fly](/pest-library/cluster-flies/) and [boxelder bug](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/) problem was finished suddenly find the bugs again at the windows on a warm April afternoon. That's the same population that spent winter dormant in the wall voids and attic, not a new one.

Spring is prime **carpenter ant** season, and Dupont has more than its share thanks to all the trees. [Carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) nest in moisture-softened wood, and a wooded lot supplies endless outdoor parent-nest sites — dead limbs, stumps, log piles — from which satellite colonies work into the house through a damp sill or leaky roofline. Winged swarmers at the windows in April and May, or big black ants indoors while it's still cold, mean a nest is active in or on the structure. Catch it early and you save real structural repair.

By late spring the **ticks** are out in force. This is the season Dupont residents dread, and for good reason — the woods edge is crawling by May.

### Summer (June–August): ticks, stinging insects, and the bat window

Summer is when Dupont's wooded character bites hardest, literally. **Deer ticks** carrying Lyme disease live in the leaf litter and tall grass along every woodland edge, and a property where lawn runs up to the trees puts the whole family in the transition zone. Add wood ticks, and summer in Dupont means checking yourself after every walk to the shed. A [tick](/pest-library/ticks/) yard program that treats those edge zones — detailed in our [tick and Lyme yard program guide](/guides/tick-lyme-yard-program/) — is one of the highest-value services we offer here.

Stinging insects build all summer. Paper wasps under eaves, yellowjackets in the ground and in wall voids, and bald-faced hornets in the trees reach full, defensive size by August, which is when most of our [wasp and hornet](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) calls come in. Shaded, wooded lots also hold humidity, so **mosquitoes** stay active in the shade long after open ground has dried out.

Summer is the one window when we **cannot** seal bats out. Wisconsin protects its bats, and the DNR keeps roost exits legally open **June 1 through July 31** while flightless pups are present. Find [bats](/pest-library/bats/) in a Dupont attic in July, and the move is to schedule the exclusion for after the window — never to seal the building with pups inside. The full timing is in our [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/).

### Fall (September–October): the invasion

Fall is the defining season in Dupont, and it's when most homeowners call. As the nights cool, the **fall invaders** — cluster flies, boxelder bugs, and [Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/) — mass on the warm south and west walls and push into every soffit, fascia, and siding gap they can find, headed for the wall voids and attic to overwinter. On a wooded, high property like most of Dupont, the volume can be staggering; a south-facing wall can go black with lady beetles on a sunny October afternoon.

Cluster flies deserve a specific word because Dupont residents blame themselves for them and shouldn't. The larvae parasitize **earthworms** in the soil, so any home ringed by field, lawn, and forest floor grows a population every year no matter how clean it's kept. The fix is an **early-fall exterior treatment** of the walls where they gather, paired with sealing entry points — not a can of spray in the living room in February. Our [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/) covers the strategy in depth.

Fall is also when **mice** leave the cooling fields and woods for the warmth of the house, so September and October are the time to seal and treat both problems at once, before winter locks them in.

### Winter (November–February): what's in is in

By winter the outdoor invasion is done and the indoor situation is set. [Mice](/pest-library/mice/) that found a warm nest breed year-round once inside, so a few in November become a real infestation by February if the entry points aren't closed. A thaw in the dead of winter warms the wall voids and sends the overwintering cluster flies and lady beetles crawling back to the panes. Deer mice, common in rural Dupont, can carry hantavirus, which makes cleaning out an infested cabin or shed a job to take seriously. Winter work here is mostly **rodent exclusion, fall-invader cleanup**, and buttoning up the seasonal properties that got mice while nobody was watching.

## Woodlots, hunting land, and seasonal cabins

Dupont's forest cover doesn't just drive the insect calendar — it shapes the buildings that need protecting. Alongside the year-round farmhouses, the town has a real stock of **hunting cabins and seasonal properties**, and those come with their own pest logic. A building that sits empty for weeks at a time is an open invitation to mice, cluster flies, and the occasional bat or squirrel that finds a gap. Nobody's there to catch the problem early, so by the time the owner arrives for opening weekend, a small entry point has become an established nest.

The answer for seasonal property is the same as for a house, done before you close up: **seal the building envelope** — soffits, fascia, foundation, and utility penetrations — so nothing moves in over the off-season, and set up a monitoring program if the place sits empty for long stretches. ## The Dupont tick problem, in detail

Ticks earn their own section here because Dupont is genuinely one of the worse spots in the county for them, and because how you manage a yard makes a measurable difference. **Blacklegged (deer) ticks** — the species that transmits Lyme disease, and less commonly anaplasmosis and babesiosis — don't live in your mowed lawn. They live in the **transition zone**: the leaf litter, brush line, and tall grass where the maintained yard meets the woods, exactly the border most Dupont properties have in abundance. They quest by climbing to the tip of vegetation and waiting for a host to brush past, which is why the risk concentrates in a band a few feet wide along the treeline, the woodpile, the stone wall, and the trail into the woods.

That geography is also the opening for control. A yard tick program treats those **edge and transition zones** rather than blanketing the lawn, cutting the questing population where you actually encounter it. Reducing leaf litter, widening the mowed buffer, and moving the woodpile and bird feeders away from the house all help, and a targeted barrier treatment in late spring and again in early fall — the two activity peaks — knocks the population back hard. Deer are the tick taxi that restocks the woods, so properties that back onto heavy deer runs see more, and we account for that when we lay out the treatment. The full plan is in our [tick and Lyme yard program guide](/guides/tick-lyme-yard-program/); the biology is on our [ticks](/pest-library/ticks/) page.

## Not just cluster flies: the whole fall-invader complex

"Fall invaders" is a category, not a single bug, and Dupont's wooded ground grows the full set. Alongside the cluster flies, wooded properties here get **boxelder bugs** breeding on and around boxelder, ash, and maple, **Asian lady beetles** that arrived as an agricultural aphid predator and now overwinter in homes by the thousands, and increasingly **brown marmorated stink bugs**, a spreading invasive that shelters indoors the same way. They all exploit the same warm south and west walls and the same soffit, fascia, and window gaps, and they all overwinter in the wall voids and attic to reappear on the first warm days of late winter. Because they behave alike, the control is the same: an **exterior treatment in early fall** on the sun-warmed walls before they enter, plus sealing the entry points. What doesn't work is spraying them indoors in January — by then they're in the walls, out of reach, and killing the visible few does nothing about the thousands behind the plaster.

## Farmhouses and rural homes: where things get in

Dupont's year-round homes are mostly **older farmhouses and rural houses**, and they carry the standard weaknesses of rural construction. Fieldstone and block foundations develop mortar gaps. Sill plates go unsealed. Soffit and fascia junctions — especially on homes tucked against the trees, where squirrels and raccoons have easy roof access — give wildlife and fall invaders a way in. The oldest homes may have **balloon framing**, an open vertical cavity from basement to attic that lets a mouse entering at the foundation nest in the attic, and lets cluster flies entering at a soffit turn up in an upstairs bedroom.

In a town tucked this tight against the woods, these buildings reward a careful **exclusion inspection** more than any amount of spraying. Sealing the right gaps solves problems that years of traps and foggers never touched. ## Wildlife in Dupont: the attic is the front line

In a town this wooded, [wildlife removal](/wildlife-removal/) is core work, not a sideline. **Bats** roost in attics and behind fascia. **Squirrels** — with the whole forest as a launch pad — chew into attics and soffits through weak roof edges, and gray and red squirrels both turn up in Dupont ceilings. **Raccoons** den in chimneys, torn soffits, and outbuildings, especially spring females raising young. **Skunks** den under porches, decks, and sheds. The woods put more animals against more buildings here than in the open-field towns to the south.

With the whole forest feeding the roofline, a trap only buys a night's peace here; the line between a real wildlife job and a quick trap is **exclusion** — sealing the building so the animal, and the next one, can't get back in — with bats handled on the legal timeline. BugBoss does both, and we clean up the guano, latrines, and chewed insulation these animals leave. Our [attic wildlife removal guide](/guides/attic-wildlife-removal/) explains how we approach the most common Dupont wildlife jobs.

Bats deserve a specific note in a wooded town, because they're both a nuisance and a conservation story. Wisconsin's cave bats were hammered by **white-nose syndrome** after it reached the state in 2014, and four species are now state-threatened, with the northern long-eared bat also federally endangered. That's the reason the exclusion rules are strict and the maternity-season window is protected — the law is trying to keep a crashing population from losing its summer roosts and its pups. Doing bat work right in Dupont means respecting that timeline, evicting the colony humanely with one-way devices, and sealing only once every bat is confirmed out. Done wrong, you either break the law or seal live bats into your attic to die in the wall — a smell you won't forget. We do it right.
