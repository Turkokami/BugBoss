## The gateway to the Northwoods

Suring is a small Oconto County village — about **517 people** as of 2020 — sitting on the **45th parallel**, the halfway line between the equator and the North Pole, which the village marks with a sign. It sits where the **Oconto River** and **Peshtigo Brook** frame its southwest edge; the Menominee, who lived here for centuries before the 1836 Treaty of the Cedars, called the place *Naeqniw-Sīpiah*, "three rivers." The post office opened in **1897**, and the settlement — first nicknamed Mudville for its swampy ground — grew up on lumber, like most of this country did. Wisconsin Highway 32 runs north out of Suring toward Lakewood and into the **Nicolet National Forest**, which makes the village a genuine **gateway to the Northwoods**: the last real stop with services before the timber, the trout streams, the flowages, and the deer camps take over.

That gateway position defines the pest work here. Suring isn't a suburb or a resort strip — it's a small village surrounded by forest, field, and a dense scatter of **hunting cabins, deer camps, and seasonal properties** that sit empty most of the year. The pest pressure comes off the woods and the vacancy: mice and cluster flies by the season, carpenter ants in damp cabin wood, wasps nesting unnoticed in closed-up sheds, and a steady procession of wildlife into unheated attics and gable ends. BugBoss The X-Terminator is built to cover exactly this ground. We're based south in Clintonville, we run this northern corridor of Oconto County on purpose because the national brands treat the forest edge as the end of the line, and — the difference that matters most up here — we do **both pest control and wildlife removal**, with **no long-term contract**, which is exactly the flexibility cabin owners need.

## The Suring pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): opening up, swarmers, and the thaw

Spring in Suring is when cabin owners come back to camps that sat closed all winter — and find out what moved in. Overwintered mice have had months to breed undisturbed, and the **overwintering insects** — the [cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/) and [boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/) that came in last fall — wake on warm days and crawl to the windows by the dozen. This is the single biggest reason cabin owners call us in spring: they open the door to a mess that built up while nobody was watching.

Spring is also **carpenter ant season**, and the damp, wooded conditions around Suring are ideal for it — log and timber cabins, shaded lots, woodpiles stacked against buildings, and older farmhouses with softened wood. In April and May, winged [carpenter ant](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) swarmers appear at windows and big black ants show up indoors, and finding them while it's still cold usually means the nest is living inside the structure. By late spring, **wasp** queens scout eaves, cabin overhangs, and outbuildings, and **ticks** turn active along the forest edges — the same edges where people hike, hunt, and let dogs run.

### Summer (June–August): mosquitoes, stinging insects, and the bat window

The rivers, brooks, flowages, and low forest ground around Suring make **mosquito** pressure heavy all summer, worst at dusk in the cabin clearings where people want to be. A seasonal program treating shaded resting vegetation and reducing standing-water breeding keeps a dooryard or a camp usable.

Stinging insects peak now, and cabins take the worst of it: a paper wasp nest under an eave or a yellowjacket nest in a shed wall builds all season with no one there to notice, so owners often meet a large, defensive colony at their next visit. Bald-faced hornets hang their gray footballs in the surrounding trees. By August the colonies are at their largest, which is when most of our [wasp and hornet](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) calls come in.

Summer is the one window when we **cannot** seal bats out. Wisconsin protects its bats, and the DNR requires exits stay open **June 1 through July 31** during the maternity season, when flightless pups are in the roost. Seal then and you trap the pups — illegal, and a far worse mess. Bats in a cabin gable end or a farmhouse attic in July mean scheduling the exclusion for the legal window. See the [bats](/pest-library/bats/) page and the [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/).

### Fall (September–October): closing up, and the invasion

Fall is the make-or-break season in Suring, and it cuts two ways. As the nights cool, the **fall invaders** — cluster flies, boxelder bugs, and Asian lady beetles — mass on warm south and west walls and work into every gap, and **mice** pour indoors from the woods and fields as the ground goes cold. At the same time, cabin owners are closing up for the season, which is the single best moment to get ahead of the problem.

Cluster flies are near-universal here because their larvae parasitize **earthworms** in the soil, so a forest-edge cabin, a field-side farmhouse, or a village lot all grow their own populations regardless of housekeeping. The fix is an **early-fall exterior treatment** of the walls they gather on, plus sealing gaps at soffits, log joints, windows, and penetrations — the [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/) has the timing. For anyone locking up a camp for the winter, this is the moment to seal and treat before it sits empty; the [cabin seasonal pest control guide](/guides/cabin-seasonal-pest-control/) is written for exactly this situation. On the farms, harvest drives rodents toward feed and buildings all at once, so [farmhouse rodent exclusion](/guides/farmhouse-rodent-exclusion/) belongs on the fall list too.

### Winter (November–February): the empty-cabin problem

Winter is deer season and then a long stretch of quiet. In an occupied village home, winter work is rodent exclusion and cleanup of the fall invaders that now have the walls to themselves — mice breed year-round once warm and fed, so a few in November becomes a real problem by February. But the harder winter problem in Suring is the **empty cabin**. A camp that closed in fall without being sealed spends the whole winter as a rent-free rodent hotel, and the damage — chewed wiring, fouled insulation, a mouse-fouled kitchen — is what greets the owner in spring. The only real defense is exclusion done before the vacancy, not after. It's also worth remembering that a long, cold Northwoods winter extends the dormant period for the fall invaders — cluster flies and boxelder bugs that settled into the walls in October may not fully stir until a warm stretch in March or even April, which is why owners who thought a problem was over meet it again at spring opening. The population never left; the cold just held it in place. Sealing the walls the previous fall is what breaks that cycle for good, rather than treating the same insects season after season.

## Suring housing and building stock

Pests exploit specific weaknesses, and Suring's building mix hands them several.

The **hunting cabins, deer camps, and seasonal properties** are the defining type here, and they're a pest control challenge unlike a year-round house. Many are older, built simply, on shaded and wooded lots, with log or board construction, pier or block foundations, and the generous gaps that come with rustic building. Log joints, unsealed sill plates, and gaps around stovepipes and utility runs are all open doors, and the long stretches of vacancy mean nothing interrupts an infestation once it starts.

The **older village homes and farmhouses** tend toward fieldstone or block foundations, balloon framing, and decades of settlement gaps — balloon walls being open vertical channels from basement to attic. And the **farmsteads** between the village and the forest add pole barns, sheds, and feed storage that keep rodent and fly pressure running year-round; our [dairy and livestock farm pest control guide](/guides/dairy-farm-pest-control/) covers managing those building by building. ## Geography and ecology: the forest edge

Suring sits right on the line where Oconto County's farmland gives way to the **Nicolet National Forest** and the Northwoods, and that edge is what makes the pest range so wide. The **forest** brings carpenter ants, the [ticks](/pest-library/ticks/) that carry Lyme along every brushy edge, and relentless wildlife pressure — squirrels, raccoons, and bats all looking for a way into a cabin or a barn. The **rivers, brooks, and flowages** bring moisture and mosquitoes. The **fields and farms** are the reservoir for cluster flies and rodents that reload every fall.

Deep-woods country also means [deer mice](/pest-library/deer-mice/) are common — the rodent most associated with hantavirus in rural Wisconsin. A cabin that's been closed all winter can hold a heavy deer-mouse population and a lot of droppings, which is why that cleanup gets done carefully, with the right protection, rather than with a shop vac and a bare face. And because life up here happens in the woods — hunting, hiking, running dogs, working a camp — the ticks and stinging insects that live at the forest edge are a genuine health concern, not just a nuisance.

## Spiders, ground nests, and the rest of the Suring picture

Beyond the headline pests, forest-edge country produces its own supporting cast. **Spiders** gather thick on cabins because a porch light near the woods pulls in the flying insects they feed on, and a building nobody visits for weeks lets webs build undisturbed — heavy webbing at opening is a symptom of the insect supply and the vacancy, not a spider problem in itself. **Ground-nesting yellowjackets** are a real hazard on wooded acreage, where a hidden nest along a trail or woodpile goes untouched until someone stumbles onto it; treating the nest entrance is the fix. **Rodents chewing wiring** in an empty cabin are more than a nuisance — they're a fire and repair risk that owners discover at the worst possible time. And clover mites and other tiny invaders climb sunny south walls in spring, the same surfaces the cluster flies use, which is why the exterior wall gets treated as a whole rather than one pest at a time.

## Exclusion is the part that lasts in Suring

For a place this far out, and for cabins especially, **exclusion** isn't just the best fix — it's the only one that survives the stretches when nobody's around to react. A treatment clears what's present now; sealing the building is what holds through a season of vacancy. On a Suring cabin or farmhouse that means sealing around **utility runs, stovepipes, and chimney gaps**, closing the **sill-plate and log-joint gaps** where rustic construction leaves openings, addressing **soffit, fascia, and gable-end junctions** where bats, squirrels, and raccoons get in, and correcting the **moisture** that draws carpenter ants to shaded, damp wood. Because the animals and insects at the forest edge never stop testing the building, the sealed envelope — not the last treatment — is what actually keeps a camp livable year to year.
