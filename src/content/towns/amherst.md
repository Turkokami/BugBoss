## The Amherst pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): thaw, swarmers, and the farmhouse wake-up

Spring in Amherst begins with the farmhouse waking up. The first warm, sunny days heat the walls and attics, and the **[cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/) and [boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/)** that overwintered inside crawl toward the light at the windows — often by the dozen in an old farmhouse with generous wall voids. It looks like an invasion; it's really last fall's population leaving. Spraying them indoors does nothing lasting.

As the frost leaves the ground, **carpenter ant** swarmers appear. Winged ants at a window, or big black ants trailing across a farmhouse kitchen on a cold day, usually mean a nest inside the structure — and in old, moisture-prone farm wood near the river and millpond, indoor nests are common. Our [carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) page covers the signs. Spring is also when **[mice](/pest-library/mice/)** that wintered in the house or the barn are breeding hard, and when the barns and outbuildings come alive after the cold — wasps scouting the rafters, rodents moving between shed and house. Along the pasture and woodlot edges, **[ticks](/pest-library/ticks/)** wake as soon as the grass greens.

### Summer (June–August): millpond mosquitoes, stinging insects, and the bat window

Summer brings the water pests. The **Tomorrow River**, the **48-acre millpond**, and the wet valley bottom breed **[mosquitoes](/pest-library/mosquitoes/)** that rest through the day in shaded vegetation and come out to feed at dusk — right when a farm family wants to be outside. A seasonal program treating those resting sites and cutting standing water is what makes a riverside or pond-side yard usable in July and August.

Stinging insects peak now. Paper wasps under farmhouse eaves and porch roofs, yellowjackets in the ground and in barn wall voids, and bald-faced hornets in yard trees all grow toward their largest, most defensive colonies by late summer. Barns and outbuildings give them abundant sheltered nest sites, so a working farm often has several colonies going at once.

Summer is also the one window when we **cannot** seal bats out of an Amherst building. Wisconsin protects its bats, and the DNR requires exits stay open **June 1 through July 31**, the maternity season, when flightless pups are in the roost. Seal a farmhouse or barn attic then and you trap the young inside — illegal and a worse mess. If you find [bats](/pest-library/bats/) in summer, we schedule the exclusion for the legal window; the rules are in our [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/). Barns and old farmhouses are classic bat roosts out here, so this comes up often.

### Fall (September–October): the invasion off the fields

Fall is the decisive season in farm country. As nights cool, the **fall invaders** — cluster flies, boxelder bugs, Asian lady beetles, brown marmorated stink bugs — mass on the warm south and west walls in the afternoon sun and press into every gap: soffit and fascia junctions, window frames, and the countless seams of an old farmhouse. They're heading for the wall voids and attics to overwinter. Cluster flies are the signature fall pest here precisely because Amherst is surrounded by lawn, pasture, and field — their larvae parasitize **earthworms**, so the rural landscape grows an endless supply no matter how clean the house is. The control is an **early-fall exterior treatment** of the walls they gather on, plus sealing, and the timing is in our [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/).

Fall is also peak **rodent** season, and it's the biggest job of the year in Amherst. September through November, mice — and on farms, sometimes rats — pour out of the harvested fields and cooling woodlots toward the warm, food-rich shelter of houses, barns, and grain storage. An old farmhouse offers dime-sized gaps everywhere: sill plates, penetrations, foundation cracks, and the base of every door. Sealing before winter is the single most effective move, and our [farmhouse rodent exclusion guide](/guides/farmhouse-rodent-exclusion/) lays out the whole-property method.

### Winter (November–February): the reservoir problem

By winter the outdoor invasion is over, and the pests that matter are the ones already inside the house, the barn, and the grain storage. Mice breed year-round once warm and fed, so a handful in October becomes a colony by spring, chewing wiring, fouling stored feed and cupboards, and moving between outbuildings and the house. Deer mice on farms are a **hantavirus** concern, which makes cleanup as important as control. A midwinter thaw warms the wall voids just enough to send last fall's cluster flies back to sunny windows. Winter is the season for interior rodent work and for the inspection that finds where the invaders are getting in — because on a farm, an unsealed house and a mouse-filled barn keep feeding each other until the whole property is dealt with.

## Amherst's housing and building stock

Amherst's building stock is dominated by rural types with a lot of entry points.

The **older farmhouses** are the defining stock, and they're built to give pests every advantage: **fieldstone or block foundations, unsealed sill plates, balloon framing** that runs open from basement to attic, additions bolted on over generations, and the settlement gaps of a house that has stood a century. Ringed by open field, these homes meet constant rodent and cluster-fly pressure with the least resistance. The **village homes** in Amherst proper are more conventional but still leak at the usual points — soffits, penetrations, and the siding-to-foundation seam.

Then there are the **outbuildings** — pole barns, machine sheds, grain storage, and old dairy barns — and out here they're not an afterthought. They're prime harborage for mice, rats, wasps, and wildlife, and they act as reservoirs feeding the house nearby. Treating the farmhouse while ignoring the barn just leaves a source that reinfests it, which is why BugBoss works the whole property, not one building.

## The rest of the Amherst picture: rats, wasps, and stored-product pests

Farm country produces pests a village or suburb rarely sees. **Norway rats** turn up around grain storage, feed rooms, silage, and old dairy barns, and they're a bigger structural and health problem than mice — they burrow under slabs and foundations and gnaw through material a mouse can't. Where there's stored feed or grain, there are also **stored-product pests** — grain and flour beetles, meal moths — that infest bins, feed, and pantries and travel from the outbuilding into the house. And the sheer number of **wasp and hornet** nests on a working farm, tucked into eaves, rafters, machinery, and wall voids across several buildings, is something a single perimeter treatment never keeps up with.

There's a rural habit worth naming, too: firewood, feed, and equipment moved between the barn and the house carry insects and rodents straight across the threshold, which is why we look at how a property is used, not just how it's built. On a farm, the pest problem is a system spread across buildings, and it has to be treated as one.
