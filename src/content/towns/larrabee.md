## The Larrabee pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): the thaw and the exclusion window

When the frost leaves the ground, the first insects to reappear are ones that never left — **cluster flies** and **[boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/)** that wintered in wall voids and attics, now waking on warm days and crawling to farmhouse windows. It looks like a fresh invasion but it's the fall population finishing its winter indoors. Spring also brings **[carpenter ant](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/)** swarmers to the windows; winged ants in April or May usually mean a nest living in damp, softened wood inside the structure.

For a farm, though, spring's real value is the **rodent exclusion window**. The mice and rats that wintered in the barn and granary are breeding now, and sealing the farmhouse and securing feed storage before the growing season is the single move that pays off for the rest of the year. By late spring the first **wasp** queens are scouting shed rafters and eaves, and **ticks** turn active in the fencerows and woodlot edges.

### Summer (June–August): stinging insects and the bat window

Summer is stinging-insect season on a Larrabee farm. Paper wasps hang nests under eaves and inside open machine sheds, and yellowjackets set up in the ground along field edges and old foundations, growing large and defensive by August — right when someone reaches into a shed corner or mows over a ground nest. Our [wasp and hornet](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) calls peak here. Low, damp spots and any standing water around the farmstead keep [mosquito](/pest-library/mosquitoes/) pressure up through the season.

Summer is also the one stretch when we legally **cannot** seal bats out. The DNR maternity window runs June 1 through July 31, with flightless pups in the roost. If you find bats in a Larrabee barn or farmhouse attic in July, the right move is to plan the exclusion for later, not to seal the building and trap the colony inside — that's both illegal and a far bigger mess.

### Fall (September–October): the push indoors

Fall is the busiest season, and on a Larrabee farm it's driven straight off the land. As nights cool and crops come off, **[cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/)**, **boxelder bugs**, and **[Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/)** mass on the warm south and west walls of the farmhouse and work into every gap toward wall voids and attics. At the same time, **[mice](/pest-library/mice/)** and deer mice leave the harvested fields and fencerows and pour toward warm buildings, and rats around the barn get bolder as outdoor food thins.

Cluster flies are textbook here: their larvae parasitize earthworms in field and pasture soil, so a farmhouse surrounded by worked ground produces a heavy population no matter how clean the house is. The fix is an **early-fall exterior treatment** in September of the walls they gather on, paired with sealing the soffit, fascia, and foundation gaps they use — everything you seal in fall pays off across the whole winter. Our [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/) lays out the timing.

### Winter (November–February): what's already inside

By winter the outdoor invasion is over and you're living with whatever got in during fall. Mice that found a warm, feed-rich nest breed year-round indoors, so a handful in November becomes a real infestation by February if the entry points aren't sealed. A warm January day is all it takes to rouse the cluster flies and boxelder bugs holed up in the farmhouse walls; they turn up at the sunniest windows while the barn's mice keep breeding out of sight. Winter is rodent-cleanup and exclusion season, and — being just down the road — the quiet stretch when we can get the sealing done that keeps next fall's wave out.

## How farm rodents actually behave — and what that means for control

Getting rid of rodents on a Larrabee farm starts with understanding how they live. **House mice** are homebodies with a small range — often just ten to thirty feet from the nest — which is why they colonize a barn or farmhouse in pockets and why scattered traps miss most of the population. They breed relentlessly: a single female can produce dozens of young in a year, so a problem that looks minor in October is a serious one by spring. **Deer mice** range farther and move in from the fields as cold sets in, carrying the hantavirus concern that makes cleanup a careful job. **Norway rats** are the heavy hitters — larger, burrowing along foundations and manure pits, gnawing through surprisingly tough material, and capable of real structural and wiring damage. They're also neophobic, wary of new objects, which is why a rushed bait placement often gets ignored for days.

That behavior dictates the method. Effective control means placing monitored traps and bait where rodents actually travel — along walls, beam runs, and burrow entrances — not out in the open. It means securing feed so the colony loses its food base, because a well-fed rodent ignores bait. And it means sealing the building so that as the population drops, nothing refills it. Skip any one of those legs and the problem simply regrows.

## The exclusion sequence on an older farmhouse

Sealing an old farmhouse against rodents and fall invaders is methodical work, and it pays to know what it involves. We start at the **foundation and sill plate** — the seam where the wood framing meets the masonry is often the single biggest highway into an old house, and it's frequently hidden behind a deck, porch, or overgrown planting. From there we close **utility penetrations** (water lines, electrical, gas, dryer and bath vents), which are almost always oversized and stuffed loosely if at all. We screen **foundation and crawlspace vents**, seal the corners and thresholds of **garage and service doors**, and work the **soffit, fascia, and roofline** where fall invaders, squirrels, and bats get in. Each opening gets the right material for the job — rodent-proof mesh, sealant, or hardware cloth — because a mouse will chew straight through foam or steel wool alone. Done properly, this is what turns a recurring seasonal problem into a solved one, and it's detailed in our [farmhouse rodent exclusion guide](/guides/farmhouse-rodent-exclusion/).

## The health stakes around livestock and feed

Rodents on a working farm aren't only a nuisance and an expense; they're a health issue. **Norway rats** around manure, feed, and water can spread leptospirosis and shed salmonella into feed that livestock — and people — then contact. **Deer mice** carry hantavirus, which is why a droppings-laden feed room or granary should never be dry-swept: that aerosolizes the hazard. Rodent contamination of stored feed is also a straight economic loss, spoiling far more than the animals actually eat. And accumulated **bat guano** in a long-used barn can harbor the fungus behind histoplasmosis. We handle removal, exclusion, and contaminated-material cleanup with those risks in view, so a rodent job doesn't quietly become a health exposure.

## Why DIY usually falls short on a farm

A jug of bait and a flat of snap traps from the co-op have their place, but on a Larrabee farm they rarely end the problem, because they treat the mice in front of you and ignore the system feeding them. Clear the kitchen and the barn refills it within weeks. Knock the numbers down with bait and they rebound once the bait's gone, because nothing sealed the gaps or secured the feed. The wasps you can reach get sprayed; the nests in the shed rafters don't. Real, lasting control on a farm is a source-feed-and-structure problem — find where they live, cut off what feeds them, and close how they get in — which is exactly the coordinated work a DIY pass leaves undone.
