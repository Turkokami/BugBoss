## The Pulaski pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): thaw, swarmers, and reawakening

Spring in Pulaski wakes two things at once. The **overwintering insects** — cluster flies, boxelder bugs, the odd lady beetle — that spent winter in wall voids and attics start moving toward the light and appearing at windows. It looks like a fresh invasion but it's the fall population stirring, and it flags where the building leaks.

Spring is also **carpenter ant** season, and Pulaski gets it in both housing types. Older homes offer aged wood and decades of minor leaks; newer homes offer moisture around decks, window and door flashing, and roof-edge leaks that people assume new construction is immune to. April and May bring large black ants indoors and, in mature colonies, winged **swarmers** at the windows — and swarmers inside while it's still cold means the nest is in the heated structure, not foraging in. Catching it early saves wood-repair money. See the [carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) page. By late spring **wasp** queens are scouting eaves and deck structures, and **ticks** turn active along the woodlot and field edges that border Pulaski's growing subdivisions.

### Summer (June–August): stinging insects, events, and the bat window

Summer is stinging-insect season, and in Pulaski it collides with the event calendar. Paper wasps and yellow jackets peak in size and aggression in August — the source of most stings and most of our [wasp and hornet](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) calls. Big summer gatherings, above all **Pulaski Polka Days**, concentrate food, waste, and crowds in the July and August heat, which draws yellow jackets, flies, and rodents to event grounds, food vendors, and nearby restaurants and halls — a good reason for venues to schedule stinging-insect and sanitation-pest control ahead of the event.

Summer is also the one stretch we **cannot** seal bats out. Wisconsin protects its bats, and the DNR keeps one-way exits open **June 1 through July 31** during the maternity season, when flightless pups are in the roost. Find a colony in your Pulaski attic in July and the move is to schedule exclusion for after the window — not seal the building and trap pups inside, which is illegal and a bigger mess. Larger newer homes with complex, multi-plane rooflines actually give bats more soffit and ridge gaps to exploit, not fewer. The timing is on the [bats](/pest-library/bats/) page and in our [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/).

### Fall (September–October): the busiest season, subdivisions included

Fall is the defining pest season in Pulaski, and it doesn't spare the new neighborhoods. As the nights cool and the surrounding crops come off, the fields release two waves at once. **Fall invaders** — [cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/), [boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/), and [Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/) — swarm warm south and west walls and push into every seam toward wall voids and attics. At the same time, **mice** begin their annual move indoors as the harvested fields turn cold.

This is where Pulaski's growth matters. Homeowners in newer subdivisions are often surprised to get hammered by cluster flies and mice, assuming those are old-house problems. They're not. Cluster fly larvae **parasitize earthworms** in field soil, and Pulaski's subdivisions back right up to that soil, so a two-year-old house on the edge of the village produces the same fall population as a farmhouse — it just lets them in through soffit returns, weep holes, and utility penetrations instead of a stone foundation. The fix is the same either way: an **early-fall exterior treatment** plus sealing entry points, the method in our [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/). Everything sealed in September pays off across the winter.

### Winter (November–February): what's already inside

By winter the outdoor invasion is over, and whatever bothers you indoors got in during fall. **Mice** that found a warm, food-rich nest breed year-round — a few in November become an infestation by February if the gaps stay open. A warm winter afternoon heats the wall voids in both the old village-core homes and the newer subdivision builds, and the overwintering cluster flies drift back out to the windows in each alike. And winter is prime **bed bug** season, moving with holiday travel and Pulaski's lodging and metro connection. Winter work here is mostly rodent exclusion, cleanup of the fall invaders, and dealing with anything that came home in a suitcase.

## Two kinds of housing, two sets of entry points

Pulaski's growth means we work two very different building types, sometimes on the same street.

The **older Polish-era homes** in the village core have the classic settled-town weak points: stone or block foundations, some balloon framing, and decades of settlement gaps. Balloon-framed walls are open vertical runs from sill plate to attic, so a mouse entering low nests high, and a cluster fly at a soffit gap turns up upstairs. These reward a careful exclusion inspection.

The **newer subdivision homes** are tighter and better sealed on paper, but they have their own predictable leaks — and homeowners are often caught off guard because they expect a new house to be pest-proof. The reality is that fall invaders and mice exploit **construction junctions**: soffit returns and ridge vents, weep holes in brick veneer, gaps around utility and HVAC penetrations, garage-door corners, and unsealed sill plates behind the siding. A complex roofline with multiple planes gives more of these gaps, not fewer. New construction changes where you seal, not whether you have to.

And on the **rural edges**, across the surrounding townships in all three counties, you get the full farm picture: pole barns, outbuildings, and older farmhouses with the rodent and wildlife pressure that comes with living next to open field. We treat all three on their own terms, and our [farmhouse rodent exclusion guide](/guides/farmhouse-rodent-exclusion/) covers the rural sealing work.

## Beyond the fall invaders: the rest of the Pulaski pest picture

Cluster flies and mice lead the list, but a growing village with a real commercial district and farm edges carries a broader mix, and each gets its own treatment.

**Nuisance ants** are one of the most common calls we get from Pulaski's newer subdivisions. Pavement ants trailing up out of driveway and patio cracks, odorous house ants in the kitchen — these aren't the wood-damaging carpenter ants, and spraying the visible trail just scatters them. The fix is finding the colony and treating it at the source. Our [ants](/pest-library/ants/) page covers the difference between nuisance ants and carpenter ants, which matters because people often confuse the two.

**Spiders** show up wherever the insect supply is good, which in Pulaski means well-lit newer homes, garages, and porches that pull in flying insects at night. Spiders follow their food, so the durable control is an exterior program that knocks down the insects they feed on, not sweeping webs off the eaves. See the [spiders](/pest-library/spiders/) page.

**Mice** deserve their own note because Pulaski's new construction gives homeowners a false sense of security. A tight, modern house still has the gaps mice exploit — around plumbing and HVAC penetrations, at garage-door corners, behind the siding at the sill — and with farm fields close by, a dime-sized opening is all it takes. Sealing those construction junctions is the fix; the [mice](/pest-library/mice/) page walks through where they get in.

**Ticks** are a growing concern as Pulaski's subdivisions push against woodlots, trails, and field edges. Deer ticks carry Lyme disease and wait in the tall grass along those edges, so families with kids and dogs backing up to green space benefit from a perimeter [tick and Lyme yard program](/guides/tick-lyme-yard-program/).
