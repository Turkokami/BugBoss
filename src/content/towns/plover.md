## The Plover pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): the sandy-ground wake-up

Spring in Plover starts under the ground as much as above it. As the frost leaves the well-drained sand, the soil warms and dries fast, and **ground-nesting wasps and ants** get an early start — earlier than on the heavier clay soils east of here. The first warm, sunny days also wake the **[cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/) and [boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/)** that overwintered in walls and attics; they drift to the windows and fool homeowners into thinking a new invasion has started, when really they're watching last fall's population leave.

**Carpenter ant** swarmers appear as the ground thaws — winged ants at a window or big black ants trailing indoors on a cold day usually mean a nest inside the structure, most often tied to a moisture leak. Our [carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) page explains what to look for. Along the Little Plover River corridor and the wet, low spots of the outwash plain, **[ticks](/pest-library/ticks/)** wake early where mowed lawn meets brush. And in the newer subdivisions, spring is when [mice](/pest-library/mice/) that wintered indoors are breeding and the first paper wasp queens scout the eaves and deck corners of new homes for nest sites.

### Summer (June–August): wasps, mosquitoes, and the bat window

Summer is peak **stinging-insect** season, and Plover feels it hard because of the soil. The well-drained Central Sands ground is easy to dig, so **ground-nesting yellowjackets and digger wasps** thrive in the open, sunny, disturbed ground of new subdivisions and graded lots. Paper wasps build under the eaves and deck rails of newer homes, and bald-faced hornets nest in trees. Colonies grow toward their largest and most aggressive by late summer, and nest calls stack up fast in neighborhoods full of decks, patios, and playsets.

Water drives the **[mosquito](/pest-library/mosquitoes/)** picture. The Little Plover River, the Wisconsin River, the irrigation ponds serving the region's cropland, and the low, damp pockets that dot flat outwash ground all breed mosquitoes, and they rest by day in shaded vegetation before coming out at dusk. A seasonal program treating those resting sites and cutting standing water is what keeps a Plover backyard usable on a summer evening.

Summer is also the one window when we **cannot** seal bats out of a Plover building. Wisconsin protects its bats, and the DNR requires exits stay open **June 1 through July 31**, the maternity season, when flightless pups are in the roost. Seal an attic then and you trap the young inside — illegal and a worse mess. If you find [bats](/pest-library/bats/) in summer, we schedule the exclusion for the legal window; the full rules are in our [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/).

### Fall (September–October): the invasion, even in new houses

Fall is the make-or-break season, and Plover homeowners with newer houses are often the most surprised by it. As nights cool, the **fall invaders** — cluster flies, boxelder bugs, Asian lady beetles, brown marmorated stink bugs — mass on the warm south and west walls in the afternoon sun and press into every gap: soffit returns, window frames, weep holes, and the seam where siding meets slab. New construction is not immune; the gaps are just newer. Cluster flies in particular grow right out of the ground here — their larvae parasitize **earthworms**, so any Plover lot near lawn, field, or river bottom raises its own supply no matter how new or clean the house is. The control is an **early-fall exterior treatment** plus sealing, and the timing is in our [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/).

Fall is also peak **rodent** season. September through November, mice push in from the surrounding fields and open ground, and on Plover's sandy soil they burrow easily right up against foundations and slabs, then slip in at the utility penetrations and garage-door corners that every house — new or old — leaves open. Sealing before winter is the single most effective move a homeowner can make.

### Winter (November–February): what's already inside

By winter the outdoor invasion is done, and the pests that matter are the ones already in. Mice breed year-round once warm and fed, so a few that came in during October become a colony by spring, chewing wiring and fouling cupboards and pantries. A midwinter thaw warms the wall voids just enough to send last fall's cluster flies and lady beetles back to sunny windows — the same insects, not a new wave. Winter is the season for interior rodent work and for the inspection that finds exactly where the fall invaders are getting in, so we can seal it before they return. In a Plover subdivision, that inspection often turns up a builder's gap nobody ever closed.

## Geography and ecology: the Central Sands and a trout stream

Plover sits squarely in the **Central Sands** — a flat glacial outwash plain of well-drained sandy soil that defines both the region's famous irrigated **potato and vegetable farming** and its pest profile. Sand drains fast, warms early, and digs easily, which favors **ground-nesting wasps and digger bees**, lets **rodents and voles** burrow against foundations, and dries the surface so that pests concentrate around the moisture a house and its irrigation provide.

Water still shapes the picture. The **Little Plover River**, a spring-fed trout stream, threads through the village; the **Wisconsin River** runs the western edge; and irrigation ponds and low, damp outwash pockets dot the surrounding cropland. All of it breeds **mosquitoes** and feeds the **cluster flies** whose larvae need the earthworm-rich soil of lawns and field edges. Where the village backs up to wooded corridors and the river, edge habitat brings **ticks** and gives squirrels, raccoons, and bats their route to the roofline. ## Wildlife in Plover: attics, chimneys, and the law

Even in a newer village, wildlife finds a way in, and BugBoss handles it as a core service rather than a sideline. **Squirrels** chew into attics through soffit and roof-edge gaps — including on relatively new homes where a construction gap or a lifted shingle gives them a start. **Raccoons** den in chimneys and torn soffits, especially females raising young in spring, and they're strong enough to open a soffit or roof edge. **Bats** roost in attics and behind fascia. **Skunks** den under decks, porches, and sheds — and a graded subdivision lot on sandy soil is easy digging for them.

## The rest of the Plover picture: voles, ants, and spiders

A few pests are underappreciated in Plover precisely because it's newer and sandier. **Voles** — meadow mice — thrive in the turf and landscaping of new subdivisions, runwaying under snow and mulch and chewing bark and roots; on well-drained sand with fresh plantings, they can do real damage to a young yard, and they occasionally push toward foundations. Small trailing **nuisance ants** — pavement ants especially — colonize the disturbed, sandy fill around new slabs and driveways, then trail indoors after moisture and crumbs; the sand under and around a new home is close to ideal for them. And **spiders** collect in new garages and soffits following the insects drawn to exterior lights, which is why controlling the insect supply matters more than knocking down webs.

It's also worth naming a habit that catches new-home owners off guard: mulch, firewood, and landscape timbers stacked against a fresh foundation give ants, wasps, and rodents a bridge over whatever sealing was done. In a subdivision where everyone is still building beds and hardscape, the yard itself often creates the pressure — something a franchise perimeter spray never addresses.
