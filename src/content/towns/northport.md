## The Northport pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): snowmelt, moisture, and the first movers

Spring in the Little Wolf valley starts wet. Snowmelt and spring rain raise the river and saturate the bottomland, and that moisture is the season's defining feature for pests. **[Carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/)** wake and start foraging, and river-valley homes give them exactly what they want — damp sill plates, humid crawlspaces, and shaded framing. Winged carpenter ant swarmers at the windows in April and May point to a nest living inside heated, moisture-softened wood.

Meanwhile the fall invaders finish their winter indoors: **cluster flies** and **[boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/)** that overwintered in wall voids wake on warm days and crawl toward the light at windows. That's not a new invasion — it's last fall's population leaving. Spring is also the smart time for **rodent exclusion** before the growing season, sealing up the farmhouse while the mice that wintered indoors are breeding. By late spring the first **wasp** queens are scouting eaves and soffits, and **ticks** turn active along the river vegetation and woodlot edges.

### Summer (June–August): mosquitoes, stinging insects, and the bat window

Summer is when the river earns its reputation. The Little Wolf's slow backwaters, floodplain pools, and the low wet spots across the valley are prime **[mosquito](/pest-library/mosquitoes/)** habitat, and riverside yards feel it most — on the deck, at the dock, at every summer gathering. A seasonal mosquito program that treats resting vegetation and knocks back standing-water breeding sites is one of the more valuable services for property along the water.

Summer is also stinging-insect season. Paper wasps build under eaves and porch roofs, and yellowjackets set up in the ground and in wall voids, growing large and defensive by August — which is when most [wasp and hornet](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) stings and calls happen. And summer is the one stretch when we legally **cannot** seal bats out: the DNR maternity window runs June 1 through July 31, with flightless pups in the roost. If you find bats in a Northport attic in July, the move is to plan the fall exclusion, not to seal the building and trap the colony inside.

### Fall (September–October): the busiest season

Fall is the peak of the year in Northport, and it's a double hit. As nights cool and the fields come off, **[cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/)**, **boxelder bugs**, and **[Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/)** mass on the sunny south and west walls and work into every gap, heading for wall voids and attics to overwinter. At the same time, **[mice](/pest-library/mice/)** and deer mice leave the harvested fields and the river cover and push toward warm buildings.

Cluster flies are worth understanding, because the valley is heavy cluster-fly country. Their larvae parasitize earthworms in field, pasture, and river-bottom soil — and moist ground grows a lot of earthworms — so any home surrounded by that ground produces a heavy population regardless of housekeeping. Spraying them indoors in November does nothing. The answer is an **early-fall exterior treatment** in September of the walls they gather on, paired with sealing the soffit, fascia, and foundation gaps they use. Our [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/) walks through the timing.

### Winter (November–February): living with what got in

By winter the invasion is over and you're living with whatever established during fall. Mice that found a warm, food-rich nest breed year-round indoors, so a handful in November becomes a real infestation by February if the entry points aren't sealed. A warm January day heats the wall voids and sends the cluster flies that wintered there crawling toward the sunniest windows in the house. Winter is rodent-cleanup and exclusion season in the valley, and the quiet stretch to do the sealing that keeps next fall's wave out.

## Moisture is the master key in the river valley

If there's one building-science idea that explains most of Northport's pest trouble, it's this: moisture drives everything. A damp crawlspace or basement doesn't just risk rot — it becomes a climate that draws and sustains pests. **[Carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/)** specifically seek out wood that has been softened by chronic dampness, so a sill plate sitting above a wet crawlspace, or a rim joist behind years of condensation, is prime nesting real estate. **Springtails** and other moisture pests bloom in damp soil and around foundations and then wander indoors by the hundreds. Even rodents prefer the humid, sheltered underside of a poorly sealed crawlspace as a runway into the house.

The fixes are mostly about controlling water and air. A crawlspace needs a proper vapor barrier over bare soil, foundation vents that are screened but functional, downspouts and grading that move roof water away from the wall, and dry firewood stored off the ground and away from the siding. Get the moisture down and you remove the welcome mat for a whole category of pests at once — which is why our inspections on river-valley homes always start below the floor, not just at the doors and windows.

## Knowing the signs on a river-valley property

Early detection saves money and structure. **Rodents** leave tapered droppings along baseboards and in cupboards, greasy rub marks on their travel routes, gnaw damage on wood and wiring, and rustling in the walls at night. **Carpenter ants** drop coarse frass — sawdust flecked with insect parts — beneath the framing they're hollowing out, and large black ants trailing indoors in late winter mean an interior nest, not a stray forager. **Bats** leave dark guano and oily staining below a fascia gap or gable vent, plus the unmistakable smell of a summer roost. And a sudden mass of black-and-red or dull-gray insects on a warm south wall in fall is the fall-invader signature — the moment to treat the exterior, before they're in the walls.

## The health stakes near the water

Some of Northport's pests carry real risk. Standing water in the river bottom breeds the **[mosquitoes](/pest-library/mosquitoes/)** that can transmit West Nile virus in Wisconsin, which is reason enough to take breeding-site reduction seriously rather than just swatting on the deck. The brushy edges hold the deer **[ticks](/pest-library/ticks/)** that spread Lyme disease, a genuine concern for anyone spending time in tall grass or woods. **Deer mice** in rural homes are the regional carrier of hantavirus, so dusty droppings in a cabin or outbuilding should never be dry-swept — that aerosolizes the hazard. And **bats**, while ecologically valuable and worth excluding humanely, are the state's main rabies vector, which is why direct handling is never a DIY job.
