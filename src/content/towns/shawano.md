## The county seat on the lake

Shawano is the largest town for a long way in any direction — about 9,200 people, the seat of Shawano County, and the commercial anchor for a rural region that stretches north into the timber country and south toward Clintonville. It sits where **Shawano Lake**, one of the biggest lakes in this part of Wisconsin at roughly 6,000 acres, drains into the **Wolf River**, and that combination of a working downtown, a major recreation lake, and open farm-and-forest country on every side is exactly what makes the pest picture here so varied. The city takes its name from **Menominee Chief Sawanoh**, whose people lived on the lake, and it grew up in the 1840s as a sawmill town on the strength of the pine that the Wolf River floated down to the mills. When the timber ran out in the early 1900s, Shawano turned into the service and tourist hub it still is — the place the surrounding towns drive to for groceries, hardware, a hospital, and a summer week on the water.

That regional-hub status matters for pest control in a way most people don't think about. Shawano has denser housing and a bigger commercial base than the villages around it, plus a heavy band of lakefront cottages and year-round lake homes, plus the usual rural farmsteads at the edges. ## The Shawano pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): thaw, swarmers, and the first movement

When Shawano Lake gives up its ice and the ground warms, the first thing we see is movement. Mice that spent the winter in a wall void or a lake cottage start breeding and ranging, and the **overwintering insects** — the cluster flies and boxelder bugs that slipped in last fall — wake up on warm days and crawl toward the windows by the dozen. Homeowners think it's a new invasion. It isn't. It's the same population coming out of dormancy, and it's a signal that the building was never sealed.

Spring is also **carpenter ant season**, and Shawano has the conditions for it in spades: mature hardwoods along the lakeshore and the river, older cottages that have taken on decades of small roof and plumbing leaks, and decks and docks sitting on damp ground. In April and May, winged carpenter ant swarmers show up at windows and big black ants appear indoors. Seeing them early — while the lake is still cold — usually means the nest is living inside the heated structure rather than foraging in from a stump outside. Our [carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) page walks through the difference. By late spring, **wasp** queens are scouting nest sites under eaves and soffits, and **ticks** turn active along every edge where mowed lawn meets the brush and woodland that ring the lake.

### Summer (June–August): lake mosquitoes, stinging insects, and the bat window

Summer in Shawano is defined by the water and the crowds it draws. Shawano Lake, the Wolf River, and the marshy ground between them make this a serious **mosquito** area, and the lakefront feels it worst in the evenings, right when people want to be on the deck or the dock. A seasonal [mosquito](/pest-library/mosquitoes/) program that treats the shaded vegetation where mosquitoes rest during the day, and reduces the standing water they breed in, is what keeps a waterfront property usable from June through September.

Stinging insects peak in this stretch, too. Paper wasps tuck nests under overhangs and boat-house eaves, yellowjackets set up in the ground and in wall voids, and bald-faced hornets build their big gray footballs in lakeside trees. By August the colonies are at their largest and most defensive, and that's when most of our [wasp and hornet](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) calls come in.

Summer is also the one window when we **cannot** seal bats out of a Shawano building. Wisconsin protects its bats, and the DNR requires that exits stay open **June 1 through July 31** during the maternity season, when flightless pups are in the roost. Seal the building then and you trap the pups inside — illegal, and a far worse mess than the colony. If you find bats in a Shawano attic in July, the right move is to schedule the exclusion for the legal window, not grab a caulk gun. The details are on our [bats](/pest-library/bats/) page and in the [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/).

### Fall (September–October): the busiest season on the lake

Fall is the defining season for Shawano pest control, and it's when the phone rings most. As the nights cool, the **fall invaders** — [cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/), [boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/), and [Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/) — mass on the warm south and west walls of homes and cottages and work into every gap they can find, heading for the wall voids and attics to overwinter. At the same time, **mice** begin their annual push indoors as the fields and woods go cold.

Cluster flies deserve a special note in Shawano because so many people misread them. They don't breed in your trash; their larvae parasitize **earthworms** in the soil, so any property surrounded by lawn, woods, or field — which is most of Shawano and virtually every lake lot — grows its own local population no matter how clean the house is kept. The fix is an **early-fall exterior treatment** of the walls they gather on, paired with sealing the gaps at soffits, windows, and penetrations. Our [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/) lays out the timing. For lakefront owners closing up a cottage for the season, fall is also the moment to seal and treat before the building sits empty — the [cabin and seasonal pest control guide](/guides/cabin-seasonal-pest-control/) covers that work.

### Winter (November–February): the payoff and the pests already inside

By winter the outdoor invasion is over, and whatever got in during fall now has the building to itself. Mice breed year-round once they're warm and fed, so a handful in November becomes a real infestation by February if the entry points aren't closed. A warm January day heats the wall voids and sends the overwintering cluster flies drifting back to the lake-facing windows. And winter is **travel-pest season** — holiday trips and hotel stays bring [bed bugs](/pest-library/bed-bugs/) home as hitchhikers, a problem that has nothing to do with cleanliness and everything to do with luggage. Winter work in Shawano is mostly rodent exclusion, cleanup of the fall invaders, and anything that came home in a suitcase.

## Shawano's housing stock: lake cottages, downtown homes, and farmsteads

Pests exploit specific weaknesses, and Shawano gives them three distinct building types to work on.

The **lakefront cottages and year-round lake homes** on Shawano Lake are the type that surprises people. Many are older, built in eras that left generous gaps: pier and block foundations, unsealed sill plates, additions bolted on over the decades, and the settlement gaps that come with a building on soft lakeshore soil. Combine those gaps with **stretches of vacancy** — a lot of these are seasonal — and you have a building that invites mice, cluster flies, and wildlife to move in without resistance. The wooded lots make it worse, handing squirrels, raccoons, and bats a direct path to the roofline.

The **older downtown and near-downtown homes** in Shawano proper, dating to the sawmill era and the decades after, tend toward stone or block foundations, balloon framing, and decades of small settlement gaps. Balloon-framed walls are open vertical channels from basement to attic, which is how a mouse entering at the sill plate ends up two stories up. And the **farmsteads and rural properties** ringing the city bring the full agricultural picture — pole barns, outbuildings, and older farmhouses with the rodent pressure that comes from living next to open field. We treat each on its own terms rather than running one blanket program on every address.

And the wooded shorelines are both prime **wildlife** country and prime habitat for the **ticks** that carry Lyme disease, which thrive exactly where mowed ground meets brush — the edge that defines nearly every lake lot and rural yard here.
