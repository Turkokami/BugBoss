## Menasha is a multi-unit town, and that changes the work

Most of what BugBoss does out in the rural corridor is single-structure work: a farmhouse, a pole barn, a cabin. One building, one owner, one problem.

Menasha is different, and it is different in a way that decides the outcome of a job. A large share of the housing here is **shared structure** — older rental stock, mill-era buildings that have been converted and subdivided, duplexes and small apartment buildings, and the tight postwar blocks on and around Doty Island. In a shared building, the boundary between "your problem" and "the building's problem" is not the wall. It is nothing at all.

That single fact is why bed bug and cockroach infestations in a town like this so often run for years while everyone involved is convinced that treatment does not work.

## The scope problem, which is the real problem

Here is the pattern, and it repeats almost exactly:

A tenant in unit 4 reports [bed bugs](/pest-library/bed-bugs/). Unit 4 gets treated. Six weeks later unit 4 is fine and unit 3 reports bed bugs. Unit 3 gets treated. A few months on, unit 4 has them again. By the second year the landlord believes the tenants keep re-introducing them and the tenants believe the landlord is using a company that cannot do the job.

Nobody in that story is being unreasonable, and nobody is right either. What is happening is that the population is **moving through the building** — along plumbing chases, electrical runs, shared wall voids, and hallways — staying ahead of a treatment that is always aimed at one unit at a time. Each treatment works. The scope is wrong.

The same mechanism drives [cockroaches](/pest-library/cockroaches/), which travel the same routes, and rodents, which use them plus the gaps around old plumbing penetrations to move between floors.

The fix is not a better chemical. It is **inspecting and scheduling the building as one job**: establishing which units are actually involved rather than which ones have complained, treating them together, and following up, because eggs hatch after the first visit and something has to be there when they do.

## If you rent, or if you own the building

This is the part that generates the most conflict and the most delay, so it is worth stating clearly.

Wisconsin has **no bed-bug-specific statute**. Responsibility falls out of the landlord's duty to keep the property habitable, the terms of the lease, and who introduced the infestation — which, with bed bugs, is essentially never provable. In practice the landlord normally has to arrange treatment and the tenant has to prepare the unit and allow access.

Our [Wisconsin landlord and tenant pest control](/compliance/landlord-tenant-pest-control/) and [bed bugs in rentals](/compliance/bed-bugs-in-rentals/) pages go through this properly.

The practical advice does not depend on how the responsibility question resolves:

- **Report it in writing immediately**, and keep a copy. Verbal reports vanish.
- **Do not treat it quietly yourself.** A retail spray scatters the population into the neighboring units and makes the eventual professional job harder and more expensive.
- **Do not move furniture or a mattress into another unit, a hallway, or a storage room.** This is the single most reliable way to spread it.
- **Do not throw out the mattress** before getting advice. An encasement traps what is inside and it dies there, which is cheaper and works better than replacement — and dragging an infested mattress down a shared stairwell seeds the whole building.

## The older building stock

Menasha grew up around the water and the mills, and the building stock reflects it. Near the channel and on the island there is a lot of nineteenth and early twentieth century structure, much of it converted, subdivided, or added to more than once.

For pest work, the features that matter are:

- **Open and shared wall cavities**, which let insects and rodents move between units and floors without ever crossing a doorway.
- **Old plumbing chases** running vertically through the building, which are pest highways and are almost never sealed at the floor penetrations.
- **Additions meeting original structure**, where the joint has settled and opened. These are reliable entry points and they are almost always on the exterior where nobody looks.
- **Settled masonry and original window openings**, which give [mice](/pest-library/mice/) and fall invaders a way in at the foundation and the sill.

Exclusion in a building like this is genuine construction work — hardware cloth, flashing, mortar, mechanical fasteners — not a tube of caulk on a Saturday. It is also the part that has lasting value, because it works on every pest that uses the same opening.

## The water: what we can help with and what we can't

Menasha sits at the north end of **Lake Winnebago**, on the Fox River channel and the locks, which produces two very different situations.

**The lake hatches — midges and mayflies — are not a pest control problem.** They come off open water in enormous numbers on their own schedule, they do not breed on your property, and nothing sprayed on a building meaningfully affects a hatch. We are not going to sell you a treatment for them, and anybody who offers one is selling you something that will not work. What genuinely helps is screening, adjusting exterior lighting so the building is less of a beacon, and cleaning up afterward.

**Mosquitoes are a different case entirely**, because they breed in standing water on your own property — the clogged gutter, the tarp, the tire, the saucer under the planter, the low spot that holds water for a week after rain. That responds well to source reduction plus targeted treatment of the shaded resting sites where the adults spend the day. Our [mosquito and tick control](/residential-pest-control/mosquito-tick/) page covers the method.

The moisture that comes with living near water also drives the basement nuisance pests — [springtails](/pest-library/springtails/), [millipedes](/pest-library/millipedes/), [sowbugs](/pest-library/sowbugs-pillbugs/), [centipedes](/pest-library/centipedes/). Every one of those is a damp indicator first. If your Menasha basement is producing them, the productive conversation is about the dehumidifier, the downspouts, and the grading, and we will say so rather than putting you on a program.

## Single-family homes too

None of the above means we only work on apartment buildings. The postwar single-family neighborhoods away from the water get the ordinary Fox Valley mix — [ants](/residential-pest-control/ant-control/) in spring, [wasps](/residential-pest-control/stinging-insects/) building on eaves and playsets through summer, [mice](/residential-pest-control/rodent-control/) moving in every fall, and [fall invaders](/residential-pest-control/fall-invaders/) on the sunny walls in late September.

The fall invader work in particular is worth timing rather than reacting to: an exterior treatment in **late August or early September**, before the insects mass on the siding, is the difference between a quiet winter and finding ladybugs at the upstairs window in February. Our post on [why bugs turn up indoors in January](/blog/bugs-indoors-in-january/) explains what is actually happening in the wall.

We also do [wildlife removal and exclusion](/wildlife-removal/), which most of the pest companies working the Fox Valley do not — bats, squirrels, and raccoons in a structure, plus the sealing and cleanup that finish the job.
