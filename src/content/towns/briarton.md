## A crossroads that farming built, and time thinned out

Briarton is small — the kind of place you'd drive through without noticing if you didn't know it was there. It sits in the **Town of Lessor** in southeastern Shawano County, a crossroads community that was busier a century ago than it is today. Its post office ran from 1883 to 1904, and by the 1920s the little settlement supported a couple of general stores, a sawmill, and a farm-implement dealer — the ordinary commercial core of a farm community when the horse-and-wagon radius still defined where people shopped. Then the world moved. In October 1950, **State Highway 47 was rerouted around Briarton**, and the old road through the settlement became "Old 47." The through-traffic went with it, and the community settled into what it is now: a quiet rural crossroads surrounded by working farmland.

That history matters for pest control in a concrete way. What's left around Briarton is **aging building stock** — old farmhouses, former commercial buildings, barns, and outbuildings, some still in daily use and some winding down — sitting in the middle of open fields. Old buildings and open farmland are the two things that drive pest pressure hardest, and Briarton has both in abundance. BugBoss The X-Terminator is built for exactly this. We're based just south in Clintonville at **600 S. Main St.**, and a scattered farm crossroads off Old 47 is normal territory for us, not an inconvenient dot at the edge of a route map. The work here is honest and predictable: rodents, fall invaders, carpenter ants in tired wood, and the wildlife that comes with barns and woodlots.

## The Briarton pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): thaw and the first movement

When the frost leaves the ground, the mice that wintered in the walls, sheds, and old buildings get active and start breeding, and homeowners hear scratching return. It isn't a fresh invasion — it's the population that overwintered indoors. The overwintering [cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/) and [boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/) in the wall voids also crawl toward the windows on warm days.

Spring is prime **carpenter ant** season in Briarton's older buildings, and there's a lot of vulnerable wood here. Decades-old farmhouses and long-standing outbuildings collect the moisture damage carpenter ants nest in — leaky roofs and gutters, damp sill plates, old porch and addition framing. Winged swarmers appear at windows in April and May, and if you're seeing big black ants indoors while it's still cold out, a nest is living inside the heated structure. That's a job for finding and treating the nest, not spraying the trail. See the [carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) page for the full picture.

By late spring, wasp and yellowjacket queens are scouting nest sites under barn eaves and porch soffits, and blacklegged **ticks** get active in the brushy edges.

### Summer (June–August): flies, stingers, and the bat window

Summer around Briarton is fly and stinging-insect season. On the working farms, **house flies, stable flies, and face flies** breed in manure and spilled feed and build up fast by midsummer. Around the buildings, **wasps and hornets** nest under eaves, in wall voids, and in the ground, reaching their largest and most defensive size by August — which drives most of the season's [wasp and hornet](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) calls.

Summer is also the one stretch when bats **cannot** be sealed out. Wisconsin protects its bats, and the DNR requires exits stay open **June 1 through July 31** while flightless pups are in the roost. Briarton's barns and old attics make good roosts, so if you find a colony in July, the move is to plan the exclusion for August or later — not to seal the building and trap pups inside, which is illegal and far messier. The [bats](/pest-library/bats/) page and the [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/) cover the timing.

### Fall (September–October): the rodent push and the invasion

This is the season that defines pest work around Briarton and the reason most people call. When the corn and beans come off and the nights cool, **field mice and deer mice** move out of the harvested fields and fencerows toward the nearest warm structure — and the older buildings here offer dozens of entry points. At the same moment, the **fall invaders** mass on sunny south and west walls: cluster flies, boxelder bugs, and [Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/), all working into gaps to overwinter.

Cluster flies are worth a word because rural Briarton gets them heavily and people misread them. They don't breed in garbage — their larvae parasitize **earthworms in the soil**, so any building ringed by lawn and field grows a local population regardless of how clean it's kept. The fix is an **early-fall exterior treatment** of the walls plus sealing the entry points, not a spray can in the kitchen in January. Same logic for the [mice](/pest-library/mice/): fall sealing is what actually holds.

### Winter (November–February): what's already inside

By winter the outdoor movement is over, and whatever got in during fall is what you live with. Mice that found a warm, food-rich nest in a wall or an old shed breed year-round indoors — a handful in November becomes a real infestation by February if the gaps stay open. A mild, sunny day in the dead of winter warms the wall voids in these old crossroads buildings and brings the overwintering cluster flies back out to the glass. Deer mice in a seldom-used building are a **hantavirus** concern, so winter cleanup should be damp and careful, not swept dry. Winter work in Briarton is rodent exclusion, cleanup, and knocking down the fall invaders.

## Old buildings, open fields: Briarton's building science

Pests exploit specific weaknesses, and Briarton's mix of aging structures gives them plenty.

The **old farmhouses and former commercial buildings** are the core challenge. Many sit on fieldstone or block foundations with unsealed sill plates, and older ones are balloon-framed, so the wall cavities run uninterrupted from basement to attic — an open vertical highway. A mouse that enters at the sill ends up nesting in the attic, and cluster flies that enter at a soffit gap show up in an upstairs room. Decades of small settlement gaps, retrofitted wiring and plumbing penetrations, and add-on porches multiply the entry points. Nothing around this Old 47 crossroads gives back more for a slow, careful exclusion inspection than one of these aging farmhouses or former storefronts.

The **barns, sheds, and grain storage** are the other half of the picture. Big doors never seal at the bottom corners, service penetrations leave gaps, and stored feed and grain are a standing invitation. These outbuildings are usually the reservoir that keeps re-supplying the house with rodents, which is why we treat the whole property as one system rather than working on the house alone. And any building that's slipping out of regular use — an idle store, a retired shed — becomes quiet, undisturbed harborage exactly the way rodents like it.

## Ecology: fields, fencerows, and woodlots

Briarton is farmland first, and the land dictates the pests. The **open fields** are a rodent reservoir that floods toward buildings each fall. The **fencerows and grassy ditches** are covered travel corridors that carry mice and voles right up to your foundation. The **worm-rich soil** of cropland and lawn is what cluster flies breed in, which is why open country gets them worse than a village lot. And the **woodlots** scattered through the Town of Lessor hold carpenter ants' outdoor parent nests, stage squirrels and raccoons before they find your roofline, and grow the brushy edges where [ticks](/pest-library/ticks/) wait. It's ordinary Shawano County farm ecology — and it's the environment BugBoss works in every day.

## Old wood, and the pests that live in it

Briarton's history as a sawmill and general-store crossroads left behind a lot of old lumber in its buildings, and aging wood attracts a specific set of pests beyond carpenter ants. In damp, older structures we run into **wood-boring beetles** — powderpost beetles in particular — whose larvae tunnel through hardwood over years and leave the tell-tale scatter of fine, flour-like frass under small round exit holes. They work slowly and quietly, which is why people often don't notice until a beam or a piece of trim starts to feel soft. We also see **springtails and psocids (booklice)** in the chronically damp basements and crawlspaces common to buildings this old; they're harmless but they signal a moisture problem that will invite worse tenants if it isn't corrected.

The through-line with all of these is moisture. Old buildings that have gone decades with minor roof leaks, poor drainage, or a damp fieldstone basement create exactly the conditions that wood pests and moisture pests need. Treating the insects without addressing the water just resets the clock. That's why an honest inspection here looks as hard at gutters, grading, and crawlspace ventilation as it does at the pests themselves.

## Spiders and the incidental invaders

In a rural crossroads like Briarton, **spiders** are a year-round fixture, and mostly a beneficial one — they eat the other insects. Cellar spiders hang in basement corners, and in fall, as the fall invaders and other bugs move toward the building, the spiders follow their food supply right along with them. Most are harmless, and the honest answer is that a house next to open field will always have some. What a treatment does is knock down the population and, more importantly, cut off the insects the spiders are feeding on by handling the fall invaders and sealing entry points. Reduce the prey and the shelter, and the spiders thin out on their own. You can read more on the [spiders](/pest-library/spiders/) page. The same "follow the food and warmth" logic drives the occasional [ant](/pest-library/ants/) trail indoors and the other incidental invaders that turn up in an old farmhouse — deal with the conditions, not just the individual bug.
