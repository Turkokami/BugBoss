## The county seat at the mouth of the river

Oconto is where the **Oconto River** finally runs out of land and empties into **Green Bay**, and that meeting of river, bay, and open country is the whole story of pest pressure here. It's the seat of Oconto County, a city of about **4,609 people** as of 2020, incorporated back in **1869**, and one of the oldest continuously settled places in the state. The ground under Oconto holds a **Copper Culture** burial site dated to roughly 5,000 to 6,000 BC — arguably the oldest known cemetery in Wisconsin, now preserved at Copper Culture State Park on the west edge of town. Father Claude-Jean Allouez said the first Mass here in 1669. Oconto grew up on lumber floated down the river, then on commercial fishing off the bay and on the paper mills that followed the sawmills, and traces of all three still shape the town — a working waterfront, an old and settled housing core, and farm-and-woodlot country pressing in from the west.

For a pest company, that history translates into three very different jobs stacked inside one small city. There's a band of **bay-front and river-adjacent property** with all the moisture and wildlife that water brings. There's an **old downtown housing stock** full of the gaps that a century of settling leaves behind. ## The Oconto pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): thaw, swarmers, and the first movement

When the bay ice breaks up and the ground thaws, the first thing we see in Oconto is movement. Mice that spent the winter in a wall void start breeding and ranging, and the **overwintering insects** — the [cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/) and [boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/) that slipped in last fall — wake on warm days and crawl toward the windows. People read it as a fresh invasion. It's the same population coming out of dormancy, and it's a plain signal that the building was never sealed.

Spring is also **carpenter ant season**, and Oconto's older homes give the ants everything they want: mature hardwoods along the river, decades of small roof and plumbing leaks, and porches and sills sitting on damp ground near the water table. In April and May, winged [carpenter ant](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) swarmers appear at windows and big black ants show up indoors. Seeing them early — while the bay is still cold — usually means the nest is living inside the heated structure rather than foraging in from a stump outside. By late spring, **wasp** queens are scouting nest sites under eaves and soffits, and **ticks** turn active along every edge where mowed lawn meets the brush and woodland west of town.

### Summer (June–August): bay mosquitoes, stinging insects, and the bat window

Summer in Oconto is defined by the water. The river mouth, the marshy bay shore, and the low ground between them make this a serious **mosquito** area, and the waterfront feels it worst at dusk. A seasonal program that treats the shaded resting vegetation and reduces standing-water breeding is what keeps a bay-front yard or a river dock usable from June into September.

Stinging insects peak now, too. Paper wasps tuck nests under overhangs and boathouse eaves, yellowjackets set up in the ground and in wall voids, and bald-faced hornets build their big gray footballs in shoreline trees. By August the colonies are largest and most defensive, and that's when most of our [wasp and hornet](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) calls come in.

Summer is also the one stretch when we **cannot** seal bats out of an Oconto building. Wisconsin protects its bats, and the DNR requires that exits stay open **June 1 through July 31** during the maternity season, when flightless pups are in the roost. Seal the building then and you trap the pups inside — illegal, and a far worse mess than the colony itself. If you find bats in an attic in July, the right move is to schedule the exclusion for the legal window. The details are on our [bats](/pest-library/bats/) page and in the [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/).

### Fall (September–October): the busiest season on the bay

Fall is the defining season for Oconto pest control, and it's when the phone rings most. As the nights cool, the **fall invaders** — cluster flies, boxelder bugs, and Asian lady beetles — mass on the warm south and west walls of homes and work into every gap they can find, heading for the voids and attics to overwinter. At the same time, **mice** begin their annual push indoors as the fields and marsh edges go cold.

Cluster flies deserve a special note in Oconto because so many people misread them. They don't breed in your trash; their larvae parasitize **earthworms** in the soil, so any property surrounded by lawn, field, or bay-shore ground grows its own local population no matter how clean the house is kept. The fix is an **early-fall exterior treatment** of the walls they gather on, paired with sealing the gaps at soffits, windows, and penetrations. Our [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/) lays out the timing. For anyone closing a river or bay cottage for the season, fall is also the moment to seal and treat before the building sits empty — the [cabin and seasonal pest control guide](/guides/cabin-seasonal-pest-control/) covers that work.

### Winter (November–February): the payoff and the pests already inside

By winter the outdoor invasion is over, and whatever got in during fall now has the building to itself. Mice breed year-round once they're warm and fed, so a few in November becomes a real infestation by February if the entry points aren't closed. A warm January day off the bay heats the wall voids and sends the overwintering cluster flies drifting back to the windows. Winter work in Oconto is mostly rodent exclusion, cleanup of the fall invaders, and the occasional hitchhiking bed bug brought home from holiday travel. It's also the ideal time to plan spring bat and wildlife exclusions, since the calendar for that work is set months ahead.

## Oconto's housing stock: bay-front, downtown, and farmstead

Pests exploit specific weaknesses, and Oconto hands them three distinct building types.

The **bay-front and river-adjacent homes** are the type that surprises people. Many are older, built in eras that left generous gaps — pier and block foundations, unsealed sill plates, additions bolted on over the decades, and the settlement gaps that come with a building on soft, damp waterfront soil. High water tables keep basements and crawlspaces humid, which is exactly what draws carpenter ants and dampness pests. Combine that moisture with a wooded or marshy lot and you've handed squirrels, raccoons, and bats a direct path to the roofline.

The **older downtown and near-downtown homes** in Oconto proper date to the lumber and paper era and the decades after. They tend toward fieldstone or block foundations, balloon framing, and a century of small settlement gaps. Balloon-framed walls are open vertical channels from basement to attic, which is how a mouse entering at the sill plate ends up two stories up, and how a cluster fly at a soffit gap ends up in an upstairs bedroom. And the **farmsteads and rural properties** west of the city bring the full agricultural picture — pole barns, outbuildings, and older farmhouses with the steady rodent pressure that comes from living next to open field. Our [farmhouse rodent exclusion guide](/guides/farmhouse-rodent-exclusion/) is built for that last group. We treat each type on its own terms rather than running one blanket program on every address.

## Geography and ecology: the river, the bay, and the fields

The **Oconto River** and **Green Bay** are the center of the local ecology, and water drives pest pressure in several directions at once. The river mouth and the shallow bay margins are prime **mosquito** habitat. The moisture around waterfront buildings draws **carpenter ants, springtails, and other dampness pests**. And the shoreline brush and the woodlots west of town are both prime **wildlife** country and prime habitat for the [ticks](/pest-library/ticks/) that carry Lyme disease, which thrive right where mowed ground meets tall grass and brush — the edge that defines nearly every bay-shore and rural lot here.

The open farm country beyond the city is the reservoir that reloads every autumn. Cluster flies come off the field soil, house mice and [deer mice](/pest-library/deer-mice/) come out of the harvested fields and fencerows, and the whole cycle repeats. Deer mice matter for more than nuisance — they're the rodent most associated with hantavirus in rural Wisconsin, which is why cabin and outbuilding cleanup gets done carefully, not with a shop vac and a bare face.

## Spiders, ants, and the rest of the Oconto picture

Beyond the mice, cluster flies, and wildlife, a bay-shore town produces its own supporting cast. **Spiders** climb the complaint list on waterfront property, because a porch or dock light draws the flying insects spiders feed on, and a building that sits quiet between visits lets webs build undisturbed.
