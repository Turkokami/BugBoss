## A railroad village where the pests come off the farms and out of the woods

Tigerton is a village of about 708 people in southern Shawano County, laid out in 1896 along the rail line that once ran the Lake Shore Railroad through this corner of Wisconsin from 1879 until 1981. The village takes its name from **Tiger Creek**, a fast, noisy little stream said to roar like a tiger in high water, and it sits where that creek and the **Embarrass River** cut through a landscape of dairy farms, hayfields, and hardwood woodlots. This is not flat prairie farmland — it's the rolling, glaciated edge of the Northwoods, where cleared pasture butts right up against standing timber. That geography is the single biggest thing shaping pest pressure in Tigerton, and it's why the pest and wildlife picture here looks different from a tidy subdivision down in the Fox Valley.

## The Tigerton pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): thaw, swarmers, and reawakening

When the frost comes out of the ground and the woodlots green up, Tigerton's pest year restarts. The first thing we see is **overwintering insects waking up** — the cluster flies and boxelder bugs that spent the winter dormant in wall voids and attics crawl toward the warmth and light of windows on the first mild days. Homeowners who thought their fall bug problem was gone find it isn't; it's the same population, not a new one.

Spring is also **carpenter ant season** in Tigerton, and the village is prime habitat for them. Between the mature hardwoods along Tiger Creek and the Embarrass River, the older wood-framed farmhouses with decades of small roof and plumbing leaks, and the porches and decks sitting close to damp ground, there's no shortage of the moisture-softened wood carpenter ants tunnel into. In April and May winged swarmers show up at windows, and large black ants start appearing indoors. When they turn up early — while it's still cold outside — that's a strong sign the colony is nesting inside the heated structure rather than just foraging in. Catching that early saves real money in structural repair. Our [carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) page walks through how to tell a nest from a trail.

By late spring the first **wasp** and **hornet** queens are scouting nest sites under eaves, in soffit returns, and inside outbuildings, and **ticks** turn active along every edge where a Tigerton lawn meets brush or woods.

### Summer (June–August): stinging insects, mosquitoes, and the bat window

Summer is stinging-insect season. Paper wasps build under porch roofs and overhangs, yellow jackets nest in the ground and in wall voids, and bald-faced hornets hang their gray paper footballs in trees and on the sides of barns and sheds. By August those colonies are at full size and at their most defensive, which is when most stings happen and when our [wasp and hornet](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) calls peak. Tigerton's low, damp spots along the creek and river, plus stock tanks and standing water on farm properties, keep **mosquito** pressure up all season — worth a seasonal [mosquito](/pest-library/mosquitoes/) program if you host summer gatherings.

Summer is also the one stretch of the year when we **cannot** seal bats out of a Tigerton attic or barn. Wisconsin protects its bats — four cave-bat species are state-threatened and the northern long-eared bat is federally endangered — and the DNR requires that bat exit points stay open **June 1 through July 31**, when flightless pups are in the roost. If you find bats in July, the right move is to plan the exclusion for the legal window, not to seal the building and trap pups inside, which is both illegal and a far worse mess. We lay out the timing on the [bats](/pest-library/bats/) page and in the [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/).

### Fall (September–October): the busiest season in Tigerton

Fall is the defining season here, and it's what drives most first-time calls. As the nights cool and the crops come off the surrounding fields, two things happen at once. **Fall invaders** — [cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/), [boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/), and [Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/) — mass on the sunny south and west walls of the house and work into every crack they can find, heading for the wall voids and attic to overwinter. And **mice** begin their annual march indoors as the fields go cold and the harvest removes their outdoor cover and food.

Cluster flies deserve a special note in Tigerton because so many people misunderstand them. They don't breed in garbage or dirty dishes — their larvae parasitize earthworms in the soil, so any home surrounded by lawn, pasture, or field produces a local population no matter how spotless the house is. In a village ringed by dairy farms and hayground, that's essentially every property. The effective fix is an **early-fall exterior treatment** of the walls they gather on, paired with sealing entry points, rather than chasing them with a spray can in January. Everything you seal and treat in September pays off across the whole winter. Our [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/) has the full playbook.

### Winter (November–February): what's already inside

By winter the outdoor invasion is over, and whatever got in during fall is what you're living with. Mice that found a warm, food-rich nest have no reason to leave, and they breed year-round once they're indoors — a couple in November becomes a genuine infestation by February if the entry points stay open. A run of bright, thawing days in late winter warms the wall cavities enough to rouse the cluster flies and beetles wintering there, and they drift back toward the windows. Winter is also travel-pest season: holiday trips bring [bed bugs](/pest-library/bed-bugs/) home in luggage. Most of our Tigerton winter work is rodent exclusion, cleanup of the fall invaders, and dealing with anything that hitchhiked back from a trip.

## Tigerton's housing stock is the other half of the story

Pests don't get into a building at random — they exploit specific weaknesses, and Tigerton's building stock has a very particular set.

The **older farmhouses** scattered through the village and across the surrounding town are the most rodent- and insect-friendly structures we work on. Many have fieldstone or block foundations with mortar that's cracked and settled over a century, and a lot were **balloon-framed**, meaning the wall cavities run uninterrupted from the sill plate all the way to the attic. That's how a mouse entering at the foundation ends up nesting in the attic insulation, and how cluster flies slipping in at a soffit gap turn up in an upstairs bedroom. Of everything we service around Tigerton, these balloon-framed farmhouses give an exclusion inspection the most to chew on.

The **village homes** along Tigerton's residential streets are tighter but still have the usual weak points — garage-door corners, dryer-vent and utility penetrations, gaps where siding meets the foundation, and soffit and fascia junctions that let in squirrels, bats, and fall invaders.

And then there are the **agricultural outbuildings** that define farm country: pole barns, machine sheds, grain and feed storage, and milk houses. These are the toughest rodent environments in the area because they combine large, deliberately open access points with a constant food source. You can't seal a pole barn like a house, so the strategy shifts to controlling the population with a system of tamper-resistant stations and knocking it down before it migrates to the farmhouse. Our [farmhouse rodent exclusion guide](/guides/farmhouse-rodent-exclusion/) and [dairy farm pest control guide](/guides/dairy-farm-pest-control/) cover how we approach both.

## Geography and ecology: the Embarrass River, Tiger Creek, and the woodlots

The **Embarrass River** and **Tiger Creek** running through and around Tigerton are genuine pest drivers. ## Spiders, and the pests that follow the pests

One thing homeowners rarely connect: **spiders in Tigerton are usually a symptom, not the root problem.** Cellar and cobweb spiders set up wherever there's a steady insect food supply, and the fieldstone basements, damp crawlspaces, and cluttered outbuildings common here are ideal for them. Every cluster fly, box elder bug, and gnat that gets into the structure feeds the spiders, so a house with a heavy fall-invader load tends to grow a heavy spider population right behind it. That's why our approach to [spiders](/pest-library/spiders/) starts with cutting off the food and sealing the same gaps that let the fall invaders in — knock down the prey and the exclusion holds, and the spider numbers fall on their own. We also see the ordinary nuisance [ants](/pest-library/ants/) that trail in from foundation cracks in warm months, distinct from the structural carpenter ants above.

## Getting ahead of it: exclusion on a Tigerton property

The single most valuable thing a Tigerton homeowner can do is close the building envelope before the fall push, and on the balloon-framed farmhouses common here that means thinking vertically. Mice and fall invaders both exploit the same weak points, so the priority list is consistent: seal the gaps where the siding meets the foundation and where the sill plate sits on the masonry, screen or close the soffit and gable vents that open into the attic, pack the annular gaps around water, gas, electrical, and dryer-vent penetrations, and add door sweeps to garage and service doors. On the older fieldstone foundations, cracked and missing mortar joints are the classic rodent entry, and they're easy to miss because they're low and often behind shrubs or grade. Because a balloon-framed wall is an open chute from basement to attic, sealing low keeps a mouse from ever reaching the upper floors in the first place. On a farmstead, the barn and sheds get the same walk-through — you can't seal them tight, but you can cut the obvious openings and control the population before it bridges to the house. This is exactly the inspection BugBoss runs, and it's the work that makes the difference between a one-time treatment and a recurring problem.
