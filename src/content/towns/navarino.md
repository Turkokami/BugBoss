## A community on the edge of 15,000 acres of marsh

Navarino is defined by what surrounds it. The community sits in southern Shawano County against the **Navarino Wildlife Area** — over **15,000 acres** of state land spreading across southern Shawano and northeastern Waupaca counties, about seven miles south of the city of Shawano. This isn't a tidy park. It's a working mosaic of **sedge meadow, emergent marsh, floodplain forest, bottomland hardwoods, bog, aspen and oak woods, pine plantation, and sandy upland ridges**, cut through by the **Wolf River** and the **West Branch of the Shioc River**. People come here to hunt, fish, trap, canoe, and bird, and the Navarino Nature Center runs trails and programs along the edge.

That's a wonderful place to live next to. It's also, from a pest and wildlife standpoint, one of the most demanding environments in the region — and it's exactly why BugBoss The X-Terminator does both pest control and wildlife removal under one roof. We're based just south in Clintonville at **600 S. Main St.**, a short drive from the marsh edge. When you live on the boundary of a 15,000-acre wetland and wildlife corridor, the animals aren't a rare event; they're a constant flow past your buildings. Every open soffit, torn vent, uncapped chimney, and unsealed sill plate gets found — and found fast. Understanding the corridor, and building the structure to keep it out, is the whole game here.

## The wetland-edge pest and wildlife calendar

### Spring (March–May): denning season on the corridor

Spring is when the corridor's mammals go looking for places to raise young, and structures near the marsh are prime real estate. **Raccoons** — females especially — pry into chimneys and torn soffits to den. **Squirrels** chew into attics at weak roof edges. **Skunks** move under decks, porches, and outbuildings. This is the highest-pressure wildlife season of the year around Navarino, and catching an animal before it has a litter inside your attic makes the whole job simpler and cleaner.

Spring also stirs the overwintering pests. Mice that spent the winter in the walls get active and breed, and the [cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/) and [boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/) that overwintered in the wall voids crawl toward the windows on warm days. And as the ground thaws, the marsh begins its long mosquito season, with the first broods emerging from standing water as soon as it warms.

### Summer (June–August): the mosquito season and the bat window

Nothing shapes summer in Navarino like the **mosquitoes**. You live beside thousands of acres of sedge meadow, marsh, and river bottomland — the single best mosquito habitat in the county — and there's no eliminating that. What a seasonal [mosquito](/pest-library/mosquitoes/) program does is realistic and worthwhile: it knocks back the adults resting in your yard's shaded vegetation and treats the standing water you actually control, so the yard is usable for an evening on the deck or a summer gathering. Around the buildings, **wasps and hornets** reach their largest, most defensive size by August, driving most of the season's [wasp and hornet](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) calls.

Summer is also the one stretch when bats **cannot** be sealed out. The Wolf River corridor is excellent bat habitat, and we find plenty of roosts in Navarino attics and outbuildings. But Wisconsin protects its bats, and the DNR requires that exits stay open **June 1 through July 31** while flightless pups are present. If you find a colony in July, the right move is to plan the exclusion for August or later — never to seal the building and trap pups inside. The [bats](/pest-library/bats/) page and the [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/) lay out the timing.

### Fall (September–October): the rodent push and a second wildlife wave

Fall brings two pressures at once. As the nights cool, **mice and deer mice** move off the sandy uplands, field edges, and marsh margins toward warm structures, and any building near the corridor is a target. At the same time the **fall invaders** — cluster flies, boxelder bugs, and [Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/) — mass on sunny south and west walls and work into gaps to overwinter. Cluster flies are common here because their larvae parasitize earthworms in the surrounding soil, so it's the habitat, not the housekeeping, that produces them.

Fall is also a second wildlife peak, as animals that summered in the marsh look for winter shelter and test your buildings. Everything you seal and treat before the freeze — rodent entry points, wall-void gaps, soffit and roof-edge weaknesses — pays off across the entire winter. It's the highest-value time of year to get ahead of the corridor.

### Winter (November–February): what wintered in

By winter the outdoor traffic slows, and what got in during fall is what you're living with. Mice that found a warm nest breed year-round indoors, so a few in November become an infestation by February if the gaps stay open. A thawing January afternoon warms the wall voids enough to rouse the overwintering cluster flies and lady beetles, which turn up again at the sunny windows. Deer mice in a cabin, garage, or seldom-used room are a **hantavirus** concern, so winter cleanup should be done damp and careful. And a raccoon or squirrel that got into an attic in fall will happily overwinter there. Winter work in Navarino is rodent exclusion, fall-invader cleanup, and dealing with any wildlife already denned in.

## Building science on the wetland edge

Living beside the corridor means your structure is under constant test, so the building details matter more here than in a sheltered village lot.

**Older farmhouses and lake-and-river cabins** around Navarino often sit on fieldstone or block foundations with unsealed sill plates, and many older ones are balloon-framed — meaning the wall cavities run straight from the crawlspace to the attic, an open route for a mouse that enters at the sill or a bat that slips behind fascia. Seasonal cabins add a wrinkle: they sit empty and undisturbed for long stretches, which is precisely when rodents move in and settle.

**Soffits, fascia, and roof edges** are the wildlife-entry front line here. In a high-traffic corridor, a lifted soffit return or a gap at a fascia board is an open invitation to bats and squirrels, and an uncapped chimney is a raccoon den waiting to happen. **Decks, porches, and outbuildings** with open skirting are where skunks and raccoons den underneath. And **crawlspaces and damp basements** near the water table invite carpenter ants and springtails, and give rodents a cool, quiet harborage.

We inspect all of it and treat the structure as the actual solution — because on a wetland edge, no amount of spraying substitutes for closing the building.

## Ecology: why the corridor drives everything

The whole point of Navarino is the habitat, and every habitat type around you drives a specific pest. The **marsh and river bottomlands** are a mosquito engine and hold the standing water broods need. The **sedge meadow and grassy edges** are where blacklegged [ticks](/pest-library/ticks/) wait, sustained by the corridor's abundant deer and small mammals — which makes Lyme disease a real yard-level concern here. The **sandy uplands and field edges** are a rodent reservoir that pushes toward buildings each fall and a soil source for cluster flies. And the **woods and wetland cover** move [raccoons](/pest-library/raccoons/), [squirrels](/pest-library/squirrels/), [skunks](/pest-library/skunks/), and bats across the landscape and right up to your walls.

## Mosquitoes on a wetland edge: setting honest expectations

Mosquitoes are the pest people ask about most in Navarino, and it's worth being straight about what control can and can't do here. You live beside thousands of acres of protected wetland, and nobody is treating that — it's a state wildlife area, and the marsh is the point. So any promise to "eliminate" mosquitoes on a property this close to the corridor is a promise no honest company can keep.

What a seasonal program does is different and genuinely useful. Adult mosquitoes don't spend their day out over the open marsh; they rest in the cool, shaded, humid vegetation close to your house — under deck skirting, in dense shrubs, in the tall grass at the yard's edge, along the north-facing foundation. Treating those resting sites knocks the local adult population down hard for a stretch at a time, which is the difference between a deck you can't use after six o'clock and one you can. On top of that, we look for the **standing water you actually control** — clogged gutters, tarps, buckets, old tires, the saucers under flower pots, low spots that hold water for more than a few days — because every one of those is a breeding site that adds to what the marsh already produces. Draining and correcting those is free control that lasts. The [mosquitoes](/pest-library/mosquitoes/) page goes deeper on the biology.

The same honesty applies to timing: mosquito pressure tracks rainfall and heat, so a wet July is worse than a dry one, and a program is about steady management across the season, not a one-and-done. On a wetland edge, that's the realistic and worthwhile goal.

## Structural pests in river-bottom dampness

Building near the Wolf River and its bottomlands means living with a high water table, and that dampness drives a set of structural pests that drier ground doesn't. [Carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) are the big one: they don't eat wood, they excavate moisture-softened wood to nest in, and a crawlspace or sill plate that stays damp from a high water table gives them exactly what they want. Spring swarmers at the windows, or big black ants indoors in winter, mean a nest is living inside the heated structure — a job for finding and treating the nest and correcting the moisture, not spraying the trail.

The same damp foundations bring **springtails, sowbugs, and the occasional nuisance [ant](/pest-library/ants/) trail** up out of the crawlspace, and they make a basement more attractive to overwintering rodents looking for a stable, humid harborage. Near the marsh, the honest fix usually starts with water: grading, gutters, a crawlspace vapor barrier, and drying out the spaces that pests are exploiting. Treat the conditions and the treatments hold; chase the bugs while the crawlspace stays wet and they come right back.
