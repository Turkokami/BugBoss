## The Pella pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): snowmelt, flooding, and the first movers

Spring in Pella starts with water. Snowmelt and spring rain raise the **Embarrass**, and when the river spreads into its bottomland it pushes rodents and other ground-dwelling pests off the low ground and toward buildings sitting on higher land. That's a genuine seasonal driver here — a late-winter or early-spring **exclusion visit** that seals the farmhouse before the push is one of the smartest moves a riverside property owner can make. As the water recedes, standing pools breed the first mosquitoes.

Spring is also when the fall invaders finish their winter indoors. **[Boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/)** and **cluster flies** that overwintered in wall voids wake on warm, sunny days and crawl toward the light at windows — not a new invasion, just last fall's population on its way out. **[Carpenter ant](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/)** swarmers appear at windows in April and May; in a river-bottom home, that usually points to a nest in moisture-softened wood. And by late spring the first **wasp** queens are scouting eaves, while **ticks** turn active along the river vegetation and woodlot edges.

### Summer (June–August): mosquitoes, stinging insects, and the bat window

Summer brings the river's moisture to a head. The Embarrass's slow stretches, floodplain pools, and the low wet spots across the township are prime **[mosquito](/pest-library/mosquitoes/)** habitat, and low-lying yards feel it most. A seasonal mosquito program that treats resting vegetation and reduces standing-water breeding sites makes summer usable again on river-bottom property.

Summer is stinging-insect season too. Paper wasps build under eaves and in outbuildings, and yellowjackets set up in the ground and in wall voids, growing large and defensive by August — peak season for [wasp and hornet](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) stings and calls. And summer is the one stretch when we legally **cannot** seal bats out: the DNR maternity window runs June 1 through July 31, with flightless pups in the roost. Find bats in a Pella attic in July, and the move is to plan the fall exclusion, not to trap the colony inside.

### Fall (September–October): boxelder bugs own the season

Fall is the busiest and most distinctive season in Pella, and boxelder bugs are the reason. Boxelder bugs feed and breed on **boxelder maples** and related trees, which grow thick along the Embarrass and the other waterways cutting through the township. Homes near those riverbank trees get genuinely heavy populations — black-and-orange bugs blanketing the warm south and west walls on sunny afternoons in September and October, then working into every gap to overwinter in wall voids and attics. Alongside them come **[cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/)** and **[Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/)**, and at the same time **[mice](/pest-library/mice/)** and deer mice push in from the harvested fields and river bottom.

The mistake people make is fighting boxelder bugs indoors in November with a can of spray. It never works, because the population is outside on the walls and in the wall voids. The real control is removing or cutting back nearby female (seed-bearing) boxelder trees where practical, and — the part that actually moves the needle — an **early-fall exterior treatment** of the sunlit walls in September, paired with sealing the soffit, fascia, and foundation gaps the bugs use. Our [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/) covers the same fall-invader timing that applies to boxelder bugs.

### Winter (November–February): living with what got in

By winter the invasion is done and you're living with whatever established during fall. The boxelder bugs and cluster flies packed into the wall voids stir on a warm January day and gather at the sunniest windows. Mice that found a warm, food-rich nest breed year-round indoors, so a few in November become a real infestation by February if the entry points aren't sealed. Winter along the Embarrass is rodent-cleanup and exclusion season, the quiet stretch to seal the gaps before the river rises and the next fall wave arrives.

## Housing and building stock in the river bottom

Homes around Pella skew rural and older, and the river amplifies their weak points. The **older farmhouses** carry fieldstone or block foundations, balloon-framed walls that run open from basement to attic, and decades of settlement gaps — the quarter-inch openings mice need and the soffit and fascia gaps that boxelder bugs, cluster flies, and bats exploit. Then there's **moisture**, which the river bottom guarantees. Damp basements and crawlspaces, humid sill plates, and wood near or in contact with wet soil draw carpenter ants, springtails, and decay. Homes on **low ground** near the Embarrass also carry a flood dimension — foundations that take on water in spring, crawlspaces that stay wet, and rodents pushed toward the building when the river rises. Getting ahead of that means sealing entry points and controlling crawlspace moisture before spring, not after. ## Boxelder bugs: knowing the enemy along the Embarrass

Because boxelder bugs define Pella's pest year, it's worth understanding how they actually live. They overwinter as adults, tucked into wall voids, attics, and any sheltered crevice they can reach. In spring they emerge and move to their host trees — **boxelder maples**, and to a lesser degree other maples and ash — where they feed on the developing seeds and lay eggs. The nymphs, bright red and wingless at first, feed through summer and mature into the familiar black-and-orange adults by late summer. Then, as the first cool nights arrive, they leave the trees and go looking for a warm place to spend winter, and a sun-warmed south or west wall reads to them as exactly that.

Two things follow from that biology. First, the seed-bearing (female) boxelder trees along the river are the engine, so removing or cutting back the ones close to the house genuinely reduces pressure — though along the Embarrass there are usually more than any one property can clear. Second, and more important, the population is outside on the walls and in the wall voids, never really in your living space by choice, which is why indoor spraying is pointless. They don't bite, sting, or breed indoors, but in numbers they stain surfaces with their excrement and simply overwhelm a house. The control that works is timing: hit the exterior walls in early fall before they enter, and seal the gaps they use.

## Reading the signs on a river-bottom property

Catching problems early is half the battle. **Boxelder bugs** and **[cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/)** tip their hand by massing on warm exterior walls in fall and by reappearing at sunny interior windows on warm winter days — both signs the wall voids are occupied. **Rodents** leave tapered droppings along walls, greasy rub marks, gnawed corners, and nighttime rustling in the ceilings, with pressure spiking whenever the river rises and pushes them off low ground. **[Carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/)** drop coarse frass beneath the wood they hollow out, and large black ants indoors in late winter signal an interior nest in damp framing. **Bats** leave dark guano and staining below a gable vent or fascia gap. Spotting any of these early turns a big job into a small one.

## The health and structural stakes

Pella's pests carry more than nuisance value. The floodplain **[mosquitoes](/pest-library/mosquitoes/)** that thrive as spring water recedes can transmit West Nile virus in Wisconsin. The brushy edges hold deer **[ticks](/pest-library/ticks/)** and the Lyme disease they carry. **Deer mice** pushed toward the house by rising water are the region's hantavirus carrier, so dusty droppings should never be dry-swept. And the quieter threat is structural: **carpenter ants** and chronic moisture in a river-bottom foundation slowly degrade framing, and a bat colony's accumulated guano damages insulation and drywall over time. Handling these promptly protects both health and the building itself.
