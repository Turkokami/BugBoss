## The Home of the Hamburger, in the middle of dairy country

Seymour is a small Outagamie County city of about **3,546 people** sitting squarely in the farm country between Appleton and Green Bay, at roughly **784 feet** of elevation. It grew up where **Black Creek flows into the Shioc River** — the settlement took root at the mouth of the Shioc — was founded in 1868, and **incorporated in 1879**. But the thing Seymour is known for statewide isn't its rivers; it's a hamburger. In 1885, at the Seymour Fair, a teenager named **Charlie Nagreen** flattened a meatball, put it between two slices of bread so people could walk the fairgrounds while they ate, and — by the town's account — served the world's first hamburger. Seymour has called itself the **Home of the Hamburger** ever since, cooked a 5,520-pound burger in 1989, and throws **Burger Fest** every year in the first week of August.

That heritage isn't just trivia — it tells you what kind of town this is. Seymour is a **food-and-farm town**. It has restaurants, a fair, a summer festival that draws crowds and grills by the dozen, and it sits in the middle of working dairy and crop country, with the Seymour Community School District spreading across 175 square miles and a dozen municipalities. Where there's food and livestock, there are flies and rodents; where there are older homes and open fields, there are cluster flies and mice.

## The Seymour pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): thaw, swarmers, and fieldwork

When the ground warms in Seymour, the **[cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/)**, **[boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/)**, and **[Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/)** that overwintered in the walls wake up and crawl toward the windows on warm days — not a new invasion, but the same fall population resurfacing. Spring is also when **[mice](/pest-library/mice/)** that wintered indoors are breeding and most active, and when farms gear up: as fieldwork and calving begin, rodent and fly pressure around barns and feed climbs.

Spring is **[carpenter ant](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/)** season, and Seymour's older homes and farm buildings give them what they want — slow roof and plumbing leaks, damp sills, decks and posts in contact with soil. Winged swarmers appear near windows in April and May; seeing large black ants indoors while it's still cold out points to a nest living inside the heated structure. By late spring the first **[wasp](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/)** queens are scouting eaves, and **[ticks](/pest-library/ticks/)** are active along the fencerows, woodlots, and pasture edges.

### Summer (June–August): flies, stinging insects, and Burger Fest season

Summer is the busiest food-and-fly stretch of the year in Seymour, and it peaks right at Burger Fest in early August. Crowds, grills, food vendors, and garbage draw filth flies and stinging insects, and a food operation that isn't ahead of the problem finds out fast. This is prime time for **[commercial](/commercial-pest-control/)** fly and rodent management at restaurants and food sites. Out on the farms, barn and stable flies are at their worst, breeding in manure and wet bedding, and fly pressure around a dairy in July and August is a herd-health and comfort issue, not just a nuisance.

**[Mosquitoes](/pest-library/mosquitoes/)** breed in the Shioc and Black Creek corridors and the low, damp spots around town, and **[wasps](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/)** — paper wasps under eaves, yellow jackets in the ground and in wall voids — reach their largest and most defensive size by August. Summer is also the one stretch when bats cannot legally be sealed out: the DNR requires exclusion exits stay open **June 1 through July 31** during the maternity season, when flightless pups are in the roost. If you find bats in a Seymour attic or barn in July, plan the exclusion for after the window. See the [bats](/pest-library/bats/) page and the [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/).

### Fall (September–October): the invasion from the fields

Fall is the defining season for Seymour pest control, and in farm country it's dramatic. As nights cool, the **fall invaders** — cluster flies, boxelder bugs, Asian lady beetles — mass on warm south and west walls and push into every gap toward the wall voids and attic. And as the fields are harvested, **field mice and deer mice** pour out of the stubble and fencerows toward the nearest warm building, which means both farmhouses and barns.

Cluster flies frustrate farm families in particular, because a spotless farmhouse gets them just as badly as a neglected one. The reason is biology: cluster fly larvae **parasitize earthworms in the soil**, so a house ringed by field and pasture produces its own local population regardless of housekeeping. The fix is an **early-fall exterior treatment** of the walls they gather on plus sealing the entry points — done in September, before they get in. We lay out the whole approach in the [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/), and the rodent side in the [farmhouse rodent exclusion guide](/guides/farmhouse-rodent-exclusion/).

### Winter (November–February): what's already inside

By winter the outdoor invasion is over and what you have indoors is what got in during fall. Mice that found a warm nest breed year-round once inside — a handful in November becomes an infestation by February if the entry points aren't closed, and on a farm the reservoir outside never runs dry. Let the sun break through on a mild January afternoon and the cluster flies that overwintered in a farmhouse wall stir and drift toward the upstairs glass. Winter work in Seymour is mostly rodent exclusion, cleaning up the fall invaders, and keeping farm buildings from becoming rodent cities over the cold months.

## Farmhouses, village homes, and farm buildings: where they let pests in

Seymour's building stock runs from tidy village homes to century-old farmhouses to big open ag buildings, and each has its own weaknesses.

The **older farmhouses** are the classic challenge: fieldstone or block foundations, decades of settlement gaps, and in the oldest of them balloon framing that turns the wall cavities into open highways from basement to attic. That's how a mouse entering at the sill plate ends up in the attic, and how cluster flies entering at a soffit gap end up in an upstairs bedroom. On a Seymour farmstead, an hour spent walking one of these old houses with an exclusion eye is worth more than any amount of spraying.

The **village homes** in town are tighter but still have the usual weak points — garage-door corners, utility and dryer-vent penetrations, gaps where siding meets foundation, and soffit and fascia junctions that let in fall invaders, bats, and squirrels.

The **farm buildings** — dairy barns, milkhouses, machine sheds, corn cribs, and pole buildings — are a category of their own. They're never going to be sealed like a house, so the strategy is different: control the food and harborage, exclude what can be excluded, and manage rodents and flies at the source. ## Spiders, ants, and the everyday pests

Between the seasonal invasions, Seymour homes and buildings collect the steady background pests. **[Spiders](/pest-library/spiders/)** are common wherever there's an insect food supply — around barn and porch lights, in basements and machine sheds, in the corners of any building that stays quiet — and heavy spider activity is really a sign of the insects they're eating. Treating the perimeter and cutting off that food does more than sweeping webs. **[Ants](/pest-library/ants/)**, beyond the wood-damaging carpenter ants, show up as the small nuisance trails that follow moisture and food scraps across kitchens and along foundations, especially in spring and after rain. Both come in through the same gaps as the seasonal pests, so we treat them as one perimeter problem rather than a series of separate calls.

## Reading the signs: when to call in Seymour

Knowing what you're looking at saves time and money. **Fresh sawdust-like shavings** (frass) under a windowsill or beam, and large black ants indoors in winter, point to a carpenter-ant nest inside the structure. **Droppings the size of rice grains** along walls, gnaw marks on packaging, and a musky smell in a pantry or feed room mean rodents are established, not just passing through. **Flies clustering at upstairs windows on a warm winter day** are overwintering cluster flies, not a plumbing or drain problem. **A stain and a faint ammonia smell on an attic ceiling**, plus small droppings below a gable vent, suggest a bat roost. And **greasy rub marks along a barn sill or a beam** trace the runways rodents use night after night.

The pattern across all of them is the same: what you see indoors is usually a symptom of a source you can't see, and the longer it runs the more it costs. A carpenter-ant colony works through structural wood; a rodent population doubles; a bat colony grows and coats an attic in guano. Catching it early — and treating the source rather than the symptom — is the whole game. When you spot any of these on a Seymour home, farm, or business, that's the point to call.
