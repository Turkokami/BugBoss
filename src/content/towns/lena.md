## A village built on dairy and farm country

Lena is a small Oconto County village — about **537 people** as of 2020, sitting at roughly **699 feet** of elevation — in the heart of the county's dairy and farm country. It grew up along **Jones Creek**, where French-Canadian and French-Swiss settlers did small-scale logging after Étienne Clement Roserens and his wife homesteaded here in October **1872**. The place was first called **Maple Valley**, then renamed in 1879 for Helena "Lena" Louisa McCartney, and it incorporated as a village in **1921**. The railroad set the location, the timber came and went, and what stayed — what still defines the country on every side — is **dairy farming**: rolling fields, pole barns, milk houses, grain storage, and the older farmhouses that anchor them.

That agricultural setting is the whole story of pest control in Lena. This isn't lakeshore or downtown work; the pressure comes off the **land and the farm buildings**. Mice and rats work the feed and the barns year-round. Cluster flies come off the field soil every fall. Carpenter ants find the damp wood in aging farmhouses. Wasps and hornets nest in sheds and eaves. And wildlife — bats, raccoons, squirrels — treats barns and farmhouse attics as prime real estate. ## The Lena pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): thaw, swarmers, and the first movement

When the snow melts and the ground warms, the first thing we see in Lena is movement across every building on a farmstead at once. Overwintered mice start breeding and ranging through farmhouse, barn, and shed, and the **overwintering insects** — the [cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/) and [boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/) that came in last fall — wake on warm days and crawl to the windows. People read it as a new invasion; it's the same population coming out of dormancy, and it means the building was never sealed.

Spring is **carpenter ant season**, and Lena's older farmhouses give the ants what they want: decades of small roof and plumbing leaks, porches and sills on damp ground, and woodpiles against the house. In April and May, winged [carpenter ant](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) swarmers appear at windows and big black ants show up indoors — and finding them while it's still cold usually means the nest is inside the heated wall, not foraging in from a stump. On the farm side, spring is when the winter's rodent activity becomes visible and the fly season begins to build in the barns. By late spring, **wasp** queens scout eaves, soffits, and outbuildings, and **ticks** turn active where mowed ground meets brush and fencerow.

### Summer (June–August): barn flies, stinging insects, and the bat window

Summer on a Lena farm is fly season. House flies and stable flies breed wherever manure, bedding, and calf areas sit, and left alone they become both a nuisance and a herd-health concern — which is why we manage flies as part of a farm program built around sanitation and targeted treatment rather than a single spray. Jones Creek and the low, damp ground also keep **mosquito** pressure up around dooryards, worst at dusk.

Stinging insects peak now. Paper wasps tuck nests under overhangs and shed eaves, yellowjackets set up in the ground and in barn and wall voids, and bald-faced hornets build their gray footballs in yard trees. Around a farm the nests often go unnoticed until someone opens a shed door or runs a mower near a ground nest, and by August the colonies are at their largest and most defensive, which is when most of our [wasp and hornet](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) calls come in.

Summer is the one window when we **cannot** seal bats out. Wisconsin protects its bats, and the DNR requires exits stay open **June 1 through July 31** during the maternity season, when flightless pups are in the roost. Barns and farmhouse attics are classic bat roosts, and sealing during the maternity window traps the pups inside — illegal, and a far worse mess. Bats in July mean scheduling the exclusion for the legal window. See the [bats](/pest-library/bats/) page and the [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/).

### Fall (September–October): harvest, invaders, and the rodent push

Fall is the defining season for Lena pest control, and on a dairy farm it's the highest-pressure time of the year. As the nights cool, the **fall invaders** — cluster flies, boxelder bugs, and Asian lady beetles — mass on warm south and west walls and work into every gap toward the voids and attics, and **mice and rats** begin a hard push toward feed and buildings as the fields go cold and harvest concentrates the food supply.

Cluster flies are near-universal here because their larvae parasitize **earthworms** in the field soil — so a farmhouse surrounded by pasture and cropland grows a heavy population no matter how clean it's kept. The fix is an **early-fall exterior treatment** of the walls they gather on, plus sealing gaps at soffits, windows, and penetrations; the [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/) has the timing. But the bigger fall job in Lena is rodents. Harvest, cooling weather, and stored feed line up to drive mice and rats into every building at once, which is why we push [farmhouse rodent exclusion](/guides/farmhouse-rodent-exclusion/) hard before the snow — sealing the farmhouse and prioritizing the outbuildings that matter most.

### Winter (November–February): the payoff and the pests already inside

By winter the outdoor invasion is over, but a farm never really goes quiet on the rodent front. Mice breed year-round once warm and fed, and a barn or feed room offers exactly that, so an unaddressed fall population multiplies straight through the winter — chewing wiring, fouling feed, and spreading into the farmhouse. In the house itself, a warm January day heats the wall voids and sends the fall cluster flies back to the windows. Winter work in Lena is mostly rodent exclusion and management across the farmstead, cleanup of the fall invaders, and planning the spring bat and wildlife exclusions whose timing is fixed months ahead.

## Lena housing and farm building stock

Pests exploit specific weaknesses, and Lena's building stock is mostly agricultural.

The **older farmhouses** tend toward fieldstone or block foundations, balloon framing, and decades of settlement gaps. Balloon-framed walls are open vertical channels from basement to attic, which is how a mouse at the sill plate ends up in the attic and how cluster flies at a soffit gap end up in an upstairs bedroom. The **modest village homes** in Lena proper share many of the same weak points — garage-door corners, utility and dryer-vent penetrations, gaps where siding meets foundation, and soffit and fascia junctions.

But the defining structures here are the **farm outbuildings** — pole barns, milking parlors and milk houses, machine sheds, and grain and feed storage. Each offers food, shelter, and countless entry points, and a farm is only as rodent-tight as its weakest building, so exclusion has to be planned across the whole operation rather than one structure at a time. Our [dairy farm pest control guide](/guides/dairy-farm-pest-control/) lays out that building-by-building approach; the short version is that a farm is managed on an ongoing basis, not sprayed once and forgotten.

## Geography and ecology: fields, fencerows, and creek

Lena's ecology is straightforward farm country, and it drives a specific pest set. The **fields and pastures** are the reservoir for cluster flies and rodents that reload every fall — cluster flies off the soil, house mice and [deer mice](/pest-library/deer-mice/) out of the harvested fields and fencerows. Deer mice matter beyond nuisance, since they're the rodent most associated with hantavirus in rural Wisconsin, which is why granary and outbuilding cleanup gets done carefully rather than by kicking up dust. **Jones Creek** and the low ground bring moisture and mosquitoes and give carpenter ants the damp conditions they favor. And the **brushy fencerows and woodlot edges** are where the [ticks](/pest-library/ticks/) that carry Lyme live — right where mowed ground meets tall grass, which on a farm is a lot of edge.

## Spiders, ground nests, and the rest of the Lena picture

Beyond the headline pests, a dairy village produces its own supporting cast. **Spiders** collect wherever a yard light or barn light burns overnight, pulling in the flying insects they feed on — heavy webbing on a porch or milk house is a symptom of that insect supply, not a spider problem in itself. **Ground-nesting yellowjackets** are a genuine hazard on a working farm, where mowing, haying, and equipment work turn up hidden nests every summer; the fix is treating the nest entrance, not chasing foragers. Ordinary **ants** trail toward moisture and spilled feed.
