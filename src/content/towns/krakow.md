## A parish community in the middle of dairy country

Krakow is a small **unincorporated community** of around **380 people** in Shawano County, sitting at about **778 feet** of elevation on **State Highway 32**, north of Pulaski and spread across the towns of **Chase and Angelica**. It grew up the way a lot of northeastern Wisconsin's Polish farm settlements did — around the church. **St. Casimir parish** has anchored the community since **1898**, with the first Catholic church built in **1903**, and that Polish-Catholic heritage still runs through the place. In its busier days Krakow supported a grocery, a **cheese factory**, a **pickle canning company**, taverns, a parochial school, and a public school. Most of those businesses are gone now, but the pattern they left behind — a tight cluster of old buildings surrounded by working dairy and crop ground — is exactly what shapes the pest picture today.

That agricultural setting is the single most important fact about pest control in Krakow, and it's why **BugBoss The X-Terminator** works this community the way we do. The pests here don't come from dense housing; they come off the land. Cluster flies breed in the field soil. Mice and deer mice winter in the fencerows and move indoors when the crops come off. Carpenter ants nest in the mature yard trees and the damp corners of century-old farmhouses. Bats roost in the barns that have stood since the parish's early decades. Solving a pest problem in Krakow starts with recognizing that the source is almost always the surrounding agriculture — and treating accordingly.

We're based down the road in Clintonville at **600 S. Main St.**, an easy run over toward Pulaski, so Krakow is squarely in our rural territory. And because we do **both pest control and wildlife removal**, a Krakow family fighting cluster flies in the house and bats in the barn loft doesn't have to line up two different companies — a real advantage in a farm community where the nearest pest-only franchise is dispatching from Green Bay or the Fox Valley.

## The Krakow pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): thaw, swarmers, and the first stirrings

When the ground thaws around Krakow, the **overwintering insects** that spent the cold in wall voids and attics — cluster flies, boxelder bugs, the odd lady beetle — start moving toward the light and turning up at windows. It looks like a new invasion but it's the fall population finally waking, and it maps out exactly where the building leaks.

Spring is also **carpenter ant** season, and Krakow's old parish-era farmhouses are prime habitat: aged wood, generations of minor roof and gutter leaks, porches and additions with wood near damp soil, and big yard trees hosting the outdoor parent colony. April and May bring large black ants indoors and, in mature colonies, winged **swarmers** at the windows. Swarmers inside while it's still cold out mean the nest is living in the heated structure, not just foraging in — and that's when catching it early saves real wood-repair money. See our [carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) page. By late spring, **wasp** queens are scouting eaves and shed rafters, and **ticks** turn active along the fencerow and woodlot edges that border nearly every rural lot here.

### Summer (June–August): stinging insects, farm flies, and the bat window

Summer around Krakow is stinging-insect and fly season, both amplified by the farm setting. Paper wasps and yellow jackets peak in size and aggression in August — the source of most stings and most of our [wasp and hornet](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) calls. **Stable and house flies** breed in manure, spilled feed, and wet organic matter around barns and feedlots, so a working dairy carries far heavier fly pressure than a village lot, which is why our [dairy farm pest control](/guides/dairy-farm-pest-control/) program treats fly management as central.

Summer is also the one stretch we **cannot** seal bats out. Wisconsin protects its bats, and the DNR keeps one-way exits open **June 1 through July 31** during the maternity season, when flightless pups are in the roost. Find a colony in your Krakow barn loft or farmhouse attic in July, and the move is to schedule exclusion for after the window — not seal the building and trap pups inside, which is illegal and a bigger mess. The timing is on the [bats](/pest-library/bats/) page and in our [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/).

### Fall (September–October): the busiest stretch

Fall is Krakow's cluster-fly season, and it's when most homeowners here first call us. As the nights cool and the crops come off, the fields release two waves at once. **Fall invaders** — [cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/), [boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/), and [Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/) — swarm the warm south and west walls and work into every seam toward the wall voids and attic. At the same time, **mice** begin their annual push indoors as the harvested fields turn cold.

Cluster flies deserve a hard look in Krakow because the misunderstanding around them makes people fight the wrong battle. Their larvae **parasitize earthworms** in the soil, so a Krakow farmhouse surrounded by lawn and field generates its own supply no matter how clean it is. Spraying the flies at the January window does nothing lasting. The real control is an **early-fall exterior treatment** of the walls they gather on, plus sealing the soffit, fascia, and utility gaps they use — the method in our [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/). Everything sealed and treated in September pays off across the winter, which is why fall is the highest-value time to call.

### Winter (November–February): what's already in the walls

By deep winter the outdoor invasion is done, and whatever bothers you indoors got in during fall. **Mice** that found a warm, food-rich nest breed year-round — a few in November become an infestation by February if the gaps stay open. A mild, sunny stretch in midwinter warms the wall voids of the old parish-era farmhouses and coaxes the cluster flies back out to the windows. And winter is prime **bed bug** season, moving with holiday travel and visitors. Winter work in Krakow is mostly rodent exclusion, cleanup of the fall invaders, and dealing with anything that came home in a suitcase.

## Old farmhouses and outbuildings: where Krakow pests get in

Krakow's building stock is old, and old buildings leak in predictable ways. The **parish-era farmhouses** commonly have fieldstone or block foundations, balloon framing, and a century of settlement gaps. Balloon-framed walls are open vertical shafts from the sill plate to the attic — the exact route a mouse takes from the foundation to an overhead nest, and the way a cluster fly at a soffit gap ends up in an upstairs room. No building around Krakow rewards a slow, careful exclusion inspection the way these St. Casimir-era farmhouses do.

Then there are the **outbuildings**, which a village exterminator can ignore but a rural one can't. On a Krakow farmstead the machine shed, pole barn, granary, and old dairy barn are usually where rodents and wildlife establish first, then filter toward the house. Treat the house and leave the barn full of mice and the population just refills. The old community buildings — the church, the hall, seldom-heated spaces — have their own issues: bats and birds in towers and attics, cluster flies in unused rooms, rodents in basements and kitchens. We treat and exclude the whole property, which is the only approach that holds in farm country. ## Wildlife in Krakow: barns, attics, and the law

Between the old barns, the church buildings, and the surrounding woods, **wildlife work** is a regular part of what we do around Krakow. **Bats** roost in barn lofts, church attics, and behind farmhouse fascia. **Raccoons** den in chimneys, barns, and torn soffits — especially females raising young in spring. **Squirrels** chew into attics at weak roof edges. **Skunks** den under porches, granaries, and sheds, and their spray reaches a house's air intake quickly.

## Beyond the fall invaders: the rest of the Krakow pest picture

Cluster flies and mice lead the list, but a farm community carries a broader mix, and each pest earns its own approach.

**Deer mice** are distinct from the common house mouse and are a real concern on Krakow farmsteads. They come out of the fields and fencerows, carry **hantavirus** risk, and colonize outbuildings, stored equipment, campers, and vehicles before they ever reach the house. On a working farm the machine shed and granary fill first — one more reason we treat the whole property, not just the living space. More on the [mice](/pest-library/mice/) page.

**Spiders** are a fixture in Krakow's old barns, cellars, and porches, and heavy spider activity almost always means the building is feeding a lot of other insects. Spiders follow their food, so cutting the fly and gnat supply with an exterior program thins the spiders far better than knocking down webs. The [spiders](/pest-library/spiders/) page explains the connection.

**Nuisance ants** — pavement ants, odorous house ants, and the foraging trails at the kitchen counter — are a separate problem from carpenter ants and take a separate treatment. They don't damage wood, but they're stubborn, and the fix is tracing the trail back to the colony, not spraying the counter. See the [ants](/pest-library/ants/) page.

**Ticks** are worth a second mention because rural Krakow properties are ringed by the fencerow and woodlot edges deer ticks favor, and Lyme disease is a live risk for anyone working outdoors or letting the dog into tall grass. A perimeter [tick and Lyme yard program](/guides/tick-lyme-yard-program/) knocks back that edge habitat where the ticks wait for a host.

And the **raccoons, [squirrels](/pest-library/squirrels/), and [skunks](/pest-library/skunks/)** that come with living next to woods and fields aren't pest-spray problems — they're exclusion and trapping problems, and they're where the wildlife side of our work matters most. A raccoon that's torn into a barn is one thing; one that's moved a litter into a farmhouse attic through a soffit gap is a job that has to be done right the first time.
