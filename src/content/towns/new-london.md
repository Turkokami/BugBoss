## Where two rivers meet

New London is a river town in the truest sense — it sits right where the **Embarrass River empties into the Wolf**, and the two of them have shaped the place since before it had a name. About 7,300 people live here, split across the **Waupaca–Outagamie county line** that runs through the city, and the town grew up in the 1850s as a lumber center and the head of steamboat navigation on the Wolf, the point where boats coming up from Oshkosh could go no farther. A founder named Reeder Smith named it after **New London, Connecticut**, his father's home. Two other bits of local color tell you a lot about the town's character: it's the birthplace of the **American Water Spaniel**, the breed developed here that became Wisconsin's state dog, and every March the whole city famously turns itself into **"New Dublin"** for what's billed as Wisconsin's largest St. Patrick's Day parade and Irish Fest.

For pest control, the rivers are the whole story. A confluence means low ground, damp soil, abundant cover, and standing backwater — and every one of those drives a specific set of pests. New London's housing sits close to the water on both rivers, with a good stock of older river-town homes and a band of riverfront and near-river properties that see moisture and wildlife pressure their up-the-hill neighbors don't. Ring the town with the farm country of Waupaca and Outagamie counties and you've got the classic rural-Wisconsin pest reservoir on top of the river dynamics. BugBoss The X-Terminator works this ground every week. We're based just west in Clintonville, we cover both sides of the county line the same way, and we do **both pest control and wildlife removal** — which on riverfront property, where bats and raccoons are as common a call as mice, matters a great deal.

## The New London pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): high water, swarmers, and St. Patrick's Day

Spring on the rivers means high water and softening ground, and both push pests toward the buildings. Rising river levels flood the low cover along the banks and move rodents and other ground dwellers toward higher, drier ground — which often means your foundation. Mice that wintered indoors start breeding and ranging, and the **overwintering cluster flies and boxelder bugs** wake on warm days and crawl toward the windows. That's the fall population coming out of dormancy, not a new invasion, and it's a sign the building was never sealed.

Spring is also **carpenter ant season**, and New London's riverfront and older homes are especially prone because they hold moisture. Carpenter ants nest in **moisture-damaged wood** — around a leaking roof, a damp sill plate near the water table, or a deck sitting on wet soil. In April and May, winged swarmers appear at windows and big black ants show up indoors; seeing them early, before the ground fully warms, usually means the nest is inside the heated structure. Our [carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) page covers what to look for. And when the town becomes **New Dublin** in mid-March, the parade crowds and the food service that feeds them create a short, intense burst of crowd-driven pest pressure on downtown venues — worth getting ahead of, not scrambling to catch up on.

### Summer (June–August): river mosquitoes, stinging insects, and the bat window

Summer is mosquito season, and New London gets it worse than most towns we serve, for a simple reason: two rivers meeting means twice the slow backwater and wet ground that mosquitoes breed in. Riverfront yards feel it worst in the evenings. A seasonal [mosquito](/pest-library/mosquitoes/) program that treats the shaded vegetation where they rest by day and knocks back the standing water they breed in is what makes a riverfront deck or backyard usable through the summer.

Stinging insects peak in this stretch. Paper wasps nest under eaves and porch roofs, yellowjackets set up in the ground and in wall voids, and bald-faced hornets build their gray aerial nests in the mature trees along the rivers. By August the colonies are largest and most defensive, and that's when most of our [wasp and hornet](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) calls come in.

Summer is also the one window when we **cannot** seal bats out. Wisconsin protects its bats, and the DNR requires that exits stay open **June 1 through July 31** during the maternity season, when flightless pups are in the roost. Seal a New London attic then and you trap the pups inside — illegal, and a worse mess than the colony. If you find bats in summer, we schedule the exclusion for the legal window. The details are on our [bats](/pest-library/bats/) page and in the [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/).

### Fall (September–October): the busiest season in town

Fall is the defining season for New London pest control. As the nights cool, the **fall invaders** — [cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/), [boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/), and [Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/) — mass on the warm south and west walls and work into every gap, heading for the wall voids and attics. At the same time, **mice** begin their annual push indoors as the surrounding fields and fencerows go cold.

Cluster flies are worth calling out in a farm-ringed town like New London, because people blame their houses when they shouldn't. Cluster fly larvae parasitize **earthworms** in the soil, so any property surrounded by lawn, field, or riverbank — which is nearly all of New London — grows its own population regardless of how clean the home is. The fix is an **early-fall exterior treatment** of the walls they gather on, paired with sealing the gaps at soffits, windows, and penetrations. Our [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/) lays out the timing. For homes near the fields, our [farmhouse rodent exclusion guide](/guides/farmhouse-rodent-exclusion/) covers the mouse side of the fall push.

### Winter (November–February): the payoff and the pests already inside

By winter the outdoor invasion is over, and whatever got in during fall now has the building to itself. Mice breed year-round once warm and fed, so a handful in November becomes a real infestation by February if the entry points aren't closed. A thaw in January warms the wall voids of a river-town home and coaxes the overwintering cluster flies back to the windows. And winter is **travel-pest season** — holiday travel brings [bed bugs](/pest-library/bed-bugs/) home as hitchhikers, a problem tied to luggage and used furniture, not to cleanliness. Winter work in New London is mostly rodent exclusion, cleanup of the fall invaders, and anything that arrived in a suitcase.

## New London's housing stock: riverfront, downtown, and rural

Pests exploit specific weaknesses, and New London's building stock gives them plenty to work with.

The **riverfront and near-river homes** on both the Wolf and the Embarrass are the type that needs the most attention. Proximity to water means a high water table, damp crawlspaces and basements, and sill plates that stay wet — exactly the conditions carpenter ants, springtails, and other dampness pests want. Many of these homes are older, with stone or block foundations and the settlement gaps that come with a building on soft river-bottom soil, and the natural cover along the banks hands rodents and wildlife a covered route right to the foundation. Riverfront property rewards a careful moisture-and-exclusion approach more than almost any other kind.

The **older downtown and near-downtown homes** dating to the lumber era tend toward stone or rubble foundations, balloon framing, and decades of small gaps. Balloon-framed walls are open vertical channels from basement to attic — the route a mouse entering at the sill plate uses to reach the top floor, and the route cluster flies use to move from a soffit gap into a bedroom. And the **rural farmsteads** ringing town on both sides of the county line bring the full agricultural picture: pole barns, outbuildings, and older farmhouses with the rodent pressure of living next to open field.

The wooded riverbanks are prime **wildlife** country and prime **tick** habitat — ticks thrive where mowed lawn meets the brush and cover along the banks, exactly the edge that defines a riverfront lot. ## Spiders, ants, and the rest of the New London picture

Beyond the mice, cluster flies, and wildlife, a river town grows its own supporting cast. **Spiders** are a steady complaint on riverfront and porch-heavy homes, because the same damp, insect-rich air along the water that feeds mosquitoes also feeds spiders — an exterior light near the river becomes an all-night buffet, and the webs follow. Knocking back the insect supply and sealing the gaps does more for a spider problem than chasing webs with a broom. Our [spiders](/pest-library/spiders/) page covers the common species.

Ordinary **ants** — the small trailing kind, not carpenter ants — track in on moisture and food, and a kitchen near a damp river foundation is a reliable draw; our [ants](/pest-library/ants/) page explains why the source matters more than the trail. On the farm edges of town the [mice](/pest-library/mice/) pressure runs heavy every fall, and **skunks** denning under decks and outbuildings are a routine spring call. Each is its own problem with its own fix, which is why a one-size perimeter spray rarely settles anything for long in a town this varied.

## Exclusion is the part that lasts in New London

The durable fix in New London is **exclusion**, not repeated spraying — and on riverfront property it has to start with **moisture**, because a wet foundation is what draws carpenter ants and dampness pests in the first place. We look hard at grading, gutters, crawlspace ventilation, and sill plates that sit close to a high water table, and we correct what we can before we seal. Then the sealing itself gets specific: **utility and pipe penetrations**, where the opening is always larger than the pipe; the **sill-plate gap** where the frame meets a stone or block foundation; **garage-door corners and door sweeps**; and the **soffit and fascia junctions** and weak roof edges that squirrels, bats, and raccoons use to reach the attic. On an older river-town home with balloon framing, closing the entry at the foundation is what stops a mouse from turning up two floors above where it got in. That combination — dry the building out, then close it up — is what makes the fix hold after we've gone.
