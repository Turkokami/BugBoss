## The Wausau pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): thaw, swarmers, and last fall's invaders waking up

Spring in Wausau starts with a lie the weather tells the bugs. The first warm days heat the south- and west-facing wall voids of the city's older homes, and the **cluster flies and boxelder bugs** that slipped in last September wake up and crawl toward the light at the windows. Homeowners think they've got a new infestation; they've actually got the tail end of an old one. There's no outdoor source to spray in March — the fix belongs to the previous fall — so spring is about knocking down what's active indoors and planning the exclusion that stops next year's crop.

As the ground thaws along the river valley, **carpenter ants** turn active. Wausau's mature canopy and older housing stock make it strong carpenter ant country, and April and May are when winged swarmers appear at windows and big black foragers show up on kitchen counters. Indoor swarmers before the trees leaf out almost always mean a nest living inside the structure, usually in wood softened by a leak or chronic dampness. Spring is the season to trace that moisture. Meanwhile **mice** that wintered indoors are breeding, and the first **wasp** queens are scouting sheltered spots under eaves and soffits to start their nests. On lots backing up to Rib Mountain State Park or the wooded river bluffs, **ticks** turn active with the first green-up, and that's the season to get a [tick](/pest-library/ticks/) program on the yard before the deer-tick population peaks.

### Summer (June–August): stinging insects, river mosquitoes, and the bat lockout

Summer is stinging-insect season, and in Wausau's older neighborhoods the nests turn up in exactly the spots the buildings offer: **paper wasps** under porch roofs and eaves, **yellowjackets** in ground burrows and in the wall voids of homes with worn soffit returns, and **bald-faced hornets** in the big trees along the river and the bluffs. Colonies grow all summer and are largest and most defensive by August, which is when most of our stinging-insect calls come in.

The **Wisconsin River**, Lake Wausau, and the low riverfront ground drive the summer mosquito picture. We don't treat the river itself, but the backwaters and any standing water on a property breed mosquitoes that make a riverfront yard miserable at dusk; a yard program aimed at shaded resting vegetation and standing-water sources is what buys back the evenings.

Summer is also the one stretch when we **cannot** seal bats out of a Wausau building. The Wisconsin DNR prohibits restricting bat exits **June 1 through July 31**, the maternity season, when flightless pups are in the roost. Seal a building then and you trap the pups inside to die in the wall — illegal and a far worse mess than the colony. Wausau has plenty of bat habitat: older attics, church and school belfries, and downtown rooftop structures. If you find bats in summer, we inspect and schedule the exclusion for the legal window. The full rules are on our [bats](/pest-library/bats/) page and in the [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/).

### Fall (September–October): the busiest season in the metro

Fall is the defining season for pest control in Wausau, and it's when BugBoss does its heaviest work in the corridor. As nights cool, the **fall invaders** — [cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/), [boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/), and Asian lady beetles — mass on the warm south and west walls of the city's homes and work into every gap they can find, heading for wall voids and attics to overwinter. At the same time, **mice** abandon the cooling yards, alleys, and fields at the city's edge and push indoors looking for the winter harborage an older home readily provides.

Cluster flies deserve a specific note here, because Wausau homeowners so often blame their housekeeping. Cluster flies don't breed indoors — their larvae parasitize **earthworms** in the soil, so any home with a lawn grows its own supply regardless of how clean it's kept. The only thing that works is an **early-fall exterior treatment** of the walls they gather on, timed before they get inside, paired with sealing the soffit, window, and utility gaps they use. Our [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/) lays out the timing in detail. Fall is also the last good stretch of the year to do bat and wildlife exclusion before winter, and the season to seal a home against the coming rodent push. Do that work in September and October and you spend the winter watching, not fighting.

### Winter (November–February): what's already inside has the run of the house

By deep winter the outdoor pressure is over and Wausau's pest picture is entirely about what's already in the building. **Mice** breed year-round once they're warm and fed, so a few that came in during October can be an established colony by March if nothing was sealed — chewing wiring, fouling insulation, and gnawing into stored food. A brief January thaw is enough to warm the wall voids and send the overwintering cluster flies and lady beetles drifting to sunny windows, a mid-winter preview of the spring emergence.

Winter is also when structural pests announce themselves indoors. Big black **carpenter ants** wandering a warm kitchen in January are not coming in from outside — nothing is — which means a nest is active inside heated, moisture-damaged wood, and it's time to find it. And in the metro's older homes, winter is when squirrels and the occasional raccoon, holed up in an attic against the cold, get loud enough overhead that owners finally call. It's a good season for us to inspect and plan, because the entry points an animal is using show clearly against the snow and the frost line.

## Wausau's housing stock: from century homes to river-bluff lots

Wausau's buildings are older and more varied than the newer suburbs to the south, and that variety is exactly why exclusion-focused work matters here. The **near-downtown and east-side neighborhoods** are full of homes from the lumber-era boom and the decades after — balloon-framed houses with stone and block foundations, unsealed sill plates, and settlement gaps that give mice and fall invaders a dozen ways in. Balloon framing is a special problem: it leaves open vertical wall cavities running straight from the foundation to the attic, so a mouse or a cluster fly that enters at the sill can travel the whole height of the house unseen. These homes reward a thorough exclusion far more than a spray.

The **wooded river-bluff and hillside lots**, including properties backing up to Rib Mountain, put homes right against mature hardwoods and brush. That means more carpenter-ant parent nests in the trees, more squirrels and raccoons working the roofline, more bats, and heavier tick pressure on the yard edge. The city's **mid-century neighborhoods** are more conventional but still leak at the usual points — garage-door corners, dryer-vent and utility penetrations, and worn soffit and fascia junctions. And around the metro's rural edges sit the **farmhouses and outbuildings** of Marathon County proper, carrying the same rodent and bat pressure we handle across our home territory. That rural-edge and older-home work is where BugBoss is strongest, and it's the honest core of what we offer Wausau.

## Geography and ecology: the river, the mountain, and the north woods

Two landforms shape pest pressure in Wausau. The **Wisconsin River**, with Lake Wausau upstream and the dam-controlled flowages, runs a corridor of moist bottomland and backwater through the middle of the city — mosquito habitat, dampness that feeds carpenter ants and springtails in riverfront basements, and a green travel corridor that funnels wildlife into town. And **Rib Mountain**, the ancient quartzite monadnock rising some 700 feet above the valley on the city's southwest side and preserved as one of Wisconsin's oldest state parks, presses a large block of hardwood forest right up against the metro's homes. Where that forest edge meets mowed yards, you get the classic conditions for **ticks**, carpenter ants, and roofline wildlife all at once.

Wausau also sits at the transition from the state's farm country into the northern forest — the reason the region became a ginseng and timber center — and that mixed landscape means the metro deals with both the rodent pressure of open agricultural edges and the wildlife and structural-insect pressure of the woods. It's a genuinely different environment from the flat dairy country to the southeast, and it calls for a company comfortable with both.
