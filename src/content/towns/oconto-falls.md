## The city the river runs through

Oconto Falls is a small Oconto County city of about **2,957 people** (2020) that the **Oconto River** splits clean down the middle, separating the east and west sides of town. It was named for waterfalls on the river that no longer exist — the water still drops over a dam and impoundment locals know as Oconto Falls Pond, but the natural falls are long gone. That impoundment and the slow river backwaters behind it hold standing water through the warm months, a straightforward driver of the mosquito pressure on the properties nearby. European settlement started in **1846** when John and Almira Volk arrived and put up a sawmill on the strength of the pine upstream. The village incorporated in **1903** and became a city in **1919**. Its most durable claim to fame is quiet but real: in 1908 Anton Gustin built a pulp mill here that became **the first commercial producer of kraft paper in the United States**. Around that industrial core grew the thing that still defines the countryside — **dairy farming**, spread across the rolling farm-and-woodlot country on every side of town.

That mix — a river running through the middle, an old mill-town housing core, and dairy country pressing in from all directions — is what shapes pest work in Oconto Falls. The **river corridor** brings moisture, carpenter ants, and mosquitoes. The **older village housing** brings the entry points a century of settling leaves behind. And the **dairy farms** bring a rodent and fly picture that has nothing in common with a tidy in-town lot. BugBoss The X-Terminator is set up to cover all three. We're based southwest in Clintonville, we run this corridor of Oconto County deliberately because the national brands treat it as the outer edge of a route, and — the difference that matters up here — we do **both pest control and wildlife removal**, with **no long-term contract**.

## The Oconto Falls pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): thaw, swarmers, and the first movement

When the river ice goes out and the ground warms, the first thing we see is movement. Overwintered mice start breeding and ranging, and the **overwintering insects** — the [cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/) and [boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/) that came in last fall — wake on warm days and crawl to the windows. Homeowners think it's new. It's the same population coming out of dormancy, and it says the building was never sealed.

Spring is **carpenter ant season**, and the river corridor gives the ants what they need: mature hardwoods along the water, older homes carrying decades of small roof and plumbing leaks, and porches and sills on damp, river-adjacent ground. In April and May, winged [carpenter ant](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) swarmers gather at windows and big black ants appear indoors — and seeing them while it's still cold usually means the nest is living inside the heated wall, not foraging in from a stump. On the farms, spring is when barn cats stop keeping up with the thaw's rodent activity and outbuilding populations start to climb. By late spring, **wasp** queens scout eaves and soffits, and **ticks** turn active where mowed ground meets brush and woods.

### Summer (June–August): river mosquitoes, stinging insects, and the bat window

The **Oconto River** and the low, damp ground along it keep **mosquito** pressure up all summer, worst in the evenings when people want to be outside. A seasonal program that treats the shaded resting vegetation and reduces standing-water breeding keeps river-side yards and farm dooryards usable.

Stinging insects peak now. Paper wasps tuck nests under overhangs, yellowjackets set up in the ground and in barn and wall voids, and bald-faced hornets build their gray footballs in yard trees. On a dairy, the fly picture also comes into full force — house flies and stable flies breeding in manure, bedding, and calf areas, a pressure we manage as part of a farm program rather than a one-off spray. By August the wasp and yellowjacket colonies are at their largest, and that's when most of our [wasp and hornet](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) calls come in.

Summer is the one window when we **cannot** seal bats out. Wisconsin protects its bats, and the DNR requires exits stay open **June 1 through July 31** during the maternity season, when flightless pups are in the roost. Seal then and you trap the pups — illegal, and a far worse mess. Bats in the attic in July mean scheduling the exclusion for the legal window, not reaching for caulk. See the [bats](/pest-library/bats/) page and the [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/).

### Fall (September–October): the busiest season in dairy country

Fall is the defining season for Oconto Falls pest control. As the nights cool, the **fall invaders** — cluster flies, boxelder bugs, and Asian lady beetles — mass on warm south and west walls and work into every gap toward the voids and attics to overwinter, and **mice** begin their annual push indoors as the fields and pastures go cold.

Cluster flies matter especially here because Oconto Falls is surrounded by exactly what they need. Their larvae parasitize **earthworms** in the soil, so a dairy farm, a river lot, or a village yard all grow their own populations no matter how clean the house is kept. The fix is an **early-fall exterior treatment** of the walls they gather on, plus sealing the gaps at soffits, windows, and penetrations. Our [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/) lays out the timing. On the farms, fall is also the make-or-break moment for rodents — harvest pushes mice and rats toward feed and buildings all at once, which is the whole reason we push [farmhouse rodent exclusion](/guides/farmhouse-rodent-exclusion/) before the snow flies.

### Winter (November–February): the payoff and the pests already inside

By winter the outdoor invasion is over, and whatever got in during fall has the building to itself. Mice breed year-round once warm and fed, so a few in November becomes a real problem by February if the entries aren't closed — and in a barn or feed room with a steady food supply, that math runs even faster. A warm January day heats the wall voids and sends cluster flies back to the windows. Winter work in Oconto Falls is mostly rodent exclusion, cleanup of the fall invaders, and planning the spring bat and wildlife exclusions whose timing is fixed months in advance.

## Oconto Falls housing and building stock

Pests exploit specific weaknesses, and Oconto Falls offers three distinct sets.

The **older village homes** on both sides of the river date to the mill era and the decades after — fieldstone or block foundations, balloon framing, and a century of small settlement gaps. Balloon-framed walls are open vertical channels from basement to attic, which is how a mouse at the sill plate ends up in the attic and how a cluster fly at a soffit gap ends up in an upstairs bedroom. The **river-adjacent properties** add the moisture problem: damp basements and crawlspaces on ground near the water table, which is precisely what pulls carpenter ants and dampness pests in.

And the **dairy farms and rural properties** ringing the city are a different animal entirely. Pole barns, machine sheds, grain and feed storage, milk houses, and older farmhouses all offer food, shelter, and countless entry points. Rodent pressure never really lets up on a working farm, flies breed wherever manure and bedding sit, and the sheer number of outbuildings means exclusion has to be prioritized building by building. Our [dairy farm pest control guide](/guides/dairy-farm-pest-control/) walks through that approach; the short version is that a farm is managed, not sprayed once and forgotten.

## Geography and ecology: the river and the farm country

The **Oconto River** through the middle of town is the biggest single driver of the local pest picture. The **dairy and farm country** beyond town is the reservoir that reloads every fall. Cluster flies come off the field and pasture soil. Beyond the headline pests, a river-split dairy town produces its own supporting cast. **Spiders** draw complaints wherever an exterior light burns near the river or a barn, because the light pulls in the flying insects spiders feed on — heavy webbing is a symptom of that insect supply, not a spider problem in itself, and it clears when the insects and entry points are handled.
