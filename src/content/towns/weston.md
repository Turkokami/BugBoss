## The Weston pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): swarmers, last fall's invaders, and the new-yard edge

Spring in Weston opens with the overwintering **cluster flies and boxelder bugs** waking in the walls. The first warm days heat the south- and west-facing wall voids and send last fall's population crawling to the windows — not a fresh invasion, but the old one ending. There's no outdoor source to spray yet, so spring work is knocking down what's active inside and planning the fall exclusion that breaks the cycle.

As the ground thaws, **carpenter ants** turn active. Weston's mix of mature trees left standing in subdivisions and its wooded and riverside lots gives carpenter ants their outdoor parent-nest habitat, and winged swarmers appear at windows in April and May. Indoor swarmers before the trees leaf out point to a nest inside moisture-softened wood — around a deck ledger, a leaky window, or a damp sill. Spring is also when **mice** that wintered indoors are breeding and when the first **wasp** queens scout eaves, soffits, and the ledges of Weston's many decks and porches. And on the countless Weston lots that back up to woods, the Eau Claire River corridor, or leftover farm fields, **ticks** turn active with the first green-up — the season to get a [tick](/pest-library/ticks/) program on the yard before the deer-tick population peaks in the edge habitat new subdivisions create.

### Summer (June–August): stinging insects, deck and yard pressure, and the bat window

Summer in Weston is stinging-insect season, and a village full of decks, porches, sheds, and playsets offers no shortage of nest sites. **Paper wasps** build under deck rails and eaves, **yellowjackets** nest in lawn burrows and in wall voids where a soffit gaps, and **bald-faced hornets** hang their gray nests in the mature trees and along the river. Colonies grow all summer and are largest and most defensive by August, when most stinging-insect calls come in.

Where a Weston lot sits near the **Eau Claire River** or on low, wet ground, **mosquitoes** breed in the standing water and make the yard miserable at dusk. We don't treat the river, but a yard program aimed at shaded resting vegetation and standing-water sources on the property is what makes a Weston backyard usable through summer evenings.

Summer is also the one stretch when we **cannot** seal bats out of a Weston home. The Wisconsin DNR prohibits restricting bat exits **June 1 through July 31**, the maternity season, when flightless pups are in the roost. Seal a building then and you trap the pups inside — illegal, and a worse mess than the colony. Even newer Weston homes get bats where a ridge vent, gable end, or soffit return leaves a gap. If you find them in summer, we inspect and schedule the exclusion for the legal window. The rules are on our [bats](/pest-library/bats/) page and in the [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/).

### Fall (September–October): the invasion, and the season we do our heaviest Weston work

Fall is the defining season for pest control in Weston. As the nights cool, the **fall invaders** — [cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/), [boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/), and Asian lady beetles — mass on the warm south and west walls of Weston homes and push into every gap, heading for wall voids and attics to overwinter. At the same time, **mice** leave the cooling yards, fields, and wood edges that ring the village and move indoors, and a village built out across former farmland sits right in their path.

Cluster flies deserve a specific note, because Weston homeowners in tidy new houses so often assume they've done something wrong. Cluster flies don't breed indoors — their larvae parasitize **earthworms** in the soil, so any lawn, new or old, grows its own supply regardless of housekeeping. In fact, subdivisions carved from old fields and pasture sit on ground thick with earthworms, so newer Weston neighborhoods often get hit hard. The only real control is an **early-fall exterior treatment** of the walls they gather on, timed before they get inside, plus sealing the soffit, window, and utility gaps. Our [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/) lays out the timing. Fall is also the last good window for bat and wildlife exclusion before winter and the season to seal a home against the rodent push.

### Winter (November–February): what's inside has the run of the house

By deep winter the outdoor pressure is over, and Weston's pest picture is about what's already indoors. **Mice** breed year-round once warm and fed, so a few that entered in October can be an established colony by spring if nothing was sealed — chewing wiring, fouling insulation, and getting into stored food. A January thaw warms the wall voids enough to send overwintering cluster flies and lady beetles to sunny windows, a mid-winter preview of spring.

Structural pests show themselves indoors, too. Big black **carpenter ants** in a warm Weston kitchen in January aren't coming from outside — they mean an active nest in heated, moisture-damaged wood inside the home, and it's time to find it. Winter is also when squirrels and the occasional raccoon holed up in an attic against the cold get loud enough to prompt a call, and it's a good season for us to inspect: entry points and animal traffic show clearly against snow and frost.

## Weston's housing stock: new subdivisions and rural-edge lots

Weston's building stock is the youngest of the three metro communities, and that changes the pest work in specific ways. The dominant type is **newer subdivision housing** built during the village's rapid growth since 1996 — homes that, contrary to a common assumption, are far from pest-proof. New construction leaks at **construction gaps**: unsealed utility and dryer-vent penetrations, weep holes in brick veneer, garage-door corners that don't seal to the slab, ridge and gable vents, and soffit returns. Mice, cluster flies, and even bats find those gaps as readily as they find the settlement gaps in a century home. The difference is that on a newer home the fix is cleaner and more permanent, because the structure is sound — sealing the specific gaps genuinely ends the problem.

Those lots carry the heaviest pressure in the village: mice moving in from the fields every fall, carpenter ants and squirrels from the tree line, bats and raccoons working the roofline, and ticks on the yard's wild edge. ## The supporting cast: spiders, wasps, and the deck-and-shed pests

A Weston subdivision generates a predictable second tier of pests beyond the fall invaders, mice, and wildlife — most of it tied to the outdoor living a newer village is built around. **Spiders** top the list, and they collect exactly where the structures are: under deck rails, in garage corners, around porch lights, and along the soffits, because outdoor lighting on a subdivision home draws the flying insects spiders eat. Heavy webbing usually means an ample insect supply, not a spider problem in itself, and it thins out when the exterior insects and lighting are managed.

Decks, sheds, playsets, and detached garages — standard equipment on a Weston lot — give **wasps and yellowjackets** endless nest sites, which is why stinging-insect calls run so high here through late summer. The same structures, plus retaining walls and landscaped beds, shelter **ants** of the small trailing kind that follow moisture and crumbs onto a patio or into a kitchen; finding and treating the nest beats chasing the trail every time. And on lots against the field or wood line, **field mice and voles** work the lawn edges and mulch beds before the fall push drives them toward the house. None of it is exotic — it's the ordinary supporting cast of a growing residential village — but it responds to the same exclusion-and-source approach that handles the headline pests, not to a blanket spray.
