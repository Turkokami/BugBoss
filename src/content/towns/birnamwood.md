## High ground, big woods, and a pest picture to match

Birnamwood is a village of about 730 people straddling the Shawano-Marathon county line, and it stands out from the farm villages around it for one simple reason: elevation. At roughly **1,300 feet**, it sits on some of the highest ground in the area, up where the crop and dairy country of central Wisconsin gives way in earnest to the northern forest. The name comes from **Birnam Wood** in Shakespeare's *Macbeth* — a fitting choice for a village this close to the big timber — and **U.S. Route 45** runs right through it. Older locals still remember when Birnamwood was home to the world's largest fiberglass badger, a bit of roadside Americana that stood into the 1990s. What matters for pest control, though, is the land: forest, deer, and hunting country as much as farmland.

That setting shapes everything about the pests here, and it's why BugBoss The X-Terminator treats Birnamwood as its own kind of job rather than a copy of the farm-town work to the south. More woods means heavier [tick](/pest-library/ticks/) pressure and more wildlife pushing into attics and outbuildings. More hunting land means cabins and deer camps that sit empty most of the year — prime winter quarters for mice and cluster flies. And the mix of older farmhouses, cabins, and village homes gives pests plenty of gaps to exploit. We're based in Clintonville, a manageable drive south, close enough to handle a Birnamwood call as a normal part of our route rather than a franchise dispatch from Wausau or Green Bay.

## The Birnamwood pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): thaw, swarmers, and the cabin open-up

Spring on this high ground comes late and comes wet. As the snow finally melts and the ground warms, overwintering insects wake and rodents start moving. Homeowners find [cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/) and [boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/) at the windows again — not a new invasion, but the same population that spent the winter in the wall voids, now crawling toward the light. Cabin and deer-camp owners open up their places and discover what wintered inside: mouse-fouled cabinets, dead flies on the sills, sometimes a [bat](/pest-library/bats/) behind a shutter.

Spring is also **carpenter ant** season, and the wooded country around Birnamwood is ideal for them. All that mature timber gives [carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) their outdoor parent nests, and older homes and cabins with roof and moisture problems give them the damp wood to excavate indoors. Winged swarmers show up at windows in April and May; large black ants indoors while it's still cold means the nest is inside the heated structure. By late spring the first [wasp](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) queens are scouting nest sites, and ticks come alive along the tree lines — a bigger concern here than almost anywhere in our service area, given the forest and the deer.

### Summer (June–August): ticks, stinging insects, and the bat window

Summer is when the woods make themselves felt. Tick pressure stays high through the season on properties bordered by forest, and the deer that move through Birnamwood's edges keep the deer-tick population fed. Stinging insects hit their stride too: paper wasps under overhangs, yellowjackets in the ground and wall voids, and bald-faced hornets hanging their gray nests in yard and woodlot trees. By August those colonies are at their largest and most defensive, which is when the stings and the calls come.

Summer is also the one time of year we legally **cannot** seal bats out. Wisconsin protects its bats, and the DNR requires exits stay open **June 1 through July 31** during the maternity season, when flightless pups are in the roost. Birnamwood's older farmhouses, barns, and cabins — with their open gables and worn soffits — are classic roosts, so if you find bats in July, the move is to plan the exclusion for August, not to seal the building and trap the pups. We lay out the timing in our [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/).

### Fall (September–October): the busiest season, and it overlaps deer season

Fall is the defining pest season in Birnamwood, and it lands right as hunters start heading to camp. As the nights turn cold, two things happen. **Fall invaders** — cluster flies, boxelder bugs, and [Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/) — mass on the sunny south and west walls and work into every gap toward the wall voids and attic. And **mice** make their annual push indoors as the woods and fields go cold and the cover clears.

Cluster flies get misread constantly. They don't breed in garbage; their larvae parasitize earthworms in the soil, so any home ringed by field, pasture, or woodland edge — which is most of Birnamwood — produces a heavy local population no matter how clean it is. The fix is an **early-fall exterior treatment** of the sun-facing walls plus sealing, not a can of spray indoors in January. For cabin and deer-camp owners, fall is the single most important service of the year: treat and seal before you close up, and you come back to a clean building instead of a wall full of flies and a mouse mess. Our [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/) and our [cabin and seasonal-home pest guide](/guides/cabin-seasonal-pest-control/) both apply directly to properties out here.

### Winter (November–February): mice inside, and the empty cabins

By winter the outdoor invasion is done, and what you have indoors is what got in during fall. In a year-round home, mice that found a warm, food-rich nest breed straight through the cold — a handful in November becomes an infestation by February if the entry points aren't sealed. On farm properties, the barns and sheds hold a large rodent reservoir pushing toward any heated space.

The harder winter problem in Birnamwood is the empty cabins and camps. A place closed after deer season with unsealed gaps becomes winter quarters for mice and a graveyard for cluster flies, and the damage — chewed wiring, fouled insulation, contaminated cabinets — builds for months before anyone sees it. A warm late-winter day wakes the overwintering cluster flies in the walls of an occupied home on this high ground and sends them crawling to the windows. And it's travel-pest season everywhere, so any place that hosted a crowd can pick up a [bed bug](/pest-library/bed-bugs/) hitchhiker.

Pests exploit specific weaknesses, and the buildings around Birnamwood offer a distinctive mix of them.

Balloon-framed walls are open vertical highways from basement to attic — that's how a mouse entering at the sill plate ends up in the attic, and how cluster flies entering at a soffit gap end up in an upstairs bedroom. Paired with pole barns, grain storage, and machine sheds full of rodents, these properties are exactly what our [farmhouse rodent exclusion guide](/guides/farmhouse-rodent-exclusion/) is written for.

And the **village homes** along the US-45 corridor are tighter but still have the usual weak points — garage-door corners, dryer-vent penetrations, and soffit-fascia junctions that let in [squirrels](/pest-library/squirrels/), bats, and fall invaders. ## Ticks and Lyme: the pest that comes with the woods

If there's one pest that defines living on Birnamwood's high, wooded ground, it's the deer tick — and it deserves more than a line. Deer ticks (blacklegged ticks) are the ones that transmit **Lyme disease**, and the amount of forest, brush, and deer traffic around here keeps their numbers up. They don't live out in your mowed lawn; they wait in the leaf litter, the tall grass, and the brushy transition zone where your yard meets the tree line, questing for a host that brushes past. That's why the highest-risk spots on a property are the exact places people walk without thinking — the path to the woodshed, the dog's run along the fence, the trail to the deer stand.

A real tick program targets that edge habitat rather than blanket-spraying open grass where ticks aren't. We treat the yard-to-woods transition and the shaded, humid harborage where ticks survive, and we time it to the two activity peaks: late spring, when nymphs are active and hardest to spot, and fall, when adults come back out. Keeping the grass short at the edges, clearing leaf litter, and putting a mulch or gravel break between lawn and woods all help, and our [tick and Lyme yard program guide](/guides/tick-lyme-yard-program/) lays out the full approach. For a family with kids and dogs on wooded property, cutting tick exposure where you actually spend time is one of the highest-value pest jobs we do out here.

## Sealing a hunting cabin the right way

The seasonal cabins and deer camps around Birnamwood need a different mindset than a year-round house, because they spend most of the year defending themselves with nobody home. Log siding is the classic weak point: the chinking cracks, the logs check and gap as they dry, and every seam becomes a highway for mice and cluster flies. Board-and-batten and rough sheds are no better. On these buildings the exclusion work is the whole game — sealing the log gaps, screening the gable and soffit vents, closing the pier and skirting openings, and stuffing every utility penetration with material a mouse can't chew.

The best time to do it is when you close up after deer season or before you leave for the year, paired with an exterior treatment on the sun-facing walls so the fall invaders never mass in the first place. A cabin sealed and treated in fall carries clean through winter; one left open becomes a mouse nest and a fly graveyard by spring. It's the same lesson as everywhere in seasonal country — the timing is what matters — and it's why we'd rather help a Birnamwood owner button up in October than clean up in May.
