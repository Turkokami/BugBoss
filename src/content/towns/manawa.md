## A rodeo town on the Little Wolf River

Manawa is a Waupaca County city of about 1,440 people, sitting at roughly 817 feet of elevation on the banks of the **Little Wolf River**. It is best known for one thing far beyond its size: the **Mid-Western Rodeo**, a genuine sanctioned rodeo held every July that has drawn crowds to town for decades and puts a small farm town on the map for a weekend each summer. The river runs right through the heart of Manawa, held back by the **Manawa Dam**, and the water is central to the town's character — enough so that when the Little Wolf flooded hard in the summer of 2024, it strained the dam and forced the rodeo to be called off, a reminder of how tied this place is to its river.

That river, and the wet ground that surrounds it, is also the single biggest factor in Manawa's pest picture. Water means mosquitoes, moisture means carpenter ants, and the mix of a compact downtown, older river-town homes, surrounding farmland, and the seasonal cabin country out toward **Bear Lake** means the pest pressure here shifts hard with the seasons and the type of property. BugBoss The X-Terminator is built for that variety. We're based just east in Clintonville, we cover the Waupaca County countryside every week, and we do **both pest control and wildlife removal** — which matters in a town of older homes near woods and water, where a call about bats or raccoons is as likely as one about mice.

## The Manawa pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): high water, thaw, and swarmers

Spring on the Little Wolf starts with runoff. As the snowpack melts, the river runs high and the low ground around town stays saturated, and that early-season moisture sets up the whole warm-weather pest cycle. Indoors, the first thing we see is things waking up: mice that wintered in a wall or crawlspace start moving and breeding, and the overwintering **cluster flies and boxelder bugs** reappear at the windows on warm afternoons — the same population from last fall, not a new one.

Spring is **carpenter ant season**, and Manawa's riverfront and low-lying homes are especially prone. Carpenter ants nest in **moisture-damaged wood**, and a house that sits near the water, or that's taken decades of small roof and plumbing leaks, gives them exactly the damp wood they want. Winged swarmers appear at windows in April and May, and large black ants show up indoors. Spot them while it's still cold out and you almost certainly have a nest living inside the heated structure — worth catching early, before the galleries spread. Our [carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) page covers the signs.

By late spring, the first **wasp** queens are scouting nest sites under eaves and dock structures, and **ticks** turn active in the brushy edges along the river and the field lines.

### Summer (June–August): mosquitoes, the rodeo crowd, and the bat window

Summer is the season the river writes. The **Little Wolf** corridor and the standing water in the floodplain make Manawa a strong mosquito town, and riverfront yards feel it most. A seasonal [mosquito](/pest-library/mosquitoes/) program that treats the shaded resting vegetation and reduces breeding sites is what makes those yards usable through July and August — which matters more here than in a lot of towns, because summer in Manawa means outdoor gatherings, and the **Mid-Western Rodeo** brings a wave of visitors, food vendors, and crowds that concentrate pest pressure downtown for the week.

Summer is also stinging-insect season. Paper wasps build under overhangs, yellowjackets nest in the ground and in wall voids, and by August the colonies are large and defensive, which is when most of our [wasp and hornet](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) calls come in.

High water carries its own pest consequence, as the 2024 flood made plain. When the river tops its banks and the floodplain saturates, **rodents and ground-nesting insects get displaced** — pushed out of burrows, fencerows, and low outbuildings and driven toward the nearest high, dry shelter, which is often a house or a barn on slightly higher ground. A wet spring or a flood event tends to be followed by a run of rodent and ant calls a few weeks later, and it's worth getting ahead of that displacement with exclusion rather than waiting for the animals to find the gaps first.

And summer is the one window when we **cannot** seal bats out of a Manawa attic. Wisconsin protects its bats, and the DNR requires that exits stay open **June 1 through July 31** during maternity season, when flightless pups are in the roost. Seal a building in July and you trap the pups inside — illegal, and a bigger mess than the colony itself. If you find bats in summer, we plan the exclusion for the legal window. The details are on the [bats](/pest-library/bats/) page and in our [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/).

### Fall (September–October): the invaders arrive

Fall is the busiest stretch of the year for Manawa pest control. As the nights cool, the **fall invaders** — [cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/), [boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/), and [Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/) — mass on the warm south and west walls of homes and start working into every gap they can find, heading for wall voids and attics to overwinter. At the same time, the **mice** begin their annual push indoors as the surrounding fields are harvested and the ground goes cold.

Cluster flies confuse a lot of people because they seem to come from nowhere. They don't breed in the house; their larvae parasitize **earthworms** in the soil, so any property surrounded by lawn, field, or the damp river bottom grows its own local population regardless of how clean the home is. The fix is an **early-fall exterior treatment** of the walls they gather on, plus sealing entry points at soffits, windows, and penetrations, rather than fighting them indoors all winter. Our [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/) explains the timing.

Fall is also the season to **close up seasonal cabins** properly around Bear Lake and the river — the single best time to seal a building against the mice and invaders that will otherwise move in while it sits empty.

### Winter (November–February): what's already inside

By winter the outdoor invasion is done, and what's in the building got in during fall. Mice that found a warm, food-rich nest breed year-round once indoors, so a few in November can become an infestation by February if the entry points stay open. Let the winter sun warm a south wall and the cluster flies that bedded down in the voids come stumbling back to the glass. Winter work in Manawa is mostly **rodent exclusion and interior cleanup**, plus keeping an eye on empty cabins where a small problem can run unchecked for months with no one there to catch it.

## Manawa's housing stock and what it lets in

Pests exploit specific weaknesses, and Manawa's building stock offers a few distinct kinds.

The **older river-town homes** in and near downtown tend to have block or fieldstone foundations, some balloon framing, and decades of settlement gaps. Balloon-framed walls run open from the basement straight to the attic, which is how a mouse entering at the sill plate ends up in the attic insulation, and how cluster flies slipping in at a soffit turn up in an upstairs room. Their proximity to the river adds a moisture load that favors carpenter ants and dampness pests.

The **village homes** on Manawa's residential streets are tighter but still leak at the usual points: garage-door corners, dryer-vent and utility penetrations, the seam where siding meets foundation, and soffit and fascia junctions that let in squirrels, bats, and fall invaders.

Out toward **Bear Lake and the river**, the picture shifts to **seasonal cabins and lake properties**. These are a special case, because the thing that makes them vulnerable is simple: nobody's there. A cabin that sits closed for weeks gives mice, cluster flies, wasps, and the occasional squirrel or bat time to settle in with no one to notice the first sign. That's why opening and closing service — sealing, treating, and inspecting on a schedule — matters so much for lake property. Our [cabin and seasonal pest control guide](/guides/cabin-seasonal-pest-control/) is written for exactly these buildings.

## Exclusion is the part that lasts

In a town shaped by water, the durable fix is almost always **exclusion** rather than repeated spraying. A treatment clears what's present now; sealing the building keeps the next wave out. That's the work that pays off across seasons, and it's exactly where the national route model tends to cut corners, because sealing an older river-town home properly takes time and attention.

On a Manawa property, exclusion gets specific. We seal around **utility and pipe penetrations**, where the drilled opening is always larger than the pipe running through it. We close the **sill-plate gap** where the wooden frame meets a block or fieldstone foundation — a continuous crack that lets in mice and cluster flies alike. We address the **soffit and fascia junctions** along the roofline that admit squirrels, bats, and fall invaders. We check **door sweeps** on garages and walkouts, which wear and leave a mouse-sized gap. And on riverfront and lake property we pay extra attention to **moisture** — grading, gutters, crawlspace ventilation, and damp sill plates — because moisture is what invites carpenter ants and dampness pests in the first place. Correcting the moisture is half the treatment.
