## The Symco pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): the thaw wakes everything up

When the frost comes out of the ground in Union, the first calls are about insects that never really left. **Cluster flies** and **[boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/)** that spent the winter tucked in wall voids and attics wake on the first warm, sunny days and crawl toward the light at farmhouse windows. Homeowners think it's a new invasion; it's the fall population finishing its winter indoors. Spring is also when **carpenter ant** swarmers show up — winged ants near windows are a sign of a nest living inside heated wood, often in a damp porch post, a leaky addition, or framing near an old roof leak.

Spring is prime time for the real work on a farm, though: **rodent exclusion before the growing season**. The mice and rats that wintered in the barn and granary are breeding, and this is when sealing the farmhouse and securing feed storage pays off for the whole rest of the year. As the fields dry and equipment comes out of the sheds, the first **wasp** queens are already scouting rafters and eaves for nest sites, and by late spring **ticks** are active in the fencerows and woodlot edges.

### Summer (June–August): stinging insects, grain pests, and the thresheree

Summer on a Symco farm is stinging-insect season. Paper wasps hang nests under eaves and inside open machine sheds, and yellowjackets set up in the ground along field edges and old foundations. By August those colonies are big and defensive — exactly when someone reaches into a shed corner or mows over a ground nest and gets stung. Our [wasp and hornet](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) calls peak here.

Summer is also when **stored-product pests** get going in warm grain and feed — Indianmeal moths, grain and flour beetles, and weevils in bins, corn cribs, and feed rooms. Warm weather speeds their life cycle, so a problem that was quiet all winter can explode by July.

And summer brings the **Union Thresheree**. Any property that draws a crowd and serves food in late July is worth getting ahead of: a pre-event knockdown of wasps and yellowjackets around food service and seating, a rodent check of any building that stores or serves food, and attention to standing water — old tires, troughs, low spots — that breeds [mosquitoes](/pest-library/mosquitoes/). Summer is also the one stretch when we legally **cannot** seal bats out; the DNR maternity window runs June 1 through July 31, and flightless pups are in the roost.

### Fall (September–October): the big push indoors

Fall is the busiest season in Symco, and it's driven by the fields. As nights cool and crops come off, three things happen at once. **[Cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/)**, **boxelder bugs**, and **[Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/)** mass on the warm south and west walls of the farmhouse and work into every gap, heading for wall voids and attics to overwinter. **[Mice](/pest-library/mice/)** and deer mice leave the harvested fields and fencerows and pour toward warm buildings. And rats around the barn get bolder as outdoor food thins out.

Cluster flies are worth understanding because Symco is textbook cluster-fly country. Their larvae parasitize earthworms in field and pasture soil, so a farmhouse surrounded by hundreds of acres of worked ground produces a heavy local population no matter how clean the house is. Chasing them with a can of spray indoors in November accomplishes nothing. The fix is an **early-fall exterior treatment** of the walls they gather on, done in September, paired with sealing the soffit, fascia, and foundation gaps they use. Everything you seal and treat in fall pays off across the whole winter. Our [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/) lays out the timing.

### Winter (November–February): what's already inside

By winter the outdoor invasion is over and you're living with whatever got in during fall. Mice that found a warm, feed-rich nest breed year-round once inside, so a few in November become a real infestation by February if the entry points aren't closed. A warm January thaw heats the farmhouse's south wall enough to stir the cluster flies and boxelder bugs wintering in the plaster, and they crawl back out to the sunny panes. This is rodent-cleanup and exclusion season, and the quietest stretch to do the sealing work that keeps next fall's wave out.

## Farm buildings are the whole story here

On a Symco property, pests aren't a house problem — they're a **whole-farmstead** problem, and each building plays a role.

The **older farmhouse** is usually the oldest structure and the leakiest. Fieldstone or block foundations, balloon-framed walls that run open from basement to attic, additions bolted on over the decades, and porches settling away from the house all create the quarter-inch gaps mice need and the soffit and fascia gaps that fall invaders and bats exploit. Balloon framing especially matters: a mouse that enters at the sill plate can end up in the attic, and cluster flies that get in at a soffit return can turn up in an upstairs bedroom.

The **barn, granary, and corn crib** are the engine of the rodent problem. Spilled grain and old feed feed the mice and rats, and open construction gives them endless harborage. You cannot solve the farmhouse mouse problem while the barn next door is running a rodent buffet — the two have to be handled together, which is why we look at feed storage and sanitation, not just the house.

**Machine sheds and pole barns** shelter wasps and yellowjackets in their open framing, host ground nests along their foundations, and give raccoons, squirrels, and feral cats places to den. And **outbuildings and old sheds** are classic bat roosts and skunk dens. We treat the farmstead as a system, because the pests do.

## Stored-product pests: the problem hiding in the feed and grain

On most Symco properties the pests you can't see are as costly as the ones you can. Warm grain, feed, and seed are their own ecosystem. **Indianmeal moths** lay eggs in stored product, and it's their larvae — small, off-white caterpillars trailing silk webbing — that ruin feed and climb to pupate in the seams and rafters of the storage building. **Sawtoothed grain beetles**, **red flour beetles**, and **granary and rice weevils** work through cracked corn, ground feed, and small grains, and because they're tiny and slow to notice, an infestation is usually well established before anyone spots it. The tells are webbing and clumping in the grain, a faint musty or sweetish smell, and small beetles or moths near lights and windows in the storage building.

The reason this matters beyond spoilage is that spilled and infested feed is the same food that sustains the mouse and rat population, so grain sanitation and rodent control are two ends of one rope. Control starts with the unglamorous work: cleaning up spillage, rotating stock so old product doesn't sit and breed insects, and pulling out the caked residue in bin corners and auger pits. On top of that goes targeted treatment of the empty, cleaned structure and ongoing monitoring with traps that tell us whether a population is climbing. Blanket-fogging a full bin accomplishes little; the fix is sanitation plus precision.

## Reading the signs before it becomes an infestation

Half of pest control on a farm is noticing early. **Rodents** leave dark, tapered droppings along walls and on beams, greasy rub marks where their fur drags the same travel routes, gnawed corners on doors and feed bags, and shredded nesting material tucked into machinery and insulation. A scratching or scurrying sound in the farmhouse ceiling at dusk usually means mice in the wall voids or something larger in the attic. **[Carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/)** leave coarse sawdust-like frass — with bits of insect parts mixed in — below the wood they're excavating, and you'll sometimes hear a faint rustling in a hollow wall on a quiet night. **Bats** announce themselves with dark guano and staining below a gable vent or fascia gap and the smell of an active roost in summer heat. Catching any of these in September saves a far bigger job in February.

## The health stakes are real on a farm

Farm pests aren't just a nuisance; several carry genuine health risks that make prompt, professional handling worth it. **Deer mice** are the main carrier of hantavirus in the rural Midwest, which is why sweeping or vacuuming a dusty, droppings-laden granary or shed is exactly the wrong move — it aerosolizes the very particles that cause illness. Accumulated bird and **bat droppings** in a long-used barn can harbor the fungus behind histoplasmosis, another reason guano cleanup is a job for someone with the right equipment and containment, not a shop broom. **Norway rats** around livestock and manure can spread leptospirosis and contaminate feed with salmonella. We handle the removal, the exclusion, and the contaminated-material cleanup with those risks in mind, so the problem is solved without trading it for a health exposure.

## Why DIY usually falls short out here

Hardware-store sprays and a handful of traps have their place, but on a working farm they rarely end the problem, because they treat symptoms instead of sources. A can of wasp spray drops the nest you can reach and misses the three you can't. A dozen snap traps clear the mice currently in the kitchen while the barn keeps producing more. A jug of over-the-counter bait knocks numbers down for a week, then the population rebounds because nothing sealed the gaps or removed the feed drawing them in. The pests that dominate Symco — cluster flies off the fields, rodents fed by the granary, carpenter ants living in moisture-softened wood — are all source-and-structure problems. Solving them takes finding where they live and how they get in, which is exactly the work a quick DIY pass skips.
