## Ag country wrapped around a sauerkraut town

**Bear Creek Township** is the agricultural town that rings the village of Bear Creek, sitting in the western corner of **Outagamie County** where it meets Waupaca and Shawano. The village itself is small — a few hundred people — but it punches above its weight: it's home to **GLK Foods**, the operation that grew out of the Flanagan Brothers sauerkraut works founded in 1900 and bills itself as the world's largest producer of sauerkraut. That's a fitting anchor for the township around it, because Bear Creek is cabbage-and-cropland country through and through, drained by the **Embarrass River** and worked by the same mix of dairy farms, row crops, and rural homesteads that defines this whole corner of the state.

For pest control, the township's identity is written in its **agricultural outbuildings** — the barns, machine sheds, feed and grain storage, and produce-handling buildings that come with a working farm economy. ## The Bear Creek Township pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): thaw, swarmers, and rodents on the move

When the frost leaves the ground, the mice that wintered in the barn, the feed room, and the farmhouse start moving and breeding, and the overwintering insects wake. Growers who thought their fall [cluster fly](/pest-library/cluster-flies/) problem was over find the flies back at the windows on the first warm April day — the same population that spent the winter in the wall voids.

Spring is **carpenter ant** season. Older farmhouses, and homes near the Embarrass River and mature trees, collect the moisture that [carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) need — years of small roof and gutter leaks, damp sill plates, wood near soil. Winged swarmers appear at south windows in April and May, and big black ants indoors while it's still cold usually mean a nest is living inside the heated house. Catch it early and you save the wood repair.

By late spring the first **wasp** queens are scouting eaves and the open rafters of sheds and pole barns, and **ticks** turn active along fencerows, woodlots, and the river edge.

### Summer (June–August): flies, stinging insects, and the bat window

Summer on a Bear Creek farm runs on flies and stinging insects. Livestock operations fight **stable flies and house flies** breeding in manure, wet bedding, and spilled feed — a nuisance and a drag on a dairy herd. Produce and food-handling operations add their own **fly and stored-product pressure** wherever raw crop is received, stored, or trimmed. Around the buildings, paper wasps, yellowjackets, and bald-faced hornets build to full, defensive size by August, when most stings happen and most of our [wasp and hornet](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) calls come in. Low, damp ground near the Embarrass keeps **mosquito** pressure up all summer.

Summer is the one stretch when we **cannot** seal bats out. Wisconsin protects its bats, and the DNR keeps roost exits legally open **June 1 through July 31** while flightless pups are present. Find [bats](/pest-library/bats/) in a farmhouse attic or barn peak in July, and the move is to schedule the exclusion for after the window, not to seal the building with pups inside. The timing is in our [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/).

### Fall (September–October): the busiest season out here

Fall is when the farm pushes its pests indoors, and it's when most township homeowners call. As the harvest wraps and the fields go cold, **mice** and deer mice pour out of the stubble, fencerows, and machinery toward the warmth of the barn, the feed room, and the house. And the **fall invaders** — cluster flies, [boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/), and [Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/) — mass on the sunny south and west walls and push into every gap toward the wall voids and attic.

Cluster flies are worth understanding, because farm families treat them as a housekeeping failure when they aren't. The larvae parasitize **earthworms** in the soil, so a farmhouse ringed by cropland, pasture, and lawn grows a population every year no matter how clean the house is. The fix is an **early-fall exterior treatment** of the walls where they gather, paired with sealing entry points. Everything you seal in September pays off all winter, which makes fall the highest-value time to get ahead of both the rodents and the flies.

### Winter (November–February): whatever got in is what you've got

By winter the outdoor invasion is done and the indoor situation is set. [Mice](/pest-library/mice/) that found a warm, feed-rich nest breed year-round once inside, so a handful in November becomes a real infestation by February if the entry points stay open — and a farm supplies all the food they need. A midwinter thaw warms the wall voids and draws the dormant cluster flies and boxelder bugs back to the windows of a Bear Creek farmhouse. Winter work here is mostly **rodent exclusion and knockdown**, fall-invader cleanup, and protecting stored feed and grain from the rodents that would otherwise winter in it.

## Agricultural outbuildings: where the pests actually live

The defining pest-control fact about Bear Creek Township is the **outbuilding**. Barns, machine sheds, feed rooms, grain storage, and produce-handling buildings are where the rodents concentrate, and they're rarely built tight: big sliding doors, open eaves, gaps at the base of steel siding, and utility penetrations everywhere. You can't make these buildings mouse-proof, and trying is a waste of money. The right approach is to **protect the zones that matter** — feed, grain, and any food or produce storage — with sanitation and targeted exclusion, and to keep the **perimeter baited** so rodents get intercepted before they reach the farmhouse.

Two details matter here. First, **residual feed and grain** in a bin or shed that isn't cleaned between fills seeds the next infestation; sanitation is the foundation of any real program. Second, **parked machinery** — the combines, wagons, and equipment that sit over winter — makes prime rodent harborage, and a mouse nest in a wiring harness is an expensive spring surprise. A working program accounts for both. Our [dairy farm pest control guide](/guides/dairy-farm-pest-control/) lays out the full approach for a livestock or crop operation.

## Norway rats around silage, manure, and produce

Mice are the everyday rodent, but the operations in Bear Creek Township that handle silage, manure, and bulk produce can also draw **Norway rats**, and rats change the job. They burrow rather than nest in walls, so you find their holes at the base of a foundation, along a slab, under a manure pack, or beside a silage face. They're strong, they gnaw through soft concrete and around utility penetrations, and they're **neophobic** — deeply suspicious of anything new in their environment — which is why a hastily placed bait station often sits untouched for days. They also need reliable water, so they concentrate near waterers, wet processing areas, and drainage. Controlling rats means reading the burrow system, placing stations along their established runs and letting them acclimate, and closing the harborage — capping burrows, clearing the clutter and vegetation they hide in, and sealing the gnawable gaps. A mouse program applied to a rat problem underperforms, so the first job is always to identify which rodent you actually have. Our [mice](/pest-library/mice/) page covers telling them apart.

## Stored-product and food-plant pests

Where crops are received, stored, trimmed, and processed, **stored-product pests** follow: Indian meal moths, sawtoothed and merchant grain beetles, and weevils that breed in residual grain, dust, and spillage in the seams and dead spots of a facility. In a food operation these aren't just a nuisance — a single larva in a finished product is a failed audit and a recall risk. That's why food-plant pest management is built on **monitoring and sanitation** first: pheromone traps that track stored-product moth activity and flag a rising population before it reaches product, insect light traps for flying pests, rigorous cleaning of the cracks and residue where larvae develop, and exclusion at doors, docks, and utility penetrations to keep pests out in the first place. Pesticide is the last resort, applied to harborage, not to product zones. On the farm side the same logic applies at a smaller scale, keeping bins clean and monitored so an infestation doesn't migrate from the storage to the farmhouse pantry.

## Farmhouses and rural homes: the residential side

The township's homes are mostly **older farmhouses and rural houses**, and they carry the standard weaknesses of rural construction: fieldstone or block foundations with mortar gaps, unsealed sill plates, and soffit and fascia junctions that give wildlife and fall invaders a way in. The oldest homes may have **balloon framing** — an open vertical cavity from basement to attic that lets a mouse entering at the foundation nest in the attic, and lets cluster flies entering at a soffit turn up in an upstairs bedroom. Around here an old farmhouse rewards a careful **exclusion inspection** far more than repeated spraying; sealing the right gaps solves problems years of trapping never touched. Our [farmhouse rodent exclusion guide](/guides/farmhouse-rodent-exclusion/) covers the specifics.

## Geography and ecology: fields, the Embarrass, and the edges

Bear Creek Township's pest pressure comes off its landscape. The **cropland and pasture** are the reservoir for cluster flies and for the mice and deer mice that flood in every fall. The **Embarrass River corridor** and low, damp ground raise mosquito pressure, drive moisture into riverside basements and crawlspaces — which invites carpenter ants and springtails — and give rodents and wildlife a cover strip to travel. The **fencerows and woodlots** hold the ticks and the carpenter ant parent nests. Deer mice, common on farms, can carry hantavirus, so cleaning out a rodent-infested shed or bin without precautions is a genuine health risk and part of why we handle rodent cleanup as its own job.

## Wildlife in Bear Creek Township: barns, attics, and the law

Old farmhouses and barns make excellent wildlife habitat, so [wildlife removal](/wildlife-removal/) is regular work here, not a sideline. **Bats** roost in attics and barn peaks. **Raccoons** den in chimneys, torn soffits, and barn corners, especially spring females raising young. **Squirrels** chew into attics and outbuildings through weak roof edges. **Skunks** den under porches, decks, and machine sheds, and **woodchucks** tunnel under foundations and grain storage where their burrows undermine the structure.

A trap pulls one raccoon out of the barn; **exclusion** — sealing the barn peak, soffit, and foundation so the next one can't den there either — is what actually ends it, with bats worked on the legal timeline. ## The exterior perimeter: stopping rodents before the dock

For any operation with a loading dock, a receiving door, or bulk exterior storage, the rodent battle is won or lost **outside the building**. Rodents move in from the fields and fencerows toward the reliable food and warmth a farm or plant offers, and the goal is to intercept them at the perimeter before they ever reach a door. That means a ring of monitored, tamper-resistant exterior **bait or trap stations** on the rodent's likely approach routes, kept clean and checked on a schedule; it means keeping the **twelve to eighteen inches against the foundation clear** of weeds, pallets, and clutter so there's no cover to travel under; and it means sealing the gaps at dock levelers, door sweeps, and utility penetrations where a mouse or rat squeezes through. Add exterior lighting that doesn't wash the wall in the bugs that in turn draw other pests, and you've cut the pressure at the source. It's unglamorous, systematic work, and it's the backbone of keeping an ag or food operation clean inside.
