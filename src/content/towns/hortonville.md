## A growing village between the Fox Cities and the farm country

Hortonville sits on the western edge of the Fox Cities in Outagamie County — close enough to Appleton that the airport is only about ten miles southeast, but far enough out that farm fields still press against the newest subdivisions. It's a real village with real history: **Alonzo Horton** bought 1,500 acres here in 1848, laid out the plat in 1849, and the community **incorporated as a village on August 11, 1894**. That same decade Hortonville ran one of the first match-light factories in the world. Today it's home to about **3,028 people** at the 2020 count and growing steadily, a bedroom community for the Fox Valley that still keeps its own downtown, its own school district, and its own lake.

That lake matters. **Black Otter Lake** — a 75-acre impoundment created by damming Black Otter Creek — runs right through the village and is the only public-access lake in Outagamie County, with boat launches, a fishing pier, and parks on its shores. The **Wolf River** flows a couple of miles to the north. Water like that is beautiful to live near and a genuine driver of pest pressure, and it's one of the things that makes Hortonville different from a landlocked farm village.

The other defining feature is growth. Hortonville is filling in with new subdivisions, and those new homes sit on ground that used to be farm field — which means the mice, voles, cluster flies, and ground-nesting insects that lived in that soil don't disappear when the houses go up; they move in. That combination of **new residential construction, an older village core, a lake corridor, and surrounding farmland** is exactly the environment BugBoss The X-Terminator works in every day. We're not a franchise dispatching a truck out of Appleton and locking you into a contract — we work the western Fox Valley on a regular route, and we do **both pest control and wildlife removal**, which most of the pest-only outfits serving this area simply won't.

## The Hortonville pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): thaw, swarmers, and the flies you thought were gone

When the ground warms, the first thing Hortonville homeowners notice is the **[cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/)**, **[boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/)**, and **[Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/)** they thought they'd beaten in fall, crawling back toward the windows on warm days. It's not a new invasion — it's the same population that overwintered in the wall voids, waking up. Spring is also when **[mice](/pest-library/mice/)** that wintered indoors are breeding and most active.

And spring is **[carpenter ant](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/)** season. Hortonville has plenty of what they want: mature trees and damp lots near Black Otter Lake, decks and porches with wood near soil, and homes both old and new with the odd slow roof or plumbing leak. In April and May winged carpenter-ant swarmers appear near windows and large black ants show up indoors — and seeing them while it's still cold out is a strong sign the nest is living inside the heated structure. By late spring the first **[wasp](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/)** queens are scouting eaves and soffits, and **[ticks](/pest-library/ticks/)** get active along the wooded and brushy edges.

### Summer (June–August): mosquitoes off the lake, and stinging insects

Summer in Hortonville is mosquito and stinging-insect season, and the lake amplifies both. **[Mosquitoes](/pest-library/mosquitoes/)** breed in the still and slow water of Black Otter Lake, Black Otter Creek, the Wolf River corridor, and the low damp yards near the water — which makes lakeside and low-lying properties the ones that benefit most from a seasonal mosquito program that treats resting vegetation and knocks down breeding sites. Paper wasps build under overhangs, yellow jackets set up in the ground and in wall voids, and by August the colonies are at their largest and most defensive, which is when most stings and most of our wasp calls come.

Summer is also the one stretch when bats cannot legally be sealed out. Wisconsin protects its bats, and the DNR requires exclusion exits stay open **June 1 through July 31** during the maternity season, when flightless pups are in the roost. If you find bats in your Hortonville attic in July, the right move is to plan the exclusion for after the window, not to seal the building and trap pups inside. We cover the timing on the [bats](/pest-library/bats/) page and in the [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/).

### Fall (September–October): the busiest season, and the one that matters most

Fall is the defining season for Hortonville pest control. As the nights cool, the **fall invaders** — cluster flies, boxelder bugs, Asian lady beetles — mass on the sunny south and west walls and work into any gap they can find, heading for the wall voids and attic to overwinter. At the same time, **mice** push indoors from the surrounding fields and fencerows, and in a growing village that's especially true of the new subdivisions built on former farm ground.

Cluster flies confuse a lot of Hortonville homeowners because they show up in clean, new houses. The reason is biology, not housekeeping: cluster fly larvae **parasitize earthworms in the soil**, so any home surrounded by lawn, park, or field — which describes most of Hortonville — produces a local population no matter how spotless the house is. That's why the fix is an **early-fall exterior treatment** of the walls they gather on, paired with sealing entry points, rather than chasing them with a can of spray indoors in January. Everything you seal and treat in September pays off across the whole winter. We go deeper in the [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/).

### Winter (November–February): what's already inside

By winter the outdoor invasion is over, and what you have indoors is what got in during fall. Mice that found a warm, food-rich nest breed year-round once inside — a handful in November becomes a real infestation by February if the entry points aren't closed. A bright January thaw heats the south and west wall voids in the older village-core homes and pulls the overwintering cluster flies and lady beetles back out to the windows. Winter is also travel-pest season, when holiday trips bring **[bed bugs](/pest-library/bed-bugs/)** home as hitchhikers. Winter work in Hortonville is mostly rodent exclusion, cleaning up the fall invaders, and dealing with anything that came home in a suitcase.

## New subdivisions, the old village core, and the lakeside homes

Pests exploit specific weaknesses, and Hortonville's mix of housing gives them a range to work with.

The **new subdivision homes** are tighter than old houses, but they're not sealed. Utility and plumbing penetrations, garage-door corners, brick-veneer weep holes, gaps where the siding meets the foundation, and unsealed sill plates are all open doors, and a new home built on a former field sits right in the path of everything that lived in that soil. New construction reassures people into thinking they're covered when they're not.

The **older homes in the village core**, dating to Hortonville's late-1800s and early-1900s growth, have the classic weak points: fieldstone and block foundations, decades of settlement gaps, and in the oldest of them balloon framing that turns the wall cavities into open highways from basement to attic. Nothing in Hortonville's housing stock pays back a slow, careful exclusion inspection the way these Alonzo-Horton-era homes in the village core do.

The **lakeside and creek-adjacent homes** around Black Otter Lake add moisture and mature trees to the picture — more mosquitoes, damper crawlspaces that draw carpenter ants and springtails, and tree limbs that give **[squirrels](/pest-library/squirrels/)**, **[raccoons](/pest-library/raccoons/)**, and bats a highway to the roofline. We treat each type on its own terms rather than running one generic program on every house.

## Water, woods, and old fields: the ecology behind the pests

The **Black Otter Lake and Wolf River corridors** are a genuine driver of the local pest picture. Standing and slow water breeds mosquitoes; damp shoreline invites carpenter ants; and the natural cover along the water gives rodents and wildlife travel routes. The **surrounding farmland**, much of it now being converted to housing, is the reservoir for the fall invaders and rodents that show up every autumn — cluster flies off the field soil, mice and voles out of the fencerows. And the **wooded edges** where lawn meets brush are where ticks wait for a host; the blacklegged ticks here carry Lyme disease, which is why our [tick and Lyme yard program](/guides/tick-lyme-yard-program/) targets that edge directly.

## Spiders, ants, and the everyday pests

Not every Hortonville pest call is a dramatic seasonal invasion. A lot of them are the steady background pests that show up in any home. **[Spiders](/pest-library/spiders/)** are near the top of the list, and they're really a symptom: spiders go where the food is, so a house with a lot of spiders in the garage, basement, and window wells has an underlying insect supply — often the same gnats, flies, and beetles drawn to exterior lights and moisture. Knock down the food source and seal the perimeter, and the spider problem fades with it. In newer subdivision homes, spiders often move in during the first fall as the surrounding disturbed ground settles.

**[Ants](/pest-library/ants/)** are the other everyday complaint. Beyond wood-damaging carpenter ants, Hortonville homes get the small nuisance ants that trail across kitchen counters and along baseboards, following moisture and food. They're especially common in spring and after heavy rain, when outdoor colonies push indoors, and near the damp ground around Black Otter Lake. The answer isn't a can of spray on the trail you can see — it's treating back to the nest and closing the entry points along the foundation. We treat both the everyday pests and the seasonal ones as part of a single perimeter strategy, because they usually come in through the same gaps.

## What a Hortonville homeowner can do — and where it stops

There's real value in the basics, and we'll always tell you straight what you can handle yourself. Keeping firewood and mulch off the foundation, trimming tree limbs back from the roofline, sealing obvious gaps with the right materials, keeping gutters clear so water doesn't feed carpenter ants, and screening vents all cut pest pressure meaningfully. For a new subdivision home, a good exterior seal in the first fall pays for itself.

Where it stops is the point most homeowners hit: a cluster-fly problem that needs a properly-timed exterior treatment, a rodent infestation that keeps coming back because the real entry points are hidden at the sill plate or a buried utility penetration, carpenter ants whose parent nest is somewhere you can't see, or any bat or wildlife issue that carries legal requirements and contamination cleanup. ## Wildlife in Hortonville: attics, chimneys, and the law

Hortonville's mix of older homes, new construction near old trees, and lakeside lots means **wildlife work** is a regular part of what we do. Bats roost in attics and behind fascia in both the old village homes and newer homes with soffit and gable gaps. **Raccoons** den in chimneys and torn soffits, especially females looking to raise young in spring. **Squirrels** chew into attics through weak roof edges. And **[skunks](/pest-library/skunks/)** den under decks, porches, and outbuildings.

A trap catches the raccoon in your chimney today; **exclusion** — sealing the torn soffit, the open gable, the uncapped flue so the next animal off the Black Otter Lake tree line can't just move into the vacancy — is what keeps you from making the same call next spring, and for bats it has to run on the DNR's calendar. We do the trapping and the sealing both, then clean out what the animals leave behind: guano packed under a bat roost, a raccoon latrine in the attic insulation, the chewed and soiled batts they nested in.
