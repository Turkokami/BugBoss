## The Portage County seat on the Wisconsin River

Stevens Point is the biggest city BugBoss The X-Terminator serves, and it sits at the far western reach of our territory — a real drive from our Clintonville base, and exactly the corridor we built this company to cover. It's the seat of **Portage County**, home to roughly **25,600 people**, and it grew up where it did for one reason: the **Wisconsin River**. George Stevens ran a supply business at this point on the river during the logging era, the first plat went in around 1847, and the city incorporated in 1858. Today the river still forms the western edge of town, the **Plover River** joins from the northeast and supplies much of the city's water, and the whole place sits up around 1,089 feet on the sandy ground of central Wisconsin.

Two things shape pest work here more than anything else. First, this is a **university town** — the University of Wisconsin–Stevens Point brings roughly 8,000 students, and with them a dense band of rental housing that turns over every year. Second, it's a **working city** with a downtown, a paper mill, an insurance headquarters, food processing, and a brewery, which means commercial pest control is as much a part of the job here as houses are. Stevens Point is denser and more varied than the farm villages and lake cabins that make up most of our map, and that variety is the whole story: a near-campus rental, an old brick house on the near north side, and a riverside home on the Green Circle Trail each fight a completely different pest battle.

We're a rural company that reaches this city on purpose. The national brands dispatch Point calls from crews covering half of central Wisconsin, lock people into annual contracts, and hand off wildlife entirely. BugBoss does **both pest control and wildlife removal**, you deal with the owner, and there's no contract holding you. ## The Stevens Point pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): thaw, swarmers, and the first flies

Spring in Stevens Point starts with a nuisance that fools people every year. The first genuinely warm, sunny days heat the wall voids and attics of older homes, and the **[cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/) and [boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/)** that slipped in last fall wake up and crawl toward the light at the windows. Homeowners think they're being invaded; really they're watching last autumn's population leave. Spraying them indoors does nothing lasting — the fix was an exterior job the previous fall.

As the frost comes out of the ground, **carpenter ant** swarmers appear. Winged ants at a window in April, or large black ants trailing across a kitchen counter on a cold spring day, usually mean a nest is living inside the structure rather than out in the yard — a distinction that matters a lot in Stevens Point's older, moisture-prone housing near the rivers. Our [carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) page walks through the difference.

Spring is also when the **near-campus rental churn** peaks. Leases turn over, furniture moves in and out on the curb, and that movement is exactly how **bed bugs** and mice travel between units. A mattress set out by one apartment can seed the building. And along the Wisconsin and Plover river bottoms, **ticks** wake early where mowed ground meets brush — the Green Circle Trail corridor runs right through that edge habitat. By May, [mice](/pest-library/mice/) that wintered indoors are breeding, and the first paper wasp queens are scouting eaves and porch corners for nest sites.

### Summer (June–August): river mosquitoes, stinging insects, and the bat window

Summer is river season, and in Stevens Point that means [mosquitoes](/pest-library/mosquitoes/). The Wisconsin River, the Plover River joining from the northeast, the wet bottomland, and the ponds strung along the **26-mile Green Circle Trail** give mosquitoes exactly what they need — standing water to breed and shaded vegetation to rest in through the day. River-side and trail-adjacent yards feel it worst in the evening. A seasonal program that knocks down the shaded resting habitat and reduces breeding sites is what makes a backyard or patio usable through July and August.

Stinging insects hit their stride now. Paper wasps under eaves and deck rails, yellowjackets nesting in the ground and in wall voids, and bald-faced hornets in trees all build toward their largest, most defensive colonies by late summer. In a college town with a lot of porches, decks, and rental units, nest calls stack up fast.

Summer is also the one stretch when we **cannot** seal bats out of a Stevens Point building. Wisconsin protects its bats, and the DNR requires that exits stay open **June 1 through July 31**, the maternity season, when flightless pups are in the roost. Seal an attic then and you trap the young inside — illegal, and a far worse mess. If you find [bats](/pest-library/bats/) in a Point attic in summer, we schedule the exclusion for the legal window and explain the whole process; the details live in our [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/).

### Fall (September–October): the invasion season

Fall is the most important pest season in Stevens Point, because it decides what your house fights all winter. As nights cool, the **fall invaders** — cluster flies, boxelder bugs, Asian lady beetles, and brown marmorated stink bugs — mass on the warm south and west walls of homes in the afternoon sun and press into every crack: soffit gaps, window frames, utility penetrations, the seam where siding meets foundation. They're heading for the wall voids and attics to overwinter. Cluster flies deserve special note because owners so often blame themselves — their larvae parasitize **earthworms** in the soil, so any Stevens Point lot near lawn, river bottom, or field grows its own supply no matter how clean the house is. The real control is an **early-fall exterior treatment** of the walls they gather on, plus sealing their entry points, and our [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/) lays out the timing.

At the same time, **mice** make their annual push indoors as the fields and river bottoms cool. September through November is peak rodent season across central Wisconsin, and a Stevens Point home with an old fieldstone or block foundation offers plenty of dime-sized gaps to exploit. Fall is the window where sealing pays off most — get ahead of the invaders and the rodents before they settle in, and you skip the winter-long problem entirely.

### Winter (November–February): what's already inside

By winter the outdoor invasion is over, and the pests that matter are the ones already in the building. Mice breed year-round once they're warm and fed, so a few that came in during October can be an established colony by March, chewing wiring and insulation and fouling cupboards. A midwinter thaw warms the wall voids just enough to send cluster flies and lady beetles drifting to sunny windows again — the same ones from fall, not a new wave. German cockroaches, meanwhile, don't care about the season at all: in the heated multi-unit rentals near campus they breed straight through winter, spreading through shared walls and plumbing chases. Winter is prime time for the interior work — rodent control, cockroach treatment in rentals, and the inspection that finds where next fall's invaders are getting in so we can seal it before they return.

## Stevens Point's housing stock: campus rentals, old neighborhoods, and river homes

Pests exploit specific weaknesses, and Stevens Point has three very different building types, each with its own.

The **near-campus rental housing** is its own world. These are older single-family homes and small multi-units converted and subdivided for students, turning over every year, with shared walls, shared plumbing, and a constant flow of secondhand furniture. That combination is tailor-made for **German cockroaches, bed bugs, and mice**, all of which move unit to unit. Treating one apartment while ignoring the others is how these problems become permanent, which is why we look at the whole building and coordinate across units when a landlord owns several.

The **older established neighborhoods** — the near north and east sides, the streets around downtown — run heavily to pre-war and mid-century homes with **fieldstone and block foundations, balloon framing, and unsealed sill plates**. These leak at the classic points: the sill-plate gap where frame meets foundation, utility and pipe penetrations where the hole is always bigger than the pipe, and soffit and fascia junctions that let in squirrels, bats, and fall invaders. Moisture in old basements and around aging gutters also feeds carpenter ants.

The **riverside and Green Circle Trail homes** add wildlife and mosquito pressure to the mix. Backing up to the Wisconsin River, the Plover, or the wooded trail corridor puts a house right at the edge where woods meet lawn — prime tick habitat, prime mosquito habitat, and a direct highway for squirrels, raccoons, and bats to the roofline.

## Geography and ecology: two rivers, sandy ground, and the trail

Stevens Point sits on the **sandy soils of central Wisconsin**, at the meeting of the Wisconsin River and the Plover River. Water drives a big share of the pest picture. The rivers and their bottomlands are prime **mosquito** breeding ground, and the moisture in low-lying and riverside homes invites **carpenter ants** and dampness pests. The wooded corridors — especially the Green Circle Trail that rings the city — put edge habitat right up against neighborhoods, and edge habitat is where **[ticks](/pest-library/ticks/)** thrive, questing where mowed lawn meets brush and woods. Deer ticks in central Wisconsin carry Lyme disease, and our [tick and Lyme yard program](/guides/tick-lyme-yard-program/) is built to push that pressure back from the yard.

The sandy ground itself matters. Well-drained sand favors **ground-nesting yellowjackets and digger wasps**, and it lets rodents tunnel and burrow easily against foundations. And because Stevens Point blends city density with river woods and nearby farm country, it gets pressure from every direction at once — the urban pests of a rental district, the structural pests of an old neighborhood, and the wildlife of a wooded river town.

## Wildlife in Stevens Point: attics, chimneys, and the law

Wildlife work is core to what BugBoss does, and in a wooded river city with a lot of older housing, the calls are steady. **Bats** roost in attics and behind fascia on the older stock. **Squirrels** chew into attics through weak soffits and roof edges — a year-round problem, not just a fall one. **Raccoons** den in chimneys and torn soffits, especially females raising young in spring, and they're strong enough to peel back roofing and soffit material. **Skunks** den under porches, decks, and sheds.

Removing the animal is only half the job. The half that lasts is **exclusion** — sealing the torn soffit, capping the chimney, closing the roof-edge gap so the next animal working the neighborhood can't take the same opening — and for bats, doing it inside the DNR's legal window. BugBoss does both, and we clean up and decontaminate what these animals leave behind: raccoon latrines, bat guano, chewed and soiled insulation. Most pest-only companies that reach Stevens Point won't touch wildlife at all; we handle it under the same roof. ## The rest of the Stevens Point picture: spiders, ants, and roaches

Beyond the headline pests, Stevens Point produces a steady supporting cast. **Spiders** are a constant in older basements, garages, and porch eaves, where they follow the insect supply drawn to lights and gaps — clear the insects and the exclusion, and the webs stop coming back. Small trailing **nuisance ants** — pavement ants and odorous house ants, not the wood-nesting carpenter ants — home in on moisture and crumbs in kitchens across every neighborhood, and finding the nest beats spraying the trail every time. In the near-campus rentals, **German cockroaches** are the hardest of the lot: they breed fast in warm kitchens and bathrooms, ride in on secondhand appliances and boxes, and spread through shared plumbing chases, so a lasting fix means treating harborage and coordinating across units rather than fogging one apartment.

There's also a seasonal wildcard worth naming. Firewood and outdoor furniture brought in for the winter can carry **overwintering insects and the occasional mouse** straight past the building envelope, which is why we look at habits and storage, not just structure, when a problem doesn't add up. In a city with as many old porches, garages, and detached structures as Stevens Point, the entry point is often something a quick perimeter spray never even looks at.

## Why a perimeter spray isn't enough here

The reason the national model underperforms in Stevens Point comes down to what a quarterly perimeter spray actually does — and doesn't. A band of product around the foundation knocks down the insects crossing it that week, but it does nothing about the cluster flies already in the wall void, the carpenter ant nest in a damp basement joist, the mice slipping through a dime-sized gap at a pipe, or the bats in the attic. Those problems live inside the structure or in the roofline, and they need to be found and either treated at the source or physically sealed out. That's the difference between spraying and solving, and it's why every BugBoss job starts with an inspection rather than a wand.
