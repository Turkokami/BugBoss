## A small city in farm-and-forest country

Gillett is a small Oconto County city — about **1,289 people** as of 2020 — sitting where the county's dairy farms give way to the woodlots and river country to the north. The post office dates to **1871**, and the town carries the name of Rodney Stephen Gillett, a pioneer settler. For more than a decade Gillett has billed itself as the **"ATV Capital of the World,"** with the local Sno & ATV Riders maintaining well over fifteen miles of trail in and around town, and it hosts the **Oconto County fairgrounds**. That tells you what you need to know about the setting: this is outdoor, recreation-and-agriculture country, ringed by farms, timber, and the flowages and rivers of the Oconto system, including the **Machickanee Flowage** downstream on the Oconto River toward Stiles and Abrams.

That means cluster flies and mice by the season, carpenter ants in damp farmhouse wood, wasps in barns and sheds, and a steady stream of wildlife looking for a way into an attic, a soffit, or an unheated cabin. ## The Gillett pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): thaw, swarmers, and the first movement

When the snow melts and the ground warms, the first thing we see in Gillett is movement. Overwintered mice start breeding and ranging through houses, barns, and cabins alike, and the **overwintering insects** — the [cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/) and [boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/) that came in last fall — wake on warm days and crawl to the windows. People read it as a new invasion; it's the same population coming out of dormancy, and it means the building was never sealed.

Spring is **carpenter ant season**. Gillett's older farmhouses and wooded-lot homes give the ants plenty to work with: mature hardwoods, damp sills and porches, and woodpiles stacked against the house. In April and May, winged [carpenter ant](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) swarmers appear at windows and big black ants show up indoors — and finding them while it's still cold usually means the nest is living inside the heated wall, not foraging in from a stump. Spring is also when cabin owners return to camps that sat closed all winter and find what moved in. By late spring, **wasp** queens scout eaves, soffits, and outbuildings, and **ticks** turn active along every edge where mowed ground meets brush and woods — and in ATV-and-hunting country, people spend a lot of time on exactly those edges.

### Summer (June–August): mosquitoes, stinging insects, and the bat window

The flowages, the Oconto River branches, and the low, wet woods around Gillett keep **mosquito** pressure up all summer, worst at dusk. A seasonal program that treats shaded resting vegetation and reduces standing-water breeding keeps a farm dooryard or a cabin clearing usable.

Stinging insects peak now. Paper wasps tuck nests under overhangs and shed eaves, yellowjackets set up in the ground and in barn and cabin voids, and bald-faced hornets build their gray footballs in yard and woodlot trees. Out here the nests often go unnoticed until someone opens a shed door or runs a mower near a ground nest — and by August the colonies are at their largest and most defensive, which is when most of our [wasp and hornet](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) calls come in.

Summer is the one window when we **cannot** seal bats out. Wisconsin protects its bats, and the DNR requires exits stay open **June 1 through July 31** during the maternity season, when flightless pups are in the roost. Seal then and you trap the pups — illegal, and a far worse mess. Bats in a farmhouse attic or a cabin in July mean scheduling the exclusion for the legal window. See the [bats](/pest-library/bats/) page and the [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/).

### Fall (September–October): the busiest season

Fall is the defining season for Gillett pest control. As the nights cool, the **fall invaders** — cluster flies, boxelder bugs, and Asian lady beetles — mass on warm south and west walls and work into every gap toward the voids and attics, and **mice** begin their annual push indoors as the fields and woods go cold.

Cluster flies are a near-universal fall problem here because their larvae parasitize **earthworms** in the soil — so a farmstead, a wooded-lot home, or a hunting cabin all grow their own populations no matter how clean the place is kept. The fix is an **early-fall exterior treatment** of the walls they gather on, plus sealing gaps at soffits, windows, and penetrations; the [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/) has the timing. Fall is also the critical window for two other jobs in Gillett: harvest pushes rodents toward feed and buildings all at once (see [farmhouse rodent exclusion](/guides/farmhouse-rodent-exclusion/)), and it's the last chance to seal and treat a hunting cabin before it sits through the season — the [cabin seasonal pest control guide](/guides/cabin-seasonal-pest-control/) covers that work.

### Winter (November–February): the payoff and the pests already inside

By winter the outdoor invasion is over, and whatever got in during fall has the building to itself. Mice breed year-round once warm and fed, so a few in November becomes a real problem by February if the entries aren't closed — and in a feed room or an unheated cabin, that pressure compounds fast. A warm January day heats the wall voids and sends cluster flies back to the windows. Winter work in Gillett is mostly rodent exclusion, cleanup of the fall invaders, and planning the spring bat and wildlife exclusions whose timing is fixed months ahead.

## Gillett housing and building stock

The **older farmhouses and village homes** tend toward fieldstone or block foundations, balloon framing, and decades of settlement gaps. Balloon-framed walls are open vertical channels from basement to attic, which is how a mouse at the sill plate ends up in the attic and how cluster flies at a soffit gap end up in an upstairs bedroom. The **farmsteads** add pole barns, machine sheds, and grain and feed storage — food, shelter, and endless entry points that keep rodent and fly pressure running year-round. ## Spiders, ground nests, and the rest of the Gillett picture

Beyond the headline pests, farm-and-forest country produces its own supporting cast. **Spiders** collect wherever a yard light or a cabin porch light burns near the woods, drawing the flying insects they feed on — heavy webbing is a symptom of that insect supply, not a spider problem in itself. **Ground-nesting yellowjackets** deserve their own mention here, because on acreage with trails, mowing, and firewood work, someone runs a mower or a wheel over a hidden nest every summer and gets swarmed; finding and treating the nest entrance is the fix, not swatting the foragers. Ordinary **ants** trail toward moisture and crumbs in kitchens and camp cabins. And clover mites and other tiny invaders show up on sunny south walls in spring, the same warm surfaces the cluster flies use — which is why a good exterior program addresses the whole wall, not one insect at a time.

## Exclusion is the part that lasts in Gillett

The durable fix in Gillett is **exclusion**, not repeated spraying — and it matters even more where buildings sit empty for stretches, because there's no one home to notice a problem starting. A treatment clears what's present now; sealing the building keeps the next wave out. On a Gillett property that means sealing around **utility and pipe penetrations** and stovepipe and chimney gaps, closing the **sill-plate and log-joint gaps** where the frame meets the foundation, addressing **soffit and fascia junctions** and weak roof edges where squirrels, bats, and raccoons get in, and correcting **moisture** that draws carpenter ants to damp wood. On a farm the same logic runs building by building: the operation is only as rodent-tight as its weakest shed. For a cabin, exclusion done before a season of vacancy is worth more than any amount of trapping done after.
