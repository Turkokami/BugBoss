## An old rail town where the pests come off the land

Wittenberg is a village of about 1,000 people in western Shawano County, out where the farm country of central Wisconsin starts giving way to the northern woods. It grew up as a **railroad and logging town** in the 1880s — the post office opened in 1880, the village took its name from Wittenberg in Germany, and the **Wittenberg Indian School** operated here from 1886 into the early twentieth century. Like a lot of towns that boomed when the rails and the timber came through, Wittenberg built a solid core of homes and commercial buildings early, and much of that century-old building stock is still standing and still lived in. That history matters, because old buildings and pest problems go together.

Wittenberg sits on relatively high ground, near the divide between watersheds, ringed by dairy and crop farmland with woodlots and the Northwoods edge pressing in from the north. That setting — old housing, open fields, and forest close by — is exactly what drives the pest picture here, and it's the environment BugBoss The X-Terminator is built to work in. We're based in Clintonville, a straightforward drive down the corridor, close enough to handle a Wittenberg call as a routine part of our week rather than a franchise dispatch from Wausau or Green Bay. The pests around here don't come from a messy neighbor; they come from the fields, the woodlots, and the gaps in a hundred-year-old wall.

## The Wittenberg pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): thaw, swarmers, and the first movement

When the snow melts and the ground warms, the first thing we see in Wittenberg is overwintering insects waking and rodents shifting. Homeowners who thought their fall [cluster fly](/pest-library/cluster-flies/) or [boxelder bug](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/) problem was over suddenly find the bugs again at the windows — not a new invasion, but the same population that spent the winter dormant in the wall voids, now crawling toward the light on warm days.

Spring is also **carpenter ant** season, and Wittenberg has the conditions they love: older homes with decades of small roof and plumbing leaks, mature trees on established village lots, and wooded properties at the town's edge. In April and May, winged [carpenter ant](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) swarmers appear near windows, and large black ants show up indoors. Seeing them in early spring — while it's still cold outside — is a strong sign the nest is living inside the heated structure. Catching that early saves real structural repair money later. By late spring the first [wasp](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) queens are scouting nest sites under eaves and in soffits, and [ticks](/pest-library/ticks/) become active along the brushy edges where village yards and farm lawns meet woodlot and tall grass.

### Summer (June–August): stinging insects, and the bat maternity window

Summer in Wittenberg is stinging-insect season. Paper wasps build under overhangs and porch roofs, yellowjackets set up in the ground and in wall voids, and bald-faced hornets hang their gray paper nests in yard trees and barn eaves. By August the colonies are at their largest and most defensive, which is when most stings happen and most of our wasp and hornet calls come in. Farm properties add their own summer pressure — flies around livestock and manure, and rodents active in the outbuildings.

Summer is also the one time of year we legally **cannot** seal bats out. Wisconsin protects its bats, and the DNR requires bat exits stay open **June 1 through July 31** during the maternity season, when flightless pups are in the roost. If you find [bats](/pest-library/bats/) in a Wittenberg attic in July — and the village's tall old homes and steepled buildings are classic roosts — the right move is to plan the exclusion, not seal the building and trap the pups inside. That's both illegal and a far bigger mess. We cover the timing in our [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/).

### Fall (September–October): the busiest season in town

Fall is the defining pest season in Wittenberg, and it's why a lot of homeowners here call us in the first place. As the nights cool, two things happen at once. **Fall invaders** — cluster flies, boxelder bugs, and [Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/) — mass on the sunny south and west walls and work into every gap, heading for wall voids and attics to overwinter. And **mice** begin their annual push indoors as the fields go cold and the harvest clears the cover they were living in.

Cluster flies deserve special mention because so many people here misunderstand them. They don't breed in your garbage; their larvae parasitize earthworms in the soil, so any home ringed by lawn, pasture, or field — which is most of Wittenberg — produces a heavy local population regardless of how clean the house is. That's why the fix is an **early-fall exterior treatment** of the walls they gather on, paired with sealing entry points, rather than chasing them with a can of spray indoors in January. Our [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/) walks through why the timing is everything. Fall is the highest-value season to get ahead of the problem, because everything you seal and treat in September pays off across the whole winter.

### Winter (November–February): the payoff, and the pests already inside

By winter the outdoor invasion is over, and what you have indoors is what got in during fall. Mice that established a warm, food-rich nest have no reason to leave, and they breed year-round once inside — a handful in November becomes a real infestation by February if the entry points aren't closed. On farm properties, that pressure is heavier, because the barns and outbuildings hold a large reservoir of rodents pushing toward any heated space. A warm late-winter afternoon heats the fieldstone-foundation wall voids and draws the overwintering cluster flies and lady beetles back to the windows. Winter work in Wittenberg is mostly rodent exclusion, cleanup of the fall invaders, and dealing with the occasional [bed bug](/pest-library/bed-bugs/) that traveled home over the holidays.

## Wittenberg's old housing is the other half of the story

Pests don't get into a house at random — they exploit specific weaknesses, and Wittenberg's building stock has a very particular set of them thanks to its rail-boom age.

The **century-old village homes** from Wittenberg's late-1800s growth tend to have fieldstone or rubble foundations, balloon framing, and decades of settlement gaps. Balloon-framed walls are essentially open vertical highways from the basement to the attic, which is how a mouse that enters at the sill plate ends up in the attic, and how cluster flies that enter at a soffit gap end up in a second-floor bedroom. A hundred-year-old balloon-framed house is the hardest kind of building to seal by guesswork and the most rewarding to inspect carefully — and it's the backbone of Wittenberg's housing.

The **mid-century and newer village homes** are tighter but still have the usual weak points: garage-door corners, dryer-vent and utility penetrations, gaps where siding meets foundation, and soffit-fascia junctions that give [squirrels](/pest-library/squirrels/), bats, and fall invaders a way in. And out in the surrounding **Town of Wittenberg**, the farmsteads bring the full rural picture — pole barns, grain storage, machine sheds, and older farmhouses with the heavy rodent pressure that comes with living next to open field and stored feed. Our [farmhouse rodent exclusion guide](/guides/farmhouse-rodent-exclusion/) is written for exactly these properties. We treat each building type on its own terms.

## Spiders, ants, and the pests of an old house

Old houses come with a standing cast of smaller pests that rarely make the headlines but drive plenty of calls. [Spiders](/pest-library/spiders/) move into the undisturbed corners of basements, porches, and garages that century-old homes have in abundance, and they multiply wherever other insects gather — around exterior lights, in cluttered outbuildings, along foundation cracks. Nuisance [ants](/pest-library/ants/) trail indoors through the same settlement gaps that let everything else in, following moisture and food to the kitchen. Neither is a cleanliness failure in a home this old; they're a symptom of a building with a lot of openings and a lot of harborage. The fix is the same perimeter-and-exclusion approach that handles the bigger problems — treat the exterior, cut off the insect food supply, and seal the ways in.

## Reading the signs: what actually tells you it's time to call

Part of doing this work in a town of old homes is knowing what the early signs look like before a problem becomes an infestation. A few dead flies on a south-facing windowsill in October is the start of a cluster fly problem, not the end of one — it means the walls are filling. Faint scratching in the walls or ceiling at dusk points to mice on the move or, if it's up high and rhythmic, squirrels. A scatter of coarse black droppings the size of rice grains near the baseboards is mouse activity; pencil-shaving-like piles of clean wood shavings under a window frame or sill mean carpenter ants are excavating nearby. A sudden ammonia smell in an upstairs room, with staining on the ceiling, is often bats. Catching any of these early is the difference between a quick, contained job and a structural one, and it's exactly why we'd rather get a Wittenberg call in September than in February.
