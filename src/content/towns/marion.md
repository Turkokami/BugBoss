## A dairy town split by a county line

Marion is a small city of about 1,300 people that sits right on the **Waupaca–Shawano county line** — most of it in Waupaca County, a sliver over the border in Shawano — in the heart of northern dairy country. A post office has operated here since **1880**, and the town takes its name from **Francis Marion**, the Revolutionary War officer known as the "Swamp Fox." The **mill pond** in the middle of town is the kind of feature that tells you how the place grew up: a dammed stream, a mill, and a village that formed around the water and the farms that spread out from it. In the decades since, Marion has stayed what it always was — a farm-service town, now with a substantial **Amish community** that has settled the surrounding countryside since the mid-1990s and farms much of the land around it the old way.

That setting shapes the pest work here more than anything else. Marion is not a subdivision; it's a compact village core wrapped in pasture, cropland, woodlots, and working farms. The pests that trouble Marion homes come off that land — out of the fields at harvest, off the pond and the low wet ground, out of the fencerows and woodlots — and they arrive on a seasonal schedule you can nearly set a calendar by. BugBoss The X-Terminator is built for exactly this kind of place. We're based down the road in Clintonville, we already work both sides of the county line every week, and we do **both pest control and wildlife removal**, which matters a great deal in a town full of century-old farmhouses and outbuildings that draw everything from mice to bats.

## The Marion pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): thaw, swarmers, and the first movement

When the frost comes out of the ground and the fields start to green, the first calls out of Marion are usually about things waking up rather than things newly arriving. Mice that spent the winter in a wall void or a barn start moving and breeding, and the overwintering insects — cluster flies, boxelder bugs, the odd Asian lady beetle — reappear at the windows on warm afternoons. Homeowners who thought last fall's fly problem was over see it again. It's not a new invasion; it's the same population that never really left, now crawling toward the light.

Spring is also **carpenter ant season**, and Marion's older housing stock gives them plenty to work with. These ants don't eat wood — they hollow out **moisture-damaged wood** to build their galleries, and a farmhouse with a hundred years of small roof leaks, ice-dam damage, and damp sill plates is prime real estate. In April and May, winged swarmers show up around windows, and big black ants start appearing indoors. If you're seeing them while it's still cold outside, the nest is almost certainly living inside the heated structure, not just foraging in. Catching that early saves real repair money. Our [carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) page walks through what to look for.

By late spring, the first **wasp** and hornet queens are scouting nest sites under eaves and barn overhangs, and **ticks** turn active along the brushy edges where lawns and pastures meet woods.

### Summer (June–August): stinging insects, farm flies, and the bat window

Summer is stinging-insect season. Paper wasps build under porch roofs and barn eaves, yellowjackets nest in the ground and in wall voids, and by August the colonies are big and touchy — which is when most of our [wasp and hornet](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) calls come in. On the farms, summer means **fly pressure**: house flies and stable flies bred in manure, bedding, and spilled feed can make a milk house or a farm kitchen miserable, and knocking them back takes sanitation and source control, not just a strip of flypaper.

Summer is also the one stretch of the year when we **cannot** seal bats out of a Marion attic or barn. Wisconsin protects its bats, and the DNR requires that exits stay open **June 1 through July 31** during the maternity season, when flightless pups are in the roost. Seal a building in July and you trap the pups inside — illegal, and a far worse mess than the bats themselves. If you find a colony in summer, the right move is to plan the exclusion for the legal window. We lay out the timing on the [bats](/pest-library/bats/) page and in our [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/).

### Fall (September–October): the busiest season in dairy country

Fall is the defining season for Marion pest control, and it's when most people here first pick up the phone. Two things happen at once as the nights cool. First, the **fall invaders** — [cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/), [boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/), and [Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/) — mass on warm south and west walls and start working into every gap they can find, heading for wall voids and attics to overwinter. Second, the **mice** begin their annual push indoors as the crops come off and the fields go cold.

Cluster flies deserve a special note in a farm town, because so many people take them personally. They don't breed in your garbage or your drains. Their larvae parasitize **earthworms** in the soil, so any home ringed by lawn, pasture, or field — which is essentially every property around Marion — produces its own local crop of them regardless of housekeeping. That's why the effective move is an **early-fall exterior treatment** of the walls they gather on, paired with sealing the gaps at soffits, windows, and utility penetrations, rather than chasing them with a spray can indoors in January. Our [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/) covers the approach in detail.

Fall is the highest-value time to get ahead of all of it, because every gap you seal and every wall you treat in September pays off across the whole winter.

### Winter (November–February): what's already inside

By winter the outdoor invasion is over, and whatever is in the building got in during fall. Mice that found a warm, food-rich nest have no reason to leave, and they breed year-round once indoors — a handful in November can be a real infestation by February if the entry points stay open. This is the season for **rodent exclusion and interior cleanup**: sealing the building envelope, running a control program, and dealing with the fall invaders that reappear on warm days. It's also when the occasional **deer mouse** on a farm becomes a health concern, since deer mice are associated with hantavirus and their droppings shouldn't be swept up dry. Our [farmhouse rodent exclusion guide](/guides/farmhouse-rodent-exclusion/) is written for exactly this kind of property.

## Marion's housing and farm stock is half the story

Pests don't pick a house at random; they exploit specific weaknesses, and Marion's building stock has a particular set of them.

The **older farmhouses** in and around town — many well over a century old, some visible on the town's turn-of-the-century Sanborn fire maps — tend to have fieldstone or block foundations, balloon framing, and decades of settlement gaps. Balloon-framed walls are essentially open vertical shafts running from the basement to the attic, which is how a mouse entering at the sill plate ends up nesting in the attic insulation, and how cluster flies that slip in at a soffit turn up in an upstairs bedroom. Nothing in Marion pays back a slow, careful exclusion walk-through like one of these old farmhouses.

The **village homes** on Marion's residential streets are tighter but still have the usual weak points: garage-door corners, dryer-vent and utility penetrations, gaps where siding meets foundation, and the soffit and fascia junctions that give squirrels, bats, and fall invaders their way in.

And then there are the **farm buildings** — the part of Marion that sets it apart from a plain village. **Pole barns, milk houses, machine sheds, grain bins, and feed rooms** are magnets for rodents and flies because they combine food, shelter, and constant traffic in and out. A pole barn with a gap under the sheeting is a rodent turnpike. We treat each of these on its own terms; a program that works in a farm kitchen is not the program that works in a machine shed, and we don't pretend otherwise.

## Geography and ecology: the pond, the fields, and the woodlots

The **mill pond** and the low, damp ground around it are a genuine driver of the local pest picture. Standing and slow-moving water is mosquito habitat, and river- or pond-adjacent properties see more moisture in basements and crawlspaces — which invites carpenter ants, springtails, and the occasional overwintering population of other pests. A seasonal [mosquito](/pest-library/mosquitoes/) program makes a real difference for homes and events near the water.

The **surrounding farmland** is the reservoir for the fall invaders and rodents that arrive every autumn. Cluster flies come off the field soil, mice and deer mice come out of the harvested fields and fencerows, and the **woodlots** that break up the farm country give carpenter ants their outdoor parent nests and give squirrels, raccoons, and bats a path to your roofline. The [ticks](/pest-library/ticks/) that carry Lyme disease live in the brushy edges where mowed ground meets tall grass and brush — which, on a farm, is nearly everywhere. ## Wildlife in Marion: attics, barns, and the law

## Stored-product pests, spiders, and the rest of the Marion picture

The fall invaders and the mice get the headlines, but a farm town produces a longer list than that, and it's worth knowing what else we see. **Stored-product pests** — Indian meal moths, sawtoothed grain beetles, and the like — turn up wherever grain, feed, birdseed, or pet food sits for a season. On a Marion farm that can mean a feed room or a bin; in a village kitchen it's more often a forgotten bag of flour or dry pet food in the pantry. The control is the same in principle: find and remove the infested source, because these insects breed in the food itself, and no amount of spraying the shelves fixes a problem that's living in the bag.

**Spiders** are the pest most Marion homeowners notice without ever seeing what feeds them. A house with lots of spiders usually has lots of other insects — that's the food supply — so a spider problem is often really a fall-invader or fly problem wearing a different face. Reducing the insects the spiders eat, sweeping down webs, and sealing the gaps that let both in does more than any amount of chasing individual spiders. Our [spiders](/pest-library/spiders/) and [ants](/pest-library/ants/) pages cover the everyday nuisance invaders that round out the picture, and general [ant](/pest-library/ants/) trails — the small ones on the kitchen counter, distinct from carpenter ants — are a steady spring and summer call in the village homes.

None of these are dramatic, but they're the difference between a house that's genuinely handled and one that just had the obvious problem knocked back. When we inspect, we're looking at the whole building, not the single pest you called about.

## Exclusion is the part that lasts

If there's one idea worth repeating in a town like Marion, it's that **exclusion outlasts spray**. A treatment kills what's there now; sealing the building keeps the next wave out. That's the work that pays off, and it's exactly where the national route model tends to fall short, because sealing a hundred-year-old farmhouse takes time and attention, not a two-minute perimeter spritz.

On a Marion property, exclusion means getting specific. We seal around **utility and pipe penetrations**, where the drilled hole is always bigger than the pipe. We close the **sill-plate gap** where the wooden frame meets a fieldstone or block foundation — a continuous crack that runs the perimeter of many old houses and lets in everything from mice to cluster flies. We address **soffit and fascia junctions**, the roofline gaps that admit squirrels, bats, and fall invaders alike. We look at **garage and barn door sweeps**, which wear out and leave a half-inch gap a mouse walks right under. And on the farm side, we look hard at where **sheeting meets the ground on a pole barn**, the single most common rodent entry on a modern outbuilding. Done right, that work holds for years — which is the whole point.
