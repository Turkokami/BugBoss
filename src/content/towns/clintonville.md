## A river town where the pests come from the land, not the neighbors

Clintonville sits in northern Waupaca County on the **Pigeon River**, at about 814 feet of elevation, a city of roughly 4,600 people that grew up as a mill and manufacturing town after Norman Clinton settled the site along the river in 1855. The Menominee who were here first called the area something close to *Omīniahkan* — "the place where pigeons are hunted" — and the river still runs through the middle of town today. Clintonville later became the birthplace of the **Four Wheel Drive (FWD) Auto Company** in 1909 and is still home to the **Seagrave Fire Apparatus** plant, which tells you something useful about the pest picture here: this is a town with an old, established housing core, a genuine industrial and commercial base, and open farmland pressing in on every edge.

That combination is exactly what drives pest pressure in Clintonville, and it's why BugBoss The X-Terminator is built the way it is. ## The Clintonville pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): thaw, swarmers, and the first movement

When the snow melts and the ground warms, the first thing we see in Clintonville is **rodent activity shifting** as mice that wintered indoors start moving and breeding, and overwintering insects wake up. Homeowners who thought their fall cluster fly or boxelder bug problem was over suddenly find the bugs again — not a new invasion, but the same population that spent the winter dormant in the wall voids, now crawling toward the windows on warm days.

Spring is also **carpenter ant season**. Clintonville has a lot of the conditions carpenter ants love: mature trees along the Pigeon River, older homes with decades of small roof and plumbing leaks, and decks and porches with wood near or in contact with damp soil. In April and May, winged carpenter ant swarmers appear near windows, and large black ants show up indoors. Seeing them in early spring — while it's still cold out — is a strong sign the nest is living inside the heated structure, not just foraging in from outdoors. This is the window where catching an infestation early saves real structural repair money later. You can read the full breakdown on our [carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) page.

By late spring, the first **wasp** queens are scouting nest sites under eaves and in soffits, and **ticks** become active along the edges where Clintonville yards meet brush and woods.

### Summer (June–August): stinging insects, mosquitoes, and the bat maternity window

Summer in Clintonville is stinging-insect and mosquito season. Paper wasps build under overhangs, yellow jackets set up in the ground and in wall voids, and by August the colonies are at their largest and most defensive — which is when most stings happen and most of our [wasp and hornet](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) calls come in. The **Pigeon River** and the low, damp spots in and around town keep **mosquito** pressure up all season, and properties that host graduation parties, weddings, and summer gatherings are the ones that benefit most from a seasonal [mosquito](/pest-library/mosquitoes/) program.

Summer is also the one time of year we *cannot* seal bats out. Wisconsin protects its bats, and the DNR requires that bat exits stay open **June 1 through July 31** during the maternity season, when flightless pups are in the roost. If you find bats in your Clintonville attic in July, the right move is to plan the exclusion — not to seal the building and trap pups inside, which is both illegal and a far bigger mess. We cover the timing in detail on the [bats](/pest-library/bats/) page and in our [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/).

### Fall (September–October): the busiest season in town

Fall is the defining season for Clintonville pest control, and it's the reason a lot of homeowners here call us in the first place. As the nights cool, three things happen at once. **Fall invaders** — [cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/), [boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/), and [Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/) — mass on the sunny south and west walls of the house and start working into any gap they can find, heading for the wall voids and attic to overwinter. And **mice** begin their annual push indoors, looking for warmth and food as the fields go cold.

Cluster flies deserve special mention in Clintonville because so many people misunderstand them. They don't breed in your garbage; their larvae parasitize earthworms in the soil, so any home surrounded by lawn, park, or field — which is most of Clintonville — produces a local population regardless of how clean the house is. That's why the fix is an **early-fall exterior treatment** of the walls they gather on, paired with sealing entry points, rather than chasing them with a can of spray indoors in January.

Fall is the highest-value time to get ahead of the problem, because everything you seal and treat in September pays off across the entire winter.

### Winter (November–February): the payoff, and the pests already inside

By winter the outdoor invasion is over, and what you have indoors is what got in during fall. Mice that established a warm, food-rich nest have no reason to leave, and they breed year-round once inside — a handful in November becomes a real infestation by February if the entry points aren't closed. A stretch of mild January sun heats the south-wall voids and the cluster flies that settled in back in October crawl out to the windows again. This is also **bed bug and travel-pest season**, as holiday travel brings hitchhikers home. Winter work in Clintonville is mostly rodent exclusion, cleanup of the fall invaders, and dealing with anything that came home in a suitcase.

## Clintonville's housing stock is the other half of the story

Pests don't get into a house at random — they exploit specific weaknesses, and Clintonville's building stock has a very particular set of them.

The **older downtown and near-downtown homes**, many dating to the town's late-1800s and early-1900s growth, tend to have fieldstone or rubble foundations, balloon framing, and decades of small settlement gaps. Balloon-framed walls are essentially open vertical highways from the basement to the attic, which is how a mouse that enters at the sill plate ends up in the attic, and how cluster flies that enter at a soffit gap end up in a second-floor bedroom. Of every building type in Clintonville, these old downtown houses give a thorough exclusion inspection the most to work with.

The **mid-century village homes** on Clintonville's residential streets are tighter but still have the usual weak points: garage-door corners, utility and dryer-vent penetrations, gaps where the siding meets the foundation, and soffit and fascia junctions that give squirrels, bats, and fall invaders a way in.

And on the **edges of town**, where city lots give way to farmsteads and rural properties, you get the full rural picture — pole barns, outbuildings, and older farmhouses with the entry points and rodent pressure that come with living next to open field. We handle all three, and we treat each on its own terms rather than running the same program on every house.

## Geography and ecology: why the Pigeon River and the fields matter

The **Pigeon River** corridor through Clintonville is a genuine driver of the local pest picture. River-adjacent and low-lying properties see more mosquitoes, more moisture in crawlspaces and basements (which invites carpenter ants and springtails), and more rodent travel along the natural cover the corridor provides. Mature trees along the river give carpenter ants their outdoor parent nests and give squirrels and raccoons highways to your roofline.

The **farmland and woodlots** surrounding the city are the reservoir for the fall invaders and rodents that show up every autumn. Cluster flies come off the field soil; mice and deer mice come out of the harvested fields and fencerows; ticks live in the brushy edges. None of that is a knock on Clintonville — it's simply what pest control looks like in a small river city ringed by agriculture, and it's the environment BugBoss was built to work in.

## Wildlife in Clintonville: attics, chimneys, and the law

Clintonville's older homes and its proximity to river and woodland mean **wildlife work** is a regular part of what we do here, not a sideline. Bats roost in attics and behind fascia; raccoons den in chimneys and torn soffits, especially females looking for a place to raise young in spring; squirrels chew into attics through weak roof edges; and skunks den under decks, porches, and outbuildings.

A trap that removes tonight's raccoon does nothing about the torn soffit it climbed through, so the part that matters is **exclusion** — closing the building against this animal and the next one to try the same gap — and, with bats, doing it on the state's calendar.
