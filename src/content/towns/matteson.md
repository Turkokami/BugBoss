## A dairy township where the pests come with the ground

The **Town of Matteson** is farm country in the truest sense — roughly 37 square miles of Waupaca County dairy ground, cropland, and woodlot sitting west of Manawa at about 800 feet of elevation, home to fewer than a thousand people spread across working farmsteads and a scattering of rural homes. There's no downtown to speak of; the former settlement of Hunting once sat partly inside the town line, but Matteson today is what it has always been: land, barns, and the families who work them. That character is the single most important fact about pest control here.

When your township is built around dairy and row crops, the pest picture is not driven by human density the way it is in a city. It's driven by **agriculture** — by the concentration of feed, grain, and warm shelter that farms create, and by the field soil and edge habitat that surround every house. ## The Matteson pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): thaw, swarmers, and rodents on the move

When the frost leaves the ground, the mice that spent winter in your barn and farmhouse start moving and breeding, and the overwintering insects wake up. A lot of Matteson farmers think their fall fly problem ended in November — then a warm April day heats the wall voids and the [cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/) are back at the windows. That's not a new invasion; it's the same population that never left.

Spring is also **carpenter ant** season. Farmhouses collect the exact conditions [carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) want: decades of small roof and gutter leaks, damp sill plates, porch posts near soil, and mature yard trees for the outdoor parent nest. Winged swarmers show up at south windows in April and May. Seeing big black ants indoors while it's still cold out usually means a nest is living inside the heated house, not just foraging in — and that's the moment to deal with it, before the wood damage adds up.

By late spring the first **wasp** queens are scouting eaves, soffits, and the rafters of open sheds, and **ticks** get active along every fencerow and woodlot edge on the property.

### Summer (June–August): flies, stinging insects, and the bat window

Summer on a Matteson farm is fly and stinging-insect season. Beyond the cluster flies, working livestock operations deal with **stable flies and house flies** breeding in manure, spilled feed, and wet bedding — a genuine nuisance and a productivity drag on a dairy herd. Around the house and outbuildings, paper wasps, yellowjackets, and bald-faced hornets build through the summer and reach their largest, most defensive size by August, which is when most stings happen and most of our [wasp and hornet](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) calls come in. Ground-nesting yellowjackets in a hay field or along a walkway are the ones that put people in the ER.

Summer is also the one stretch when we **cannot** seal bats out. Wisconsin protects its bats, and the DNR requires that roost exits stay open **June 1 through July 31**, while flightless pups are present. If you find [bats](/pest-library/bats/) in a Matteson farmhouse attic or barn in July, the right move is to schedule the exclusion for after the window — not to seal the building and trap pups inside. We cover the timing on our [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/).

### Fall (September–October): the busiest season out here

Fall is when the farm pushes its pests indoors, and it's when most Matteson homeowners call. As the fields go cold and the harvest wraps up, two things happen together. **Mice** and deer mice come out of the stubble, fencerows, and machinery and head for the warmth of the barn, the feed room, and the house. And the **fall invaders** — cluster flies, [boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/), and [Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/) — mass on the sunny south and west walls and work into every gap they can find, aiming for the wall voids and attic to overwinter.

Cluster flies are worth understanding because farm families take them personally when they shouldn't. The larvae parasitize **earthworms** in the soil, so a farmhouse surrounded by pasture, lawn, and cropland produces a population every year no matter how clean the house is. The fix is an **early-fall exterior treatment** of the walls they gather on, paired with sealing entry points — not a can of spray in the kitchen in January. Everything you seal in September pays off all winter. This is the highest-value time of year to get ahead of the rodents and the flies both.

### Winter (November–February): whatever got in is what you've got

By winter the outdoor invasion is over and the indoor situation is set. [Mice](/pest-library/mice/) that found a warm, feed-rich nest — and a dairy provides that in abundance — breed year-round once inside, so a handful in November becomes a real infestation by February if the entry points stay open. A mild spell in midwinter warms the wall voids and coaxes the dormant cluster flies back out to the farmhouse windows. Winter work in Matteson is mostly **rodent exclusion and knockdown**, cleanup of the fall invaders, and protecting stored feed and grain from the rodents that would otherwise winter in it.

## Farm buildings and farmhouses: where the pests actually get in

Pests don't enter a building at random. They exploit specific weaknesses, and Matteson's building stock — old farmhouses and agricultural outbuildings — has a distinct set of them.

The **farmhouses** here skew old, and many carry the classic problems of pre-war rural construction: fieldstone or rubble-block foundations with mortar gaps, unsealed sill plates, and in the oldest homes, balloon framing. A balloon-framed wall is an open vertical shaft from the basement to the attic, which is exactly how a mouse entering at the foundation ends up nesting in the attic insulation, and how cluster flies entering at a soffit gap end up in an upstairs bedroom. Nothing pays off on these old Matteson farmhouses like a careful exclusion inspection — sealing the right gaps solves problems that years of trapping never touched. Our [farmhouse rodent exclusion guide](/guides/farmhouse-rodent-exclusion/) walks through the specifics.

The **pole barns, machine sheds, and dairy barns** are a different animal. They're rarely sealed tight to begin with — big sliding doors, open eaves, gaps at the base of steel siding, and utility penetrations everywhere. The goal there isn't to make them mouse-proof (you can't), it's to protect the critical zones: the **milkhouse, feed room, and any grain or stored-product area**, where sanitation and targeted exclusion keep rodents out of the parts that matter. Perimeter bait stations catch the rest before they reach the house.

## Rats versus mice: knowing which one you're fighting

On a Matteson dairy the rodent question isn't just "how many," it's "which." **House mice** are the common problem in the farmhouse and feed room — small, curious, nesting close to food, squeezing through a dime-sized gap. But a working livestock operation with silage, manure, and spilled grain can also draw **Norway rats**, and those are a different animal to control. Rats burrow — you'll see holes at the base of a foundation, along a slab edge, or under a manure pack — they're neophobic (wary of anything new, which makes them harder to bait), and they need more water, so they concentrate near the waterers and wet areas. A program that works on mice can fail on rats if it doesn't account for the burrowing and the caution. We identify which species you have before we set a single station, because the placement, the bait, and the exclusion all change with the answer. See our [mice](/pest-library/mice/) page for the identification details.

## Deer mice, hantavirus, and safe cleanup

The rodent you least want to ignore on a farm is the **deer mouse**. They're the ones that move into a shed, a grain bin, or a seasonal building over winter, and unlike house mice they're a recognized carrier of **hantavirus** — a rare but serious respiratory illness spread through aerosolized droppings and urine. The danger isn't the live mouse so much as the cleanup: sweeping or vacuuming a heavily soiled shed kicks the virus into the air. That's why we treat rodent **cleanup** as its own job, with the right containment and disinfection, rather than handing you a broom. If you've got a machine shed or grain bin that's been mouse-central all winter, don't dry-sweep it — call us to do it safely.

## Geography and ecology: dairy ground, woodlots, and field edges

Matteson's pest pressure comes straight off its landscape. The **cropland and pasture** are the reservoir for cluster flies and for the mice and deer mice that flood in every fall. The **woodlots and fencerows** hold the ticks, the carpenter ant parent nests, and the wildlife — raccoons, squirrels, and skunks — that follow the cover onto your buildings. **Low, damp field edges and ditches** breed the mosquitoes that make evening chores miserable in July. Deer mice are worth a specific mention on farms because they can carry hantavirus; cleaning out a rodent-infested shed or grain bin without the right precautions is a real health risk, and it's part of why we handle rodent cleanup as its own job.

## Timing an exclusion right on a Matteson farm

One thing farmers appreciate is that exclusion has a season, and doing it on the calendar saves money. The best time to seal a building against rodents is **late summer into early fall**, before the big push indoors — get the gaps closed in August and September and you head off the November invasion instead of chasing it. Fall-invader exterior treatment lands in the same window, early fall, on the walls the flies gather on. Bat exclusion runs the opposite of intuition: never during the June 1–July 31 maternity season, but ideally in the spring shoulder before it or the late-summer stretch after it. Wasp nests are dealt with when found, but the pressure builds all season and peaks in August. We plan the year around these windows so the work lands when it actually pays off, not whenever a franchise route happens to swing by.
