## A small village where the farm country runs into the Northwoods

Gresham is a village of about 530 people in northwestern Shawano County, sitting on the **Red River** right where the open farm country of the lower county gives way to the heavy forest of the Northwoods. Its post office opened in December 1883, and the village took its name from **Walter Q. Gresham**, the U.S. Postmaster General at the time. Gresham's best-known place in the history books came in 1975, when the former **Alexian Brothers Novitiate** just outside the village was occupied for about a month by the Menominee Warrior Society — a reminder that this is a corner of Wisconsin where the reservation communities, the river, and the forest all meet.

For pest control, that meeting point is the whole story. Gresham is a **transition-zone town**: village lots and farmsteads on one side, dense woods on the other, with the Red River threading through the middle. Transition zones are the richest pest habitat there is. You get the fall-invader and rodent pressure of open farm country, the ticks and carpenter ants and wildlife of the forest, and the mosquitoes and moisture of a river corridor — all in the same few square miles. A town like this doesn't have one pest problem; it has the full menu, and which one bites you depends on where your property sits along that gradient.

## The Gresham pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): thaw, swarmers, and the first ticks

When the snow lets go, Gresham's overwintering pests wake first. The **cluster flies and Asian lady beetles** that spent the winter in wall voids crawl toward the windows on warm days — not a new invasion, just the survivors of last fall's population showing themselves. **Mice** that wintered indoors get active and start breeding.

Spring is prime **carpenter ant** season here, and Gresham has everything they want: heavy tree cover holding outdoor parent nests, and older homes with the small roof and plumbing leaks that soften interior wood for satellite colonies. Winged swarmers at a window in April or May, or big black ants indoors while it's still cold, point to a nest living inside the heated structure. Catching that early saves real repair money, which is why our [carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) page walks through telling a foraging trail from an actual indoor nest.

Late spring is also when **ticks** turn on. In a town where lawns run straight into brush and forest, blacklegged ticks are waiting along every edge, and Lyme risk climbs. This is the window to get a [tick and Lyme yard program](/guides/tick-lyme-yard-program/) going, before the grass is tall and the deer are moving through.

### Summer (June–August): stinging insects, river mosquitoes, and bats

Summer in Gresham is stinging-insect and mosquito season. **Paper wasps** build under eaves, **yellow jackets** nest in the ground and in wall voids, and **bald-faced hornets** hang their gray paper nests in trees and shrubs. By August those colonies are at full size and most defensive — which is when the [wasp and hornet](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) calls come in and when most stings happen.

The **Red River** corridor and the woodland pools and damp ground around it keep **mosquito** pressure steady through the season. Shaded, wooded lots hold mosquitoes even in dry spells because the resting vegetation stays cool and humid, so a seasonal [mosquito](/pest-library/mosquitoes/) program that treats that foliage and eliminates standing-water breeding sites is what makes a Gresham backyard usable on a July evening.

Summer is also the one time of year we **cannot** seal bats out. Wisconsin keeps bat exits open **June 1 through July 31** for the maternity season, when flightless pups are in the roost. Find bats in a Gresham attic in July, and the move is to plan the exclusion for August — not to trap pups inside, which is illegal and a far bigger mess.

### Fall (September–October): the busiest stretch

Fall is the defining season, and it's why most Gresham homeowners first call us. As the nights cool, the **fall invaders** — [cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/), [boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/), and [Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/) — mass on the warm south and west walls and work into soffit, fascia, and eave gaps to overwinter in the wall voids and attic.

Cluster flies are worth calling out in a town like Gresham, because so many people take them personally. Their larvae parasitize earthworms in the soil, so any property with lawn, field, or woods around it — which is every property here — produces a local population no matter how clean the house is. The fix is an **early-fall exterior treatment** of the walls they gather on, paired with sealing entry points, not a can of spray in January.

At the same time, **mice** make their annual push indoors as the fields and woods go cold, and deer mice out of the surrounding forest and fencerows are a specific hantavirus concern on rural properties. Fall is the highest-value time to get ahead of all of it — everything you seal and treat in September pays off across the entire winter.

### Winter (November–February): what's already inside

By winter the outdoor invasion is over and what you have indoors is what got in during fall. **Mice** that found a warm, food-rich nest breed year-round once inside, so a couple in November becomes a real infestation by February if the entry points aren't sealed. **Overwintering cluster flies and lady beetles** rouse on a warm, sunny day and collect against the south-facing windows of a river-corridor home. And holiday travel is when **bed bugs** hitchhike home in luggage — a pest that has nothing to do with cleanliness and everything to do with where you've been. Winter work in Gresham is mostly rodent exclusion, cleanup of the fall invaders, and dealing with anything that came home in a suitcase.

## Housing stock in Gresham: where pests get in

Gresham's building stock is a small-village-and-farm mix, and each type has its own weak points.

The **older village homes and nearby farmhouses**, many dating back generations, tend to have stone, rubble, or block foundations, balloon framing, and decades of small settlement gaps. Balloon-framed walls are open vertical runs from basement to attic, which is how a mouse entering at the sill plate ends up in the attic, and how cluster flies entering at a soffit gap end up in an upstairs bedroom. Between the fieldstone footing and the open stud runs, these homes reward a careful exclusion inspection more than any other type in Gresham.

The **newer village homes** are tighter but still lose the usual battles at garage-door corners, dryer and utility vent penetrations, the gap where siding meets foundation, and soffit and fascia junctions that give squirrels, bats, and fall invaders a route in.

And on the wooded edges — the **rural and forest-adjacent properties** toward the Stockbridge-Munsee community and Bowler — you get the full Northwoods picture: outbuildings, pole barns, older cabins and homes surrounded by trees, with heavy carpenter ant, rodent, tick, and wildlife pressure. ## Ticks and Lyme: the risk that comes with living at the forest edge

Of all the pests Gresham deals with, **ticks** are the one with the highest stakes, because they carry disease. The blacklegged tick — the deer tick — is established across this part of Wisconsin, and it transmits Lyme and, less commonly, anaplasmosis and other pathogens. What makes Gresham high-risk isn't bad luck; it's geography. Ticks don't live in the open, mowed lawn and they don't live deep in the closed forest canopy. They live in the **transition** — the leaf litter, tall grass, and brush where the two meet — and that transition zone is exactly what a Gresham property is made of. Every yard that backs onto woods has a tick edge.

The practical defense is layered. On the property side, it's about **shrinking the edge**: keeping the lawn short, clearing leaf litter and tall grass back from the house and play areas, creating a dry gravel or mulch buffer between lawn and woods, and treating those edge zones during the peak windows in late spring and again in fall. That's the core of a [tick and Lyme yard program](/guides/tick-lyme-yard-program/), and it measurably lowers how many ticks are waiting where your kids and dogs actually spend time. It doesn't replace personal checks after being in the woods, but it moves the odds in your favor, and in a town this wooded, the odds matter. The tick biology and identification are on the [ticks](/pest-library/ticks/) page.

## Building science on the Red River: why moisture matters here

River-corridor homes in Gresham fight one enemy the rest of the county fights less: **moisture**. Damp crawlspaces, humid basements, and wet sill areas are magnets for the pests that need water — carpenter ants that want softened wood, springtails and centipedes that want humidity, and the mold conditions that make a structure attractive in the first place. A wet basement isn't just a comfort problem; it's a pest-pressure problem.

That's why our approach to a river-adjacent Gresham home isn't only about killing what's there — it's about **drying the conditions** that keep drawing pests. Correcting grading and downspouts so water runs away from the foundation, improving crawlspace ventilation or sealing it properly, and fixing the small chronic leaks that soften interior wood all do more to keep carpenter ants and moisture pests out than any spray. Combine that with sealing the entry points, and the fix holds. This is the kind of building-science work a route tech from a national brand isn't set up to do, and it's exactly where a local company earns its keep.
