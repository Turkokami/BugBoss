## The quarry village in the lake belt

Redgranite is a small village in Waushara County that owes its name and its existence to the rock under it. In **1889** a Scottish immigrant named William Bannerman found deposits of mahogany-red granite here on what was then called Sand Prairie, and within a few years the place had a new name and a new industry. Incorporated as a village in **1904**, Redgranite boomed through the paving-block era of roughly **1905 to 1918**, drawing quarrymen and stonecutters from across the eastern states, Minnesota, and Europe — at its peak it was the largest village in the county, with close to two thousand people, a fifty-piece band, and first-rate schools. When concrete and asphalt took over the paving business around 1920, the boom faded and the population settled back to a few hundred. The old pit filled with water and became the **Redgranite Quarry Lake**, a swimming hole and local landmark, and the red granite Bannerman found became part of what people know as Wisconsin's state rock. Today Redgranite is a quiet lake-belt village — and that quiet, small-village character is exactly what shapes its pest picture.

Here's the honest part. **There's no dedicated pest-and-wildlife company based in Redgranite**, and for a village this size that's no surprise — small towns out here rarely have one. When a Redgranite homeowner gets bats in the attic or mice in the walls, the usual options are to wait on a national brand routing a truck in from the Fox Valley, or to fight it with hardware-store spray, and neither of those touches wildlife. ## The Redgranite pest calendar, season by season

Redgranite's sandy soils, nearby water, and older housing stock give the calendar a familiar lake-country shape.

### Spring (April–May): thaw and the first movement

When the quarry lake and the surrounding waters shed their ice and the sandy ground warms, the overwintering insects wake first. **Cluster flies** and **[boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/)** that spent the winter in the walls drift to sunny windows — not a new invasion, just the survivors of last fall. Seasonal cabins around the village get opened up now, and owners often find the mouse damage a quiet winter left behind.

Spring is also **carpenter ant** season. The damp ground near the lakes and the shaded, older village homes keep wood moist, which is what carpenter ants nest in. Winged swarmers appear at windows in April and May; large black ants indoors while it's still cold out mean a nest inside the heated structure rather than one foraging in from a tree. The full breakdown is on our [carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) page. Late spring brings the first **wasp** queens scouting eaves and soffits and the first active **ticks** along brushy and wooded edges.

### Summer (June–August): stinging insects and the bat window

Summer around Redgranite is warm and, near the water, humid — good conditions for stinging insects. Paper wasps build under overhangs, yellowjackets nest in the ground and in wall voids, and by August the colonies are large and defensive, which is when most of our [wasp and hornet](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) calls come in.

Summer is also the stretch when we **cannot** legally seal bats out. Wisconsin protects its bats, and the DNR keeps bat exits open **June 1 through July 31**, the maternity season, when flightless pups are in the roost. Redgranite's older homes and lake cabins are classic bat structures, and a colony found in July gets its exclusion planned for August — never sealed inside. We cover the timing on the [bats](/pest-library/bats/) page and in our [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/).

### Fall (September–October): the invasion, and closing up cabins

Fall is the defining season. As the nights cool, **cluster flies** and boxelder bugs mass on warm south and west walls and press into every gap toward wall voids and attics, and **[mice](/pest-library/mice/)** begin their push indoors from the sandy fields and fencerows — right as seasonal owners are getting ready to leave for the winter.

The cluster fly story is worth telling straight, because people around here take it personally. Their larvae **parasitize earthworms in the soil**, and the ground around Redgranite is full of them, so a home gets cluster flies because of where it sits, not how it's kept. The fix is an **early-fall exterior treatment** of the walls they gather on plus sealing entry points, not a spray can in January. Our [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/) has the method, and for seasonal homes our [cabin and seasonal pest control guide](/guides/cabin-seasonal-pest-control/) covers buttoning up before you close for the year.

Because winter is long here, fall is the highest-value time to get ahead of everything — a house sealed and treated in late September is protected through the whole cold stretch, while one left open collects insects and rodents right up until the hard freeze. For a village where so many homes sit empty part of the year, that fall window is not just the best time to act; on a seasonal place it's often the only practical time, since it's the last visit before the property closes up.

### Winter (November–March): the payoff and the empty cabins

Once winter sets in, the outdoor invasion is over and what's indoors is what got in during fall. Mice that found a warm nest breed year-round once inside, so a few in November become a colony by March — and on the seasonal cabins, no one is there to notice until spring. Deer mice out here can carry hantavirus, so the cleanup is a health matter and not something to dry-sweep on the first warm weekend of the year. On mild, bright days the south-wall voids warm and the fall cluster flies crawl back to the windows of an empty house. Winter work in Redgranite is mostly **rodent exclusion**, cleanup, and monitoring the seasonal homes so their owners aren't opening the door to a surprise in May. For the year-round village homes, it's the season when the problem becomes plain enough to pinpoint the entry and close it.

## Redgranite's housing and building stock

Pests exploit specific weaknesses, and Redgranite's stock has a clear set of them.

The **older village homes** — many dating to the quarry boom and the decades around it — tend to have fieldstone or block foundations, balloon framing, and a century of settlement gaps. Balloon-framed walls are open vertical shafts from basement to attic, which is how a mouse entering at the sill plate ends up in the attic and cluster flies entering at a soffit gap end up in an upstairs bedroom. These homes give an exclusion inspection the most to work with.

The **lake cabins and seasonal homes** around the village are the other big category. Many are older and lightly built, and they sit quiet for weeks at a time — a combination that lets mice, bats, and cluster flies establish undisturbed. Open soffits, unscreened gable vents, and gaps around utility penetrations are the usual ways in, and no one being home to catch it early is what turns small problems into big ones. Sealing tight and setting up monitoring is the whole game here.

Out past the village, the **farm and rural properties** on the sandy ground bring the full rodent and wildlife picture — older farmhouses, pole barns, and woodlot edges. Our [farmhouse rodent exclusion guide](/guides/farmhouse-rodent-exclusion/) covers those.

## The seasonal-cabin problem, up close

The single most common way a Redgranite-area pest problem gets out of hand is time — specifically, the weeks and months a seasonal cabin sits empty with no one to notice the early signs.
