## A tiny river village that sits almost in Clintonville's backyard

Embarrass is one of the smallest incorporated villages we serve — around 353 people — tucked along the **Embarrass River** on State Highway 22 between Clintonville and Shawano, in northern Waupaca County. The name is the local landmark all by itself. It comes from French-Canadian lumberjacks who worked this country in the logging era; *embarras* is French for something that impedes, obstructs, or entangles, and it's what those loggers called the river when snags, log jams, and debris made it a nightmare to float timber downstream. That history tells you what this place is: a small, old, close-knit community grown up around a river and surrounded by farmland and woods.

## The Embarrass pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): thaw and the first movers

When the snow melts and the river bottom thaws, Embarrass's pest year restarts. First out are the **overwintering insects** — cluster flies, boxelder bugs, and Asian lady beetles that spent the winter dormant in wall voids and attics, now crawling toward warm, sunny windows. Homeowners often think this is a fresh invasion; it isn't. It's the same population that got in the previous fall, and it's a signal the building needs sealing before the next autumn.

Spring is also when **carpenter ants** get active. The older wood-framed homes in the village, combined with the damp, wooded ground along the Embarrass River, give them the moisture-softened wood they excavate to nest in. Winged swarmers appear at windows in April and May, and large black ants show up indoors. Seeing them early, while it's still cold out, usually means the colony is nesting inside the heated structure rather than foraging in — the situation worth catching early before it costs you in repairs. See our [carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) page.

By late spring the first **wasp** and **hornet** queens are scouting nest sites, and **ticks** turn active anywhere Embarrass yards meet brush, tall grass, or the river's edge.

### Summer (June–August): stingers, mosquitoes, and the bat window

Summer brings the stinging insects. Paper wasps build under porch roofs and eaves, yellow jackets nest in the ground and in wall voids, and bald-faced hornets hang their gray paper nests in trees and on outbuildings. By August those colonies are at full strength and most defensive, and that's when [wasp and hornet](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) calls peak. The **Embarrass River** and the low, damp bottomland around the village keep [mosquito](/pest-library/mosquitoes/) pressure high all season, especially for riverside properties — a seasonal program makes summer yards usable again.

Summer is also the only stretch of the year we **cannot** seal bats out. Wisconsin protects its bats — several cave-bat species are state-threatened and the northern long-eared bat is federally endangered — and the DNR requires that bat exits stay open **June 1 through July 31**, when flightless pups are in the roost. Find bats in July, and the right move is to plan the exclusion for the legal window, not to seal them in. We cover the details on the [bats](/pest-library/bats/) page and in the [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/).

### Fall (September–October): the busiest season

Fall is the defining season in Embarrass, and it's what prompts most calls. As the nights cool and the surrounding fields are harvested, **fall invaders** — [cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/), [boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/), and [Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/) — mass on the sunny south and west walls and press into any crack they can find to overwinter in the walls and attic. At the same time, **mice** start moving indoors as their outdoor food and cover disappear with the harvest.

Cluster flies confuse a lot of people in a small village like this, so it's worth being clear: they don't come from garbage or dirty dishes. Their larvae parasitize earthworms in the soil, so any home surrounded by lawn or field grows its own local population regardless of housekeeping. In Embarrass, that's practically every lot. The fix is an **early-fall exterior treatment** on the walls they gather on, paired with sealing entry points, not a spray can indoors in midwinter. Our [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/) lays it out. Fall is the highest-value time to get ahead of everything, because what you seal in September protects you all winter.

### Winter (November–February): what got in stays in

By winter the outdoor invasion is finished, and what you've got indoors is what got in during fall. Mice that found a warm, food-rich nest have no reason to leave, and they breed year-round once inside — a couple in November becomes a real infestation by February if the gaps stay open. A mild spell in January or February warms the wall voids and coaxes the overwintering flies and beetles back to the windows. Winter is also travel-pest season, when holiday trips bring [bed bugs](/pest-library/bed-bugs/) home in luggage. Most Embarrass winter work is rodent exclusion, cleanup of the fall invaders, and handling whatever hitchhiked back from a trip.

## Housing and building stock in Embarrass

Embarrass runs heavily to **older homes and rural farmsteads**, and those are among the most pest-friendly structures we deal with. Century-old homes here often have fieldstone or block foundations with mortar cracked and settled over decades, and many were **balloon-framed** — the wall cavities run uninterrupted from the sill plate to the attic. That's the reason a mouse entering low at the foundation ends up in the attic insulation, and why cluster flies slipping in at a soffit gap surface in an upstairs bedroom. No building around Embarrass repays a patient exclusion inspection like one of these century-old, balloon-framed homes.

Out on the **rural and riverside properties** around the village, you get the full farm-country picture: pole barns, machine sheds, and grain storage that combine large, open access points with a steady feed supply — the toughest rodent environments anywhere. You can't seal a pole barn like a house, so the approach shifts to controlling the population with tamper-resistant stations and knocking it down before it migrates to the farmhouse. Riverside homes add a moisture dimension: damp basements and crawlspaces that draw carpenter ants, springtails, and other moisture pests. Our [farmhouse rodent exclusion guide](/guides/farmhouse-rodent-exclusion/) covers the rodent side in depth.

Embarrass's logging-era roots also left behind a stock of **old outbuildings, sheds, and root cellars** that tend to get overlooked until they become a problem. A century-old shed with a dirt floor, an unsealed root cellar, or a lean-to added onto a farmhouse decades ago is an open invitation to rodents and denning wildlife — the walls have gaps, nobody's watching them, and they often sit close enough to the house that whatever moves in eventually tests the home next. When we inspect an Embarrass property, these secondary structures get the same attention as the house, because clearing the farmhouse does little good if the shed twenty feet away is breeding the next wave of mice.

## Spiders and the incidental invaders

Spiders in Embarrass are usually a downstream problem, not the root one. Cellar and cobweb spiders colonize wherever insects are plentiful, and the fieldstone basements and damp crawlspaces in the village's older homes are prime real estate for them. Every cluster fly, gnat, and boxelder bug that works into the structure feeds the population, so a house carrying a heavy fall-invader load tends to grow a matching spider problem right behind it. Our approach to [spiders](/pest-library/spiders/) is to cut off the food and seal the gaps the prey used to get in — reduce the insects and the spider count drops on its own. We also handle the ordinary nuisance [ants](/pest-library/ants/) that trail in from foundation cracks in the warm months, which are a different problem from the structural carpenter ants above.

## Vacant and part-time homes are a special case here

Embarrass is a small, aging village, and like a lot of rural Wisconsin communities it has homes that sit empty for stretches — an inherited farmhouse waiting to sell, a property occupied only part of the year, a place between owners. **A quiet, undisturbed building is exactly what mice, squirrels, and bats want.** With no one coming and going, no lights, and no activity, rodents settle in and breed, wildlife moves into the attic unnoticed, and a small entry point that would have been caught in an occupied home goes unsealed for months. By the time someone opens the place up, a manageable problem has become an infestation with real cleanup attached. If you own a vacant or seasonal property in or around Embarrass, the smart move is an inspection and exclusion pass before the fall push — sealing the building envelope while it's empty is far cheaper than gutting soiled insulation later. ## Why store-bought products usually fail out here

Plenty of Embarrass homeowners try to handle it themselves first, and for a stray ant or a single wasp nest, a hardware-store product is fine. Where DIY breaks down is on the problems that actually drive calls here — cluster flies, mice, carpenter ants, and bats. A can of spray kills the flies you see at the window but never touches the thousands overwintering in the wall void, so they keep coming back on every warm day. Snap traps clear the mice already inside but do nothing about the dime-sized gaps letting in the next dozen. Over-the-counter ant sprays kill foragers and leave the parent nest untouched. And bats aren't a DIY pest at all — they're legally protected, and sealing a building at the wrong time of year traps pups inside and breaks state law. The value of a professional here isn't a stronger chemical; it's correctly identifying the pest, finding how it's getting in, and closing that off for good.

## Managing the outdoor season: mosquitoes and ticks along the river

For Embarrass properties near the river bottom, the outdoor pest season deserves its own plan. **Mosquitoes** breed in standing water, and the low, slow stretches of the Embarrass River, roadside ditches, old tires, clogged gutters, and any container that holds rain all produce them. A seasonal [mosquito](/pest-library/mosquitoes/) program treats the shaded resting vegetation where adults wait out the day and knocks back breeding sites, which is what actually makes a riverside yard usable in July. **Ticks** are the other outdoor concern, and they don't need standing water — they wait on vegetation in the transition zone where a mowed lawn meets brush, tall grass, or the river's edge. Keeping that edge trimmed, widening the buffer between lawn and brush, and treating the transition zone in late spring and again in early fall meaningfully lowers the number of ticks your family and pets pick up.
