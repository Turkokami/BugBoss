## A dairy-country village built on lumber, kraut, and the Embarrass River

Bear Creek is a village of about 427 people in the southwest corner of Outagamie County, sitting on the **Embarrass River** in the middle of some of the best dairy-farm country in the region. It started in 1850 as a lumber camp founded by Captain Welcome Hyde, incorporated in 1902 as the Village of Welcome, and took the name Bear Creek in 1915 to end the confusion between the village and the railroad station. The railroad that made the place — arriving in 1878 and running until 1982 — is long gone, but the town's other defining institution is still going strong: the sauerkraut plant that began as the Flanagan Brothers factory in 1900 and operates today as **GLK Foods**, described as the world's largest producer of sauerkraut. That combination — a compact old village, a major food-processing operation, and dairy farms in every direction — is exactly what shapes pest pressure in Bear Creek.

## The Bear Creek pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): thaw, swarmers, and the first movement

When the frost leaves the ground and the fields begin to dry, Bear Creek's pest year restarts. The first thing we see is the **overwintering insects waking up** — cluster flies, boxelder bugs, and Asian lady beetles that spent the winter dormant in wall voids and attics, now crawling toward warm, sunny windows. It looks like a new invasion but it isn't; it's the same population from the previous fall, and it's a sign the building needs sealing before the next autumn.

Spring is also **carpenter ant season**, and Bear Creek offers them everything they want. The mature hardwoods along the Embarrass River, the older wood-framed farmhouses with decades of small roof and plumbing leaks, and the porches and decks near damp soil all supply the moisture-softened wood carpenter ants tunnel into. Winged swarmers appear at windows in April and May, and large black ants show up indoors. When they turn up early, while it's still cold outside, that usually means the nest is inside the heated structure rather than foraging in from outdoors — the case worth catching before it costs you in structural repair. See our [carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) page.

By late spring the first **wasp** and **hornet** queens are scouting nest sites under eaves and in outbuildings, and **ticks** turn active along every edge where a Bear Creek lawn meets fencerow, woodlot, or river brush.

### Summer (June–August): stingers, mosquitoes, and the bat window

Summer is stinging-insect season. Paper wasps build under porch roofs and eaves, yellow jackets nest in the ground and in wall voids, and bald-faced hornets hang their gray paper nests in trees and on the sides of barns and sheds. By August those colonies are at full size and most defensive, and that's when our [wasp and hornet](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) calls peak. The **Embarrass River**, farm stock tanks, and low, damp field edges keep [mosquito](/pest-library/mosquitoes/) pressure up all season — worth a seasonal program if you host summer gatherings or work outdoors on the farm.

Summer is also the one stretch of the year we **cannot** seal bats out. Wisconsin protects its bats — four cave-bat species are state-threatened and the northern long-eared bat is federally endangered — and the DNR requires that bat exit points stay open **June 1 through July 31**, when flightless pups are in the roost. Find bats in a Bear Creek attic or barn in July, and the right move is to plan the exclusion for the legal window, not to seal the building and trap pups inside. We cover the timing on the [bats](/pest-library/bats/) page and in the [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/).

### Fall (September–October): the busiest season in Bear Creek

Fall is the defining season here and the reason most people first call. As the nights cool and the surrounding fields are harvested, two things happen at once. **Fall invaders** — [cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/), [boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/), and [Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/) — mass on the sunny south and west walls and press into any crack they can find to overwinter in the walls and attic. And **mice** begin their annual march indoors as the harvest strips away their outdoor food and cover.

Cluster flies get misunderstood everywhere, and Bear Creek is no exception, so it's worth stating plainly: they don't breed in garbage or dirty dishes. Their larvae parasitize earthworms in the soil, so any home surrounded by lawn, pasture, or field grows a local population no matter how clean the house is. In a village ringed by dairy ground, that's essentially every property. The effective fix is an **early-fall exterior treatment** of the walls they gather on, paired with sealing entry points, not a spray can indoors in January. Our [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/) has the full approach. Fall is the highest-value time to get ahead of everything, because what you seal in September pays off all winter.

### Winter (November–February): what got in stays in

By winter the outdoor invasion is over, and what you have indoors is what got in during fall. Mice that established a warm, food-rich nest have no reason to leave, and they breed year-round once inside — a couple in November becomes a real infestation by February if the gaps stay open. A warm, sunny day in January or February heats the wall cavities, and the overwintering flies and beetles work their way back to the windows. Winter is also travel-pest season, when holiday trips bring [bed bugs](/pest-library/bed-bugs/) home in luggage. Most Bear Creek winter work is rodent exclusion, cleanup of the fall invaders, and handling whatever hitchhiked back from a trip.

## Bear Creek's housing and building stock

Pests exploit specific weaknesses, and Bear Creek's building stock has a particular set.

The **older farmhouses** in the village and across the surrounding township are the most pest-friendly structures we work on. Many have fieldstone or block foundations with mortar cracked and settled over a century, and a lot were **balloon-framed**, meaning the wall cavities run uninterrupted from the sill plate to the attic. That's how a mouse entering at the foundation ends up nesting in the attic insulation, and how cluster flies slipping in at a soffit gap turn up in an upstairs bedroom. Of every structure in Bear Creek, these old balloon-framed farmhouses give an exclusion inspection the most to work with.

The **village homes** on Bear Creek's residential streets are tighter but still have the usual weak points — garage-door corners, dryer-vent and utility penetrations, gaps where siding meets the foundation, and soffit and fascia junctions that let in squirrels, bats, and fall invaders.

And then there are the **dairy barns, milk houses, feed rooms, and pole barns** that define this landscape. These are the toughest rodent environments anywhere, because they combine large, deliberately open access points with a constant food supply. You can't seal a dairy barn like a house, so the strategy shifts to controlling the population with a system of tamper-resistant stations placed with livestock and feed in mind, and knocking the numbers down before they migrate to the farmhouse. Our [farmhouse rodent exclusion guide](/guides/farmhouse-rodent-exclusion/) and [dairy farm pest control guide](/guides/dairy-farm-pest-control/) cover both sides in detail.

## Geography and ecology: the Embarrass River and the dairy country

The **Embarrass River** running through Bear Creek is a genuine pest driver. River-adjacent and low-lying properties see more mosquitoes, damper basements and crawlspaces (which invite carpenter ants, springtails, and other moisture pests), and more rodent travel along the natural cover the corridor provides. The mature trees lining the river give carpenter ants their outdoor parent nests and hand squirrels and raccoons a highway to the roofline.

The **dairy farmland and woodlots** wrapped around the village are the reservoir for the fall invaders and rodents that show up every autumn. Cluster flies rise from the field soil; field mice and deer mice pour out of the harvested rows and fencerows; ticks live in the brushy transition zones between mowed lawn and standing brush or timber. On a farm property, deer mice are worth taking seriously as the classic hantavirus carrier — one more reason rodent work in Bear Creek is about sealing and cleanup, not just setting a few traps. ## Stored-product pests and the food-safety picture

In a food-production town, the pest conversation goes beyond the ones you see crawling on a wall. **Stored-product pests** — the small beetles and moths that infest grain, flour, dry ingredients, and pet and livestock feed — are a constant concern anywhere product is warehoused, and they matter both at commercial scale and on the farm. Indian meal moths and various grain and flour beetles ride in with incoming ingredients, breed inside sealed packaging, and are notoriously hard to knock out once established because the life stages hide inside the product itself. Controlling them means inspection and monitoring at receiving, rotating stock, pheromone trapping to find the source, and sanitation in the dead spots where spilled product accumulates — not just spraying shelves. For any operation that stores grain or feed around Bear Creek, this is worth building into the program before an audit or a customer complaint forces the issue. ## Spiders and the incidental invaders

Spiders in Bear Creek are usually a downstream symptom rather than the root problem. Cellar and cobweb spiders move in wherever insects are plentiful, and the fieldstone basements, damp crawlspaces, and cluttered barns and sheds here are ideal for them. Every cluster fly, gnat, and boxelder bug that gets into a structure feeds the population, so a building with a heavy fall-invader load grows a matching spider problem right behind it. Our approach to [spiders](/pest-library/spiders/) starts with cutting off the food supply and sealing the same gaps the prey used — reduce the insects and the spider numbers fall on their own. We also handle the ordinary nuisance [ants](/pest-library/ants/) that trail in from foundation cracks in warm months, a separate matter from the structural carpenter ants covered above.

## Getting ahead of it: exclusion on a Bear Creek farmstead

On a Bear Creek dairy operation, exclusion works best when you treat the whole property as a connected system rather than one building at a time. Rodents follow feed and warmth, and they move freely between the barn, the milk house, the feed room, the machine sheds, and the farmhouse along fencerows, foundations, and buried utility lines. The strategy, then, is layered: a perimeter of tamper-resistant bait stations positioned with livestock and feed safety in mind to intercept mice before they reach the buildings; tight sealing on the farmhouse itself, where you actually can close the envelope — foundation-to-siding gaps, sill plates, utility penetrations, soffit and gable vents, and door sweeps; and sanitation discipline around spilled feed, which is the fuel the whole population runs on. You can't seal a dairy barn the way you seal a house, so the goal there is to cut the obvious openings, keep feed contained, and hold the numbers down so the pressure never builds enough to push into the home. For a food or feed operation, that same perimeter thinking extends to the plant walls and dock doors. It's methodical work, and it's the part that separates a lasting result from a treatment that has to be repeated every season.
