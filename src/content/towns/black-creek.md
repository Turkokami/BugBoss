## A small dairy village built on a dark-water creek

Black Creek is a small Outagamie County village of about **1,357 people**, sitting in the dairy country roughly 20 miles west of Green Bay and 15 miles north of the Fox Cities, at around **804 feet** of elevation. It takes its name from the dark-colored creek that runs along the edge of town. The place was settled in **1862** by a Civil War veteran, Thomas J. Burdick, and his son Abraham; it was first called Middleburg and was **renamed and incorporated as Black Creek in 1904**. Two odd, true footnotes tell you the character of the town: the country's first organized baseball team was formed here in 1869 — the village calls itself the Birthplace of the First Organized Baseball Team — and for a century, from the late 1880s into the mid-1990s, this area was the **nation's leader in sauerkraut production**.

Behind those footnotes is the real story, which is dairy and food processing. Black Creek had a **creamery by 1894** and a **Borden's condensery by 1917**, and it remains a working dairy-and-processing town today. That heritage is the single most useful fact for a pest control company, because dairy, feed, grain, and food processing are exactly the things that drive rodent and fly pressure. Where there are cows, feed, and milk, there are flies and mice, in numbers a strictly residential town never sees.

## The Black Creek pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): thaw, swarmers, and the return of fieldwork

When the ground warms in Black Creek, the **[cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/)**, **[boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/)**, and **[Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/)** that overwintered in the walls wake up and crawl toward the windows on warm days — the same fall population resurfacing, not a new arrival. Spring is also when **[mice](/pest-library/mice/)** that wintered indoors are breeding and most active, and when the farm year restarts: fieldwork, calving, and feed handling all lift rodent and fly pressure around barns.

Spring is **[carpenter ant](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/)** season, and Black Creek's older homes and farm buildings offer everything they want — slow roof and plumbing leaks, damp sills, and wood posts and decks in contact with soil. Winged swarmers appear near windows in April and May; large black ants indoors while it's still cold out point to a nest living inside the heated structure. By late spring the first **[wasp](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/)** queens are scouting eaves, and **[ticks](/pest-library/ticks/)** are active along the creek, the fencerows, and the pasture edges.

### Summer (June–August): barn flies, mosquitoes, and stinging insects

Summer is peak fly season in a dairy town. Barn and stable flies breed in manure and wet bedding, and fly pressure around a Black Creek dairy in July and August is a herd-comfort and productivity issue, not just an annoyance. **[Mosquitoes](/pest-library/mosquitoes/)** breed in the creek corridor and the low, damp spots around town and farmsteads. And **[wasps](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/)** — paper wasps under eaves, yellow jackets in the ground and in wall voids — build all summer and reach their largest, most defensive size by August, which is when most stings happen.

Summer is also the one stretch when bats cannot legally be sealed out. Wisconsin protects its bats, and the DNR requires exclusion exits stay open **June 1 through July 31** during the maternity season, when flightless pups are in the roost. If you find bats in a Black Creek attic or barn in July, plan the exclusion for after the window rather than sealing the building and trapping pups inside. See the [bats](/pest-library/bats/) page and the [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/).

### Fall (September–October): the invasion from the fields

Fall is the defining season for Black Creek pest control, and in dairy country it comes on strong. As nights cool, the **fall invaders** — cluster flies, boxelder bugs, Asian lady beetles — mass on warm south and west walls and push into every gap toward the wall voids and attic. As the fields are harvested, **field mice and deer mice** pour out of the stubble and fencerows toward the nearest warm building — farmhouse, barn, or shed.

Cluster flies frustrate Black Creek farm families in particular, because a spotless farmhouse gets them as badly as a run-down one. The reason is biology: cluster fly larvae **parasitize earthworms in the soil**, so a house ringed by field and pasture grows its own local population no matter how clean it is. The fix is an **early-fall exterior treatment** of the walls they gather on plus sealing the entry points — done in September, before they get in. We cover the whole approach in the [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/), and the rodent side in the [farmhouse rodent exclusion guide](/guides/farmhouse-rodent-exclusion/).

### Winter (November–February): what's already inside, and the barn

By winter the outdoor invasion is over and what you have indoors is what got in during fall. Mice that found a warm nest breed year-round once inside — a handful in November becomes an infestation by February if the entry points aren't closed. On a farm the reservoir outside never empties, so barns and outbuildings can become rodent cities over the cold months if they aren't managed. A run of warm January sun on a south wall is enough to rouse the cluster flies wintering in the void behind it and set them tapping at the glass. Winter work in Black Creek is mostly rodent exclusion, cleaning up the fall invaders, and keeping farm buildings under control.

## Farmhouses, village homes, and farm buildings: where they let pests in

Black Creek's building stock runs from tidy village homes to century farmhouses to big open ag and processing buildings, and each has its own weaknesses.

The **older farmhouses** are the classic challenge: fieldstone or block foundations, decades of settlement gaps, and in the oldest of them balloon framing that turns the wall cavities into open highways from basement to attic. A mouse entering at the sill plate ends up in the attic; cluster flies entering at a soffit gap end up in an upstairs bedroom. Of every building type around Black Creek, these century farmhouses give back the most for a patient, gap-by-gap exclusion inspection.

The **village homes** in town are tighter but still have the usual weak points — garage-door corners, utility and dryer-vent penetrations, gaps where siding meets foundation, and soffit and fascia junctions that let in fall invaders, bats, and **[squirrels](/pest-library/squirrels/)**.

The **farm and processing buildings** — dairy barns, milkhouses, machine sheds, corn cribs, pole buildings, and food-processing floors — are a category of their own. They can't be sealed like a house, so the strategy shifts: control food and harborage, exclude what can be excluded, and manage rodents and flies at the source, with the documentation a processing operation needs. ## Spiders, ants, and the everyday pests

Between the big seasonal invasions, Black Creek homes and buildings collect the steady background pests. **[Spiders](/pest-library/spiders/)** turn up wherever there's an insect food supply — around barn and yard lights, in basements and machine sheds, and in the quiet corners of any building — and a heavy spider population is really a sign of the insects they're feeding on. Treating the perimeter and cutting off that food source does more than sweeping down webs. **[Ants](/pest-library/ants/)**, beyond the wood-damaging carpenter ants, appear as the small nuisance trails that follow moisture and crumbs across kitchens and along foundations, worst in spring and after heavy rain when outdoor colonies push indoors. Both use the same gaps as the seasonal pests, so we handle them as one perimeter problem rather than a string of separate visits.

Wildlife work in Black Creek isn't limited to houses. ## Reading the signs: when to call in Black Creek

A little knowledge of what you're seeing goes a long way on a farm or in an older village home. **Fine, sawdust-like shavings under a beam or windowsill**, and large black ants indoors during winter, point to a carpenter-ant nest living inside the wood. **Rice-grain droppings along walls and feed bags, gnaw marks, and a musky smell** in a granary or pantry mean rodents are established. **Flies massing at upstairs windows on a warm winter day** are overwintering cluster flies waking in the wall voids, not a drain issue. **A dark stain and ammonia odor on an attic ceiling with droppings below a gable vent** suggest a bat colony. And **greasy rub marks along a barn sill** trace the runways rodents travel every night.

They all share one lesson: what shows up indoors is a symptom of a source out of sight, and it compounds with time — carpenter ants chew through structural wood, rodent numbers double, bat colonies grow and foul an attic. The value is in catching it early and treating the source, not the symptom. When you notice any of these on a Black Creek home, farm, or processing building, that's the moment to call rather than wait.
