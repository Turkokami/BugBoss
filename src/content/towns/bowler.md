## The Bowler pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): thaw, swarmers, and the first ticks

When the snow melts, Bowler's overwintering pests move first. The **cluster flies and Asian lady beetles** that spent winter in wall voids crawl to the windows on warm days, and **mice** that wintered indoors get active and begin breeding.

Spring is peak **carpenter ant** season, and few towns give carpenter ants more to work with than Bowler. The surrounding forest is full of outdoor parent colonies, and older homes with small roof or plumbing leaks provide the damp interior wood satellite nests need. Winged swarmers at a window in April, or large black ants indoors while it's still cold outside, mean a nest is living inside the heated structure — and in a wooded area, that's a common find. Our [carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) page explains how to tell a foraging trail from an established indoor nest.

Late spring is when **ticks** switch on. In country this wooded, blacklegged ticks wait along every clearing edge and trail, and Lyme risk climbs for people and pets alike. Spring is the time to start a [tick and Lyme yard program](/guides/tick-lyme-yard-program/) — before the grass is tall and deer are moving the ticks through the yard.

### Summer (June–August): stinging insects, woodland mosquitoes, and bats

Summer in Bowler is stinging-insect and mosquito season. **Paper wasps** build under eaves, **yellow jackets** nest in the ground and in wall voids, and **bald-faced hornets** hang paper nests in trees and shrubs. By August those colonies are at full size and most defensive, which is when the [wasp and hornet](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) calls come in and most stings happen.

Wooded lots hold **mosquitoes** even in dry spells, because the shaded resting vegetation stays cool and humid and the woodland pools and low ground provide breeding water. A seasonal [mosquito](/pest-library/mosquitoes/) program that treats that foliage and eliminates standing-water sites around the yard is what makes an evening outdoors possible in July.

Summer is the one time of year we **cannot** seal bats out. Wisconsin keeps bat exits open **June 1 through July 31** for the maternity season, when flightless pups are in the roost. Find bats in a Bowler attic in July, and the right move is to schedule the exclusion for August — never trap pups inside, which is both illegal and a worse mess.

### Fall (September–October): the busiest stretch

Fall is the defining season and the reason most Bowler homeowners first call. As nights cool, the **fall invaders** — [cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/), [boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/), and [Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/) — mass on warm south and west walls and work into soffit, fascia, and eave gaps to overwinter in the wall voids and attic.

Cluster flies deserve a special note, because people take them personally. Their larvae parasitize earthworms in the soil, so any property with lawn, field, or woods around it produces its own local population no matter how clean the house is — and in Bowler, that's every property. The fix is an **early-fall exterior treatment** of the walls they gather on, paired with sealing entry points, not a can of spray indoors in winter.

At the same time, **mice** push indoors as the woods and fields go cold, and deer mice out of the surrounding forest are a specific hantavirus concern on rural properties. Fall is the highest-value time to get ahead of all of it — everything you seal and treat in September pays off across the entire winter.

### Winter (November–February): what's already inside

By winter the outdoor invasion is done and what you have indoors is what got in during fall. **Mice** that found a warm, food-rich nest breed year-round once inside, so a couple in November becomes a real infestation by February if the entry points aren't sealed. **Overwintering cluster flies and lady beetles** wake on a warm day and drift to the windows of a forest-ringed home. And holiday and casino travel is exactly how **bed bugs** hitchhike home in luggage — a pest tied to travel, not cleanliness, and one that rebounds fast if it's only partly treated. Winter work in Bowler is mostly rodent exclusion, fall-invader cleanup, and dealing with travel pests.

## Housing and building stock in Bowler

Bowler's structures are a rural-and-wooded mix, and each type fails differently.

The **older village and farm homes** tend to have stone, rubble, or block foundations, balloon framing, and decades of small settlement gaps. Balloon-framed walls are open vertical runs from basement to attic — the path a mouse takes from the sill plate to the attic, and the route cluster flies take from a soffit gap to an upstairs room. In a village ringed this tightly by forest, these balloon-framed homes justify a careful, seam-by-seam exclusion inspection above any other approach.

The **rural and forest-adjacent properties** are the ones that see the full Northwoods load: pole barns, outbuildings, cabins, and homes surrounded by trees, with heavy carpenter ant, rodent, tick, and wildlife pressure and lots of roofline access from overhanging limbs.

## Bed bugs and travel pests: the hospitality-town reality

A town with a casino resort and hotel has a pest exposure most small villages don't: **bed bugs and other travel pests** that ride in on luggage. This is worth stating plainly because it's so misunderstood. Bed bugs are not a cleanliness problem and not a sign of a dirty building; they are hitchhikers, and they turn up anywhere people arrive with bags — hotels, homes that host overnight guests, and vehicles. The busier the visitor traffic, the higher the baseline exposure, which is simply the trade-off of being a destination.

For a lodging operation, the answer is **proactive monitoring and fast, thorough response**, because bed bugs spread room to room and a single ignored complaint becomes a reputation problem. For a Bowler homeowner who visits or hosts, the answer is awareness — inspect luggage and secondhand furniture, and don't try to treat a confirmed infestation with store spray, because partial treatment scatters the population and lets it rebound. Bed bugs are one of the few pests where DIY almost always makes the problem worse. ## Wooded-property wildlife: the signature Bowler job

Deep woods and older buildings make **wildlife** the defining job in Bowler. Overhanging limbs give squirrels and raccoons a bridge straight to the roofline, where a weak soffit return or a rotted fascia board is all they need. **Squirrels** gnaw entry holes and nest in attics, chewing wiring as they go — a genuine fire risk. **Raccoons** are strong enough to tear open a soffit or lift a loose vent, and a female will do exactly that to make a den for her spring litter; a raccoon latrine in an attic is also a real health-hazard cleanup. **Skunks** exploit the space under porches, decks, sheds, and outbuildings.

The mistake people make is trapping the animal and calling it done. Remove one raccoon from an attic and leave the hole, and another moves in within the season, because the building is still advertising a den site. The fix is **eviction plus exclusion plus cleanup**: get the animal out, seal the entry and reinforce the weak points, and clean and treat the contamination.
