## A Wolf River village where the water sets the terms

Shiocton is a village of about **940 people** in Outagamie County, wrapped entirely by the Town of Bovina and built where the **Shioc River** empties into the **Wolf River**. The Menominee called this stretch *Māēnomehsāyak* — "wild rice along the banks" — before the Treaty of the Cedars in 1836, and the water is still what defines the place. Every spring the Wolf River draws crowds for one of Wisconsin's great natural spectacles: **lake sturgeon** run up out of the Winnebago system to spawn, and the village's **Bamboo Bend** site is one of the best public spots in the state to watch prehistoric fish thrash in the shallows. In heavy-water years those same spring floods put the river over its banks — in April 2026, high water pushed spawning sturgeon into flooded yards and the village went under an **evacuation order**.

That is not background color. It's the core of how pest control works in Shiocton, and it's why **BugBoss The X-Terminator** treats river towns differently from farm villages or lake communities. Water drives the pest picture here in ways it doesn't anywhere else in our territory. Standing and slow water breeds mosquitoes by the acre. Flooding saturates foundations, crawlspaces, and wood, leaving behind exactly the moisture that **carpenter ants** and moisture pests need for months afterward — and driving rodents up out of the low ground and into houses. The recreational traffic that the river pulls in — the spring walleye run, the sturgeon season, fall deer and waterfowl — fills cabins, cottages, and rental camps that sit empty between uses and quietly collect pests while nobody's looking.

We're based just down the road in Clintonville at **600 S. Main St.**, close enough that Shiocton is core territory, not a long-haul call. And because we do **both pest control and wildlife removal**, a river homeowner dealing with cluster flies inside and bats in the eaves — or a flooded-out property that now has mice and a damp, ant-friendly crawlspace — gets it all handled by one company that actually knows the ground.

## The Shiocton pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): high water, and everything it stirs up

Spring in Shiocton runs on the river. As the snowpack upstream melts and the Wolf swells, the sturgeon run brings crowds to Bamboo Bend — and in high-water years, the flooding brings problems. **Flood water drives rodents** out of burrows and low ground straight toward buildings, so mouse and rat pressure spikes during and right after a rise. Standing floodwater in yards, ditches, and backwaters becomes a mosquito nursery as it warms. And every basement, crawlspace, and sill plate that took on water is now carrying the moisture that **carpenter ants** and springtails move toward.

Spring is also carpenter ant swarm season generally. River-adjacent Shiocton homes are ideal habitat — damp crawlspaces, decks and docks near the water, and any wood that's stayed wet. Winged **swarmers** at the windows in April and May, or large black ants indoors while it's still cold out, point to a nest living inside the heated structure. Catch it early and you save serious wood repair. The details are on our [carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) page. By late spring **ticks** are active along the floodplain brush and trail edges, and the first **wasp** queens are scouting eaves and dock structures.

### Summer (June–August): mosquitoes own the season

If Shiocton has a signature pest, it's the **mosquito**, and summer is when the river collects on its debt. The Wolf, the Shioc, the backwaters, the low wet ground, and any water left standing after a spring flood all feed the breeding cycle, and riverfront yards can be nearly unusable at dusk without help. A seasonal [mosquito](/pest-library/mosquitoes/) program that treats the shaded resting vegetation where adults wait out the day and knocks back the breeding sites is the difference between using your riverfront in July and surrendering it.

Summer is also stinging-insect season — paper wasps and yellow jackets peak and turn defensive in August, which is when most [wasp and hornet](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) stings and calls happen — and it's the one stretch we **cannot** seal bats out. Wisconsin protects its bats, and the DNR keeps one-way exits open **June 1 through July 31** during the maternity season, when flightless pups are in the roost. Find bats in your Shiocton attic or cabin eave in July and the move is to schedule exclusion for after the window, not seal the building and trap pups inside. The timing is on the [bats](/pest-library/bats/) page and in our [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/).

### Fall (September–October): field invaders meet river homes

Once the river quiets down, Shiocton's fall looks like the rest of the region's — because beyond the water, the village is ringed by **farmland**. As the fields go cold, [cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/), [boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/), and [Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/) mass on warm south and west walls and push into wall voids and attics to overwinter, while **mice** make their annual move indoors. Cluster flies are the standout: their larvae parasitize earthworms in the field soil, so any Shiocton home surrounded by open ground produces them no matter how clean it is. The fix is an **early-fall exterior treatment** plus sealing entry points — laid out in our [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/) — not a can of spray at the January window.

Fall is also when the hunting cabins and river cottages start their seasonal cycle. A cottage closed up after Labor Day and reopened for deer season is exactly the kind of property that collects mice and ladybugs unnoticed, which is why we push seasonal inspections on recreational properties.

### Winter (November–February): the payoff and the empty cabins

By winter the outdoor invasion is over. **Mice** that found a warm nest breed year-round indoors — a few in November become an infestation by February if the gaps stay open. A clear, thawing winter day is enough to rouse the cluster flies wintering in a river-home wall and draw them to the sunlit windows. And the seasonal cabins and rentals that sit empty and unheated all winter are prime for undetected rodent and wildlife establishment — which is why our [cabin seasonal pest control guide](/guides/cabin-seasonal-pest-control/) treats the close-up and open-up inspections as the whole ballgame. Winter is also **bed bug** season, moving with holiday travel and rental turnover.

## River homes, cabins, and older village stock: where pests get in

Shiocton's housing tells its own story. The **older village and river homes** — many dating to the community's roots as Jordan's Landing — tend to have stone or block foundations, balloon framing, and generations of settlement gaps, plus the added burden of repeated flood exposure. Balloon-framed walls are open vertical runs from the sill plate to the attic, so a mouse entering low ends up nesting high, and a cluster fly at a soffit gap turns up in an upstairs room. Flood history compounds it: waterlogged sill plates and rim joists rot into soft, ant-friendly wood.

The **seasonal cabins and river cottages** are a category of their own. They sit empty for months, often unheated, frequently on crawlspaces or piers close to the water. That combination — no occupant, easy access, and constant moisture — is a standing invitation to mice, cluster flies, ladybugs, bats, and raccoons. The single most effective thing a cabin owner can do is have the building **sealed and inspected** on a schedule tied to how it's used.

And the **newer year-round homes** on higher ground are tighter but still leak at the usual points: garage corners, dryer and utility penetrations, siding-to-foundation gaps, and soffit-and-fascia junctions that let squirrels, bats, and fall invaders in at the roof.

Layered on top of the river is the surrounding **farmland**, which supplies the fall cluster flies and rodents the same way it does in any of the region's ag villages. ## Wildlife in Shiocton: attics, cabins, and the law

Between the older homes, the seasonal cabins, and the wooded river corridor, **wildlife work** is a steady part of what we do in Shiocton. **Bats** roost in attics, cabin eaves, and behind fascia. **Raccoons** den in chimneys and torn soffits and are drawn to riverside properties and the cover the corridor provides — especially females raising young in spring. **Squirrels** chew into attics at weak roof edges. **Skunks** den under decks, cabins, and outbuildings.

## Beyond mosquitoes: the rest of the Shiocton pest picture

The river makes mosquitoes the headline, but a Wolf River property collects a wider mix, and several of them trace straight back to the water.

**Moisture pests** are the quiet story of river living. Springtails, sowbugs, pillbugs, and centipedes all need damp conditions, and a Shiocton crawlspace or basement that's taken on flood water or sits in high ground moisture keeps them supplied year-round. They're mostly a nuisance rather than a hazard, but they're also a signal: if these are showing up indoors, the building has a moisture problem that's also feeding [carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) and, over time, wood decay. Fixing the moisture matters more than spraying the bugs.

**Spiders** thrive on riverfront lots because the water and lights pull in a heavy insect supply for them to feed on. Docks, boathouses, porches, and eaves near the water collect webs fast. Spiders follow their food, so the real control is knocking down the flying-insect population they're living on — an exterior program does more than sweeping webs ever will. See the [spiders](/pest-library/spiders/) page.

**Deer mice** matter on the rural and cabin properties around Shiocton the same way they do across farm country — they come out of the fields and floodplain, they carry **hantavirus** concern, and they establish in seldom-used cabins, campers, and boats. Flood events push them out of low ground and concentrate the pressure. The [mice](/pest-library/mice/) page has the details.

**Ticks** are a genuine concern given how much brushy floodplain and trail edge surrounds the village. The **Newton Blackmour State Trail** and the wooded riverbank are exactly where deer ticks wait, and Lyme disease is a real risk for anyone hunting, fishing, hiking, or just mowing near the edge. A perimeter [tick and Lyme yard program](/guides/tick-lyme-yard-program/) targets that habitat.

And the **raccoons, [squirrels](/pest-library/squirrels/), and [skunks](/pest-library/skunks/)** drawn to the river corridor and its cover aren't spray problems — they're exclusion and trapping problems. Raccoons in particular gravitate to waterfront properties and to the older homes and cabins with the torn soffits and open chimneys they love to den in. Getting them out and keeping them out is wildlife work, not pest work, and it's a big part of what we do here.
