## Working-farm country, from the fields to the machine shed

Its center of gravity is the hamlet of **Symco**, a small unincorporated crossroads community, and the town's identity is stitched tightly to farming: Symco is home to the long-running **Union Thresheree**, an annual gathering that keeps antique farm machinery, steam threshing, and homesteading skills alive every summer. ## The Union pest calendar, season by season

### Spring (March–May): thaw, swarmers, and rodents on the move

When the frost leaves the ground, the mice that wintered in the barn, the feed room, and the farmhouse start moving and breeding, and the overwintering insects wake. Farmers who figured their fall [cluster fly](/pest-library/cluster-flies/) problem was over find the flies back at the windows on the first warm April day — the same population that never left the wall voids.

Spring is also **carpenter ant** season. Union's old farmhouses collect the conditions [carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) want: years of small roof and gutter leaks, damp sill plates, and mature yard trees for the outdoor parent nest. Winged swarmers appear at south windows in April and May, and big black ants indoors while it's still cold usually means a nest is living inside the heated house. Early is cheap; late means wood repair.

By late spring the first **wasp** queens are scouting eaves, soffits, and the open rafters of sheds and pole barns, and **ticks** turn active along every fencerow and woodlot edge.

### Summer (June–August): flies, grain pests, stinging insects, and the bat window

Summer on a Union farm runs on flies and stinging insects. Livestock operations fight **stable flies and house flies** breeding in manure, wet bedding, and spilled feed — a nuisance and a real drag on a dairy herd. Around the buildings, paper wasps, yellowjackets, and bald-faced hornets build to full, defensive size by August, which is when most stings happen and most of our [wasp and hornet](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) calls come in.

Summer is also when **stored-grain pests** get busy in warm bins and feed rooms. Indian meal moths, saw-toothed grain beetles, and weevils breed fastest in summer heat, and they don't respect the property line between the grain bin and the farmhouse pantry. Catching an infestation in the storage before it migrates into the house is far easier than chasing moths through the kitchen in October.

Summer is the one stretch when we **cannot** seal bats out. Wisconsin protects its bats, and the DNR keeps roost exits legally open **June 1 through July 31** while flightless pups are present. Find [bats](/pest-library/bats/) in a Union farmhouse attic or barn peak in July, and the move is to schedule the exclusion for after the window, not to seal the building with pups inside. The timing is in our [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/).

### Fall (September–October): the busiest season out here

Fall is when the farm pushes its pests indoors, and it's when most Union homeowners call. As the harvest wraps and the fields go cold, **mice** and deer mice pour out of the stubble, fencerows, and machinery toward the warmth of the barn, the feed room, and the house. And the **fall invaders** — cluster flies, [boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/), and [Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/) — mass on the sunny south and west walls and push into every gap, headed for the wall voids and attic to overwinter.

Cluster flies are worth understanding, because farm families take them as a housekeeping failure when they aren't one. The larvae parasitize **earthworms** in the soil, so a farmhouse ringed by pasture, lawn, and cropland grows a population every year regardless of how clean the house is. The fix is an **early-fall exterior treatment** of the walls where they gather, paired with sealing entry points. Everything you seal in September pays off all winter — which is exactly why fall is the highest-value time to get ahead of both the rodents and the flies.

### Winter (November–February): whatever got in is what you've got

By winter the outdoor invasion is done and the indoor situation is set. [Mice](/pest-library/mice/) that found a warm, feed-rich nest breed year-round once inside, so a handful in November becomes a real infestation by February if the entry points stay open — and a farm gives them all the food they could want. A warm winter afternoon heats the wall voids and coaxes the overwintering cluster flies and lady beetles back out to the windows. Winter work in Union is mostly **rodent exclusion and knockdown**, fall-invader cleanup, and protecting stored grain and feed from the rodents and stored-product pests that would otherwise winter in it.

## Farm buildings, grain storage, and farmhouses: where pests get in

Pests exploit specific weaknesses, and Union's building stock — grain and feed storage, machine sheds, dairy barns, and old farmhouses — has a distinct set of them.

The **grain bins and feed storage** are the beating heart of the rodent and stored-product problem. A bin that isn't cleaned out between fills leaves residual grain that seeds the next infestation, and gaps at the base of a bin or the door of a feed room let mice in to a food supply that never runs out. Real control here is about **sanitation and exclusion at the storage itself** — cleaning out residual product, sealing the access points, and monitoring — before it's about anything else.

The **pole barns and machine sheds** are rarely tight to begin with: big sliding doors, open eaves, gaps at the base of steel siding. You can't make them mouse-proof, so the goal is to protect the zones that matter — feed, grain, and the milkhouse — and to keep the perimeter baited so rodents get caught before they reach the house. Machinery that sits parked over winter, including the antique equipment this town is known for, makes prime rodent harborage, and a mouse nest in a wiring harness is an expensive surprise come spring.

The **farmhouses** skew old, with fieldstone or block foundations, unsealed sill plates, and in the oldest homes, balloon framing — an open vertical cavity from basement to attic that lets a mouse entering at the foundation nest in the attic, and lets cluster flies entering at a soffit end up upstairs. On an old Union farmhouse a careful exclusion inspection is worth more than any number of spray visits. Our [farmhouse rodent exclusion guide](/guides/farmhouse-rodent-exclusion/) covers the specifics.

## Stored-product pests: the farm's quiet infestation

The pest problem that catches Union farm families off guard is the one that starts in the grain and ends in the kitchen. **Stored-product pests** — Indian meal moths, sawtoothed and merchant grain beetles, granary and rice weevils — breed in stored grain, feed, birdseed, pet food, and dry pantry goods, and they move freely between the farm's bulk storage and the house. An Indian meal moth infestation is the classic example: you notice the adult moths flying in loose, drifting flight near the ceiling, follow them back, and find webbing and larvae in a bag of feed, a forgotten sack of birdseed, or the flour in the pantry. Warmth speeds the whole lifecycle, so a warm bin or a heated pantry can turn out generation after generation year-round.

Control isn't about spraying. It's about **finding and removing the source** — the infested bag or bin — then cleaning out the residue in the cracks and seams where larvae pupate, and sealing storage so reinfestation doesn't happen. On a farm that means treating the grain storage and the pantry as one connected problem, because clearing the kitchen while leaving an infested feed bin in the shed just resets the clock. This is a different job than nuisance-ant or fall-fly work, and it needs a different eye.

## Antique machinery, stored textiles, and the collector's pests

Union is a town that keeps its old equipment, and stored machinery and collections bring pests of their own. **Parked machinery** — whether a modern combine or a restored steam tractor — makes prime rodent harborage over winter, and a mouse nest in a wiring harness, a seat, or an air cleaner is an expensive, smelly surprise come spring. Beyond rodents, the upholstered seats, canvas, leather, and stored fabric that come with old equipment and homesteading collections draw **fabric pests**: clothes moths and carpet beetles whose larvae feed on wool, felt, hair, and other natural fibers, quietly chewing through seat cushions, tarps, and stored textiles in a shed nobody heats. If you store equipment or collections, keeping the rodents out and monitoring for fabric pests protects real value — and it's the kind of problem a suburban route tech never has to think about.

## Geography and ecology: fields, fencerows, and low ground

Union's pest pressure comes straight off its landscape. The **cropland and pasture** are the reservoir for cluster flies and for the mice and deer mice that flood in every fall. The **fencerows and woodlots** hold the ticks, the carpenter ant parent nests, and the wildlife that follows the cover onto your buildings. **Low field edges and ditches** breed the mosquitoes that make evening chores miserable. Deer mice, common on farms, can carry hantavirus, which is why cleaning out a rodent-infested bin or shed without precautions is a genuine health risk and part of why we handle rodent cleanup as its own job.

## Wildlife in Union: barns, attics, and the law

Old farmhouses and barns are excellent wildlife habitat, so [wildlife removal](/wildlife-removal/) is regular work in Union, not a sideline. **Bats** roost in attics and barn peaks. **Raccoons** den in chimneys, torn soffits, and barn corners, especially spring females raising young. **Squirrels** chew into attics and outbuildings through weak roof edges. **Skunks** den under porches, decks, and machine sheds, and **woodchucks** tunnel under foundations and grain storage where their burrows undermine the structure.

A trap in the haymow removes one animal; sealing the barn peak, soffit, and foundation gap — the **exclusion** work — is what keeps the next raccoon or woodchuck from moving into the same spot, with bats worked on the legal timeline.
