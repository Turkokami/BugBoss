## What a bed bug actually is: biology and life cycle

A bed bug is a small, flat, oval insect, reddish-brown in color, roughly the size and shape of an apple seed when fully grown. Flatness is the whole trick of the animal — it lets a bed bug slip into a crack no thicker than a credit card, which is why they are so good at hiding and so hard to reach. After a blood meal they swell and darken, becoming rounder and more balloon-like, but between meals they are thin enough to disappear into a mattress seam or the joint of a headboard and vanish.

Bed bugs feed exclusively on blood, and they prefer human hosts. They are mainly active at night, when a sleeping person is a still, warm, carbon-dioxide-breathing target. Drawn out by body heat and the carbon dioxide we exhale, they come out of their hiding places, feed for several minutes, and retreat before most people ever wake. They do not live on your body the way lice do; they live near where you sleep and commute to feed. That is a key point, because it tells you where to look and where treatment has to reach — not on the person, but in the bed, the frame, and the room around it.

The life cycle is why a small problem does not stay small. A mated female lays eggs steadily, on the order of a few per day and hundreds over her lifetime, cementing the tiny pale eggs into cracks and crevices where they are hard to see and hard to reach. Eggs hatch in about a week or two. The young, called nymphs, look like smaller, paler versions of the adults and must take a blood meal to move from one stage to the next, shedding their skin at each step through roughly five molts. In the warmth of a heated home, a nymph can reach breeding adulthood in about a month, and adults can live for months, going surprisingly long between meals when they have to. Do the math and it is clear how a few bugs carried home in July can be a widespread infestation by fall. Two features of this cycle matter most for treatment: bed bugs breed quickly, and their eggs are protected and resilient. Both are the reason follow-up is not optional.

## How bed bugs get into a home

Understanding how bed bugs arrive takes away both the mystery and the blame, and it points straight at prevention. They are moved around by people and by the things people carry, and there are a handful of common routes.

**Travel is the classic one.** Hotels, motels, cabins, rentals, and even the tidiest lodgings can have bed bugs, because they see a constant flow of guests and luggage from everywhere. A bag set on an infested bed or luggage rack, or clothing packed against an infested surface, can carry a few bugs or eggs home without anyone noticing. It only takes one pregnant female to start a new infestation. This is why the moment of return from a trip — unpacking, laundering, inspecting the bag — is such an important prevention point.

**Used furniture is right up there with travel.** Secondhand mattresses, box springs, upholstered chairs and couches, and even wooden headboards, bed frames, and dressers can harbor bed bugs in their seams and joints. Curbside "free" furniture is especially risky, because sometimes the reason it is on the curb is bed bugs. A good deal on a used couch turns into a very expensive problem when it brings an infestation with it.

**Movement from other infested places.** Bed bugs travel with people and belongings between homes. They come home from a stay with relatives, from a college dorm, from a trip in a shared vehicle, in a bag brought by an overnight guest, or with a child's belongings moving between households. In apartments, duplexes, and other multi-family buildings, they can also move between units through wall voids, shared utility runs, and gaps around pipes and outlets, which is why treating one unit in isolation is often only part of the answer.

None of these routes has anything to do with cleanliness. They have to do with the ordinary business of living — traveling, furnishing a home on a budget, having people over. That is worth remembering both for your own peace of mind and for how you treat anyone else dealing with them.

## How to detect them and read the signs

Bed bugs are secretive, so most people read the signs before they ever see a live bug in daylight. The most reliable approach is to look for several clues together rather than trusting any single one.

**Bites** are often the first thing people notice — itchy welts that frequently appear in a line or a small cluster on skin that was exposed during sleep, such as the arms, shoulders, neck, and legs. But bites are an unreliable diagnosis on their own. People react very differently; some develop obvious welts, others get mild marks, and a fair number show no reaction at all, which means an absence of bites does not prove an absence of bugs. Bites can also be confused with reactions to other insects. Treat them as a prompt to look closer, not as proof.

**The physical evidence is more dependable.** Look for small, dark, reddish-brown or blackish spots on the sheets, along the piping and seams of the mattress, on the box spring, and where the wall meets the baseboard behind the bed — this is bed bug fecal spotting, and it often smears if wiped with a damp cloth. Look for tiny rust-colored blood smears on the sheets. Look for the pale, papery shed skins the nymphs leave behind as they grow, which collect near hiding spots. In heavier, established infestations there can be a distinctive sweet, musty odor. And of course there are the bugs themselves and their eggs, tucked into mattress and box-spring seams, behind headboards, in the joints and cracks of the bed frame, and as numbers grow, farther out into nightstands, upholstered furniture, and baseboards.

Because bed bugs hide so well and can be confused with other insects, a professional inspection is the surest way to confirm what you are dealing with. Part of the value of that inspection is not just finding bed bugs but ruling out the look-alikes — most commonly [carpet beetles](/pest-library/carpet-beetles/) — so you get the right treatment for the actual problem instead of spending money chasing the wrong pest.

## Why bed bugs are so hard to eliminate

Bed bugs have a well-earned reputation for being difficult, and it comes down to three things working together: where they hide, how resistant they have become to common chemicals, and how well their eggs survive.

**Hiding is their specialty.** A flat body lets a bed bug fit into a crack thinner than a credit card, and a bedroom offers an enormous number of such cracks — mattress and box-spring seams, the folds and tufts of upholstery, the joints of a bed frame, the hollow interior of a headboard, the gaps behind baseboards and around outlets, cracks in the wall, the undersides of furniture, and any clutter stored nearby. A treatment that does not reach essentially all of these places leaves survivors, and survivors are all it takes to rebound. This is why thoroughness, not the strength of any one product, is what determines success.

**Resistance is the second factor.** Bed bugs across much of the country have developed significant resistance to the pyrethroid insecticides found in most over-the-counter bed bug sprays. In plain terms, the exact products a homeowner is most likely to buy are the ones many bed bug populations can now shrug off. Spraying them may kill a few and irritate the rest into moving, but it often will not clear the population, and it can drive bugs deeper into voids and out into adjacent rooms.

**The eggs are the third factor, and often the decisive one.** Bed bug eggs are cemented into hidden cracks and are more protected than the active bugs. A treatment can do a good job on the adults and nymphs present on the day and still leave viable eggs that hatch days later, restarting the cycle. This single fact is why a planned follow-up visit is essential rather than optional, and why no honest company promises a guaranteed one-and-done cure. You have to account for the next generation, not just the one you can see.

## Why DIY sprays and foggers fail, and can make it worse

It is completely understandable to want to handle bed bugs yourself, quickly and privately, with something off the shelf. The trouble is that the do-it-yourself approach usually does not just fail — it often makes the situation harder to fix.

**Store-bought sprays** run into all three of the problems above at once. They only treat the surfaces you can see and spray, while the bulk of the population sits in cracks and voids out of reach; many bed bugs are resistant to the active ingredients in those sprays; and the sprays do little to nothing about the protected eggs. The common result is that a homeowner sprays for weeks, knocks the visible bugs down enough to feel like progress, and then watches the infestation come roaring back as eggs hatch and hidden bugs return to feed.

**Foggers, or "bug bombs," are worse than useless for bed bugs and can actively backfire.** A fogger releases insecticide into the open air of a room, but bed bugs are hiding deep in seams, voids, and cracks where the fog never penetrates. What the fog does do is irritate them, and irritated bed bugs scatter — driving deeper into the structure and out into neighboring rooms and, in multi-family buildings, potentially toward adjoining units. A person who sets off foggers to solve a bed bug problem in one bedroom can end up spreading a contained infestation through the whole home. Foggers also introduce real safety concerns of their own and simply are not the right tool for this pest.

The other quiet failure of the DIY route is **time**. Every week spent on an approach that will not work is another week the population grows and spreads, turning a one-room problem into a whole-house one and making the eventual professional job larger. This is not a knock on anyone who has tried — most people do — it is just the honest reason bed bugs are considered a job for professional treatment with proper preparation and follow-up rather than a hardware-store fix.

## Preventing bed bugs: travel habits and used furniture

Because bed bugs are hitchhikers, preventing them is largely about the two doorways they use most — travel and used furniture — plus a general habit of paying attention.

**When you travel,** keep luggage off the bed and the upholstered chair when you first enter a room; use a hard luggage rack, the bathroom, or a hard floor while you check things out. Pull back the sheets and look at the mattress seams, the box spring, and behind the headboard for the telltale dark spotting, shed skins, or live bugs. Keep your bag zipped and elevated during the stay. When you get home, unpack in the garage or laundry area rather than carrying the suitcase straight into the bedroom, run washable clothing through a hot wash and dryer, and give the luggage a look before storing it. These small habits catch the great majority of travel-related infestations before they can start.

**With used furniture,** inspect anything before it comes inside — check seams, folds, joints, undersides, and backs, and be especially wary of soft, upholstered pieces and of curbside "free" items, which may have been discarded because of bed bugs in the first place. If a piece looks questionable, pass on it. The money saved on a secondhand couch is small next to the cost, time, and stress of clearing an infestation it might bring home.

**In general,** a little vigilance goes a long way: notice bites, spots, and shed skins early; be thoughtful about belongings coming back from stays elsewhere; and if you live in a multi-family building, take reports of bed bugs nearby seriously, since they can travel between units. Catching a problem when it is a few bugs in one room is far easier than catching it after it has spread.

## Telling bed bugs apart from carpet beetles and other look-alikes

One of the most useful things a professional inspection does is make sure you are treating the right pest, because several things get mistaken for bed bugs and call for entirely different handling.

**Carpet beetles** are the most common mix-up, and the distinction is worth understanding. Carpet beetles do not bite people and do not feed on blood. The damage is done by their small, bristly, slow-moving larvae, which feed on wool, fur, feathers, silk and other natural fibers, dead insects, pet hair, and stored dry goods, chewing irregular holes in sweaters, blankets, and rugs and leaving shed bristly skins in closets and undisturbed corners. The little rounded adult beetles tend to fly toward windows and light. So if the problem is holes in a wool sweater and bristly specks in the closet rather than nighttime bites and dark spotting on the mattress, you are likely looking at carpet beetles, which BugBoss addresses by finding the infested source, treating harborage, and helping protect susceptible items — a completely different job from bed bug work. You can compare the two on the [bed bugs](/pest-library/bed-bugs/) and [carpet beetles](/pest-library/carpet-beetles/) pages.

**Other look-alikes** round out the picture. Fleas bite, but they concentrate around pets and typically bite around the ankles and lower legs, and they jump, which bed bugs cannot do; if you have a dog or cat and are getting bitten around the feet, [fleas](/pest-library/fleas/) are worth ruling in or out. Young [cockroaches](/pest-library/cockroaches/), certain beetles, and other small household insects get confused with bed bugs too. Skin reactions from non-pest sources are also frequently blamed on bed bugs when no bug is present at all. The point is simple: the treatments for these pests differ completely, so confirming the identification first saves you from spending money and effort on the wrong problem.
