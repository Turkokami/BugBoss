## Cockroaches are the indoor pest you cannot afford to ignore

Most of the pests that trouble homes across rural Northeast Wisconsin come from outside and stay seasonal — the mice pushing in from the fields each fall, the cluster flies on the sunny walls, the boxelder bugs at the windows. Cockroaches are different, and in some ways more serious, because they are an indoor pest that lives, breeds, and spreads entirely inside your heated home, year-round, out of sight. By the time most people see a roach in the open, the hidden population is already well established. That is what makes cockroaches the pest homeowners most want gone the fastest, and the one where a wrong first move can make things dramatically worse.

There are two roaches we deal with in this region, and they are so different that lumping them together is the first mistake. The **German cockroach** is the serious one: small, fast, tied to kitchens and food areas, and capable of building a large hidden population in a matter of weeks. The **American cockroach** is the big one people picture — an inch and a half or more, reddish-brown, associated with drains, basements, and warm damp mechanical spaces. One is an explosive indoor breeder that spreads through a building; the other is more of a moisture-and-drain wanderer. Getting rid of either starts with knowing which one you have, and it almost never involves the can of spray under the sink. This page walks through both species in depth, the real health risks, why store products so often backfire, and how professional control actually ends an infestation rather than shuffling it around. It pairs with our [cockroach pest-library page](/pest-library/cockroaches/) and our overall [residential pest control](/residential-pest-control/) approach.

## German cockroach: small, fast, and built to infest

The German cockroach is the most important structural insect pest in the world, and for good reason. Everything about it is engineered to exploit the inside of a building. It is small — roughly half an inch long as an adult — light brown to tan, with two dark parallel stripes running lengthwise behind its head. That small size lets it hide in cracks and voids a larger insect could never fit into: the seams of cabinets, the gaps behind baseboards and trim, the motor housings and warm interiors of appliances, the space under and behind the stove and refrigerator, the voids around plumbing under the sink. During the day you will rarely see them, because they are nocturnal and pressed tightly into these harborages. When you do see one in the open in daylight, it usually means the hidden population has grown large enough that roaches are being crowded out of the best hiding spots.

Where German roaches live tells you everything about how to control them. They stay close to the two things they cannot do without: **food and moisture**. That puts them overwhelmingly in kitchens and bathrooms — around the stove, the fridge, the dishwasher, the sink, the coffee maker, small appliances, and inside cabinets and pantries. They are drawn to the warmth of running appliance motors, and to the grease film and food residue that build up in exactly the places people rarely deep-clean. A German cockroach does not need much: a smear of grease behind the range, crumbs in a drawer, and the condensation under a leaking trap will sustain a colony indefinitely.

### Why German cockroaches breed so explosively

The reason a German cockroach problem gets out of hand so fast is reproduction, and the numbers are genuinely startling. A female carries her egg case — a brown, capsule-shaped structure called an ootheca — attached to her body until just before it hatches, protecting the roughly thirty to forty eggs inside from the very sprays and treatments meant to kill them. She produces several of these egg cases over her life. The young, called nymphs, hatch and reach breeding age in a matter of weeks in the warm, stable conditions of a heated home, and then they start the cycle over. Under indoor conditions there is no winter pause: the population compounds straight through the Wisconsin cold as if it were July.

Do the arithmetic across a few generations and a single introduced female can be responsible for hundreds of roaches within a few months. This is why a couple of roaches that hitchhiked in on a grocery box in October can be a serious kitchen infestation by winter — and why timing matters so much in treatment. Because the eggs are shielded inside the case, a single treatment kills the active roaches but leaves the next generation waiting to hatch. That is the entire reason cockroach control is a multi-visit job rather than a one-and-done spray, a point we will come back to.

It is worth understanding the nymphs, too, because they are a large and easily overlooked part of any German cockroach population. When an egg case hatches it releases dozens of tiny nymphs — miniature, wingless, darker versions of the adults — that immediately seek out the same tight cracks and warm harborages the adults use. They molt several times as they grow, and at any given moment a healthy infestation is mostly nymphs and eggs rather than the fully grown adults people tend to notice. This matters for control in two ways. First, the sheer proportion of the population that is immature or still in the egg means the visible adults are only a fraction of what is actually present, so an infestation is almost always larger than it looks. Second, nymphs feed on the same non-repellent bait the adults do and share it through the population, which is precisely why properly placed bait can reach a whole colony — but also why the treatment has to persist long enough to carry through their development and the hatching of protected eggs.

## American cockroach: the big one from the drains

The American cockroach is the roach that makes people jump — large, often an inch and a half or longer, glossy reddish-brown, and capable of flight in short bursts. Despite the size and the alarming appearance, it is in most ways a less difficult pest than its small cousin, because it does not build the same explosive indoor breeding populations and it is not primarily a kitchen-counter insect.

American roaches are moisture animals. They want it warm and damp, so in Wisconsin homes they concentrate in basements, crawlspaces, utility and boiler rooms, and — above all — around the drain and sewer system. They frequently enter through the plumbing itself, coming up a floor drain or a seldom-used drain whose trap has dried out and lost the water seal that would normally block them. A sump pit, a leaking pipe, a chronically damp basement corner, or the warm humid space around a water heater or boiler all give them what they need. Finding a large reddish-brown roach in the basement near a floor drain is the classic American cockroach encounter.

Because they are tied so tightly to moisture and drains, controlling American roaches is as much about the building as about the insect. Treating the harborage and the drains matters, but the durable fix is correcting the moisture that draws them and closing the routes they use — making sure floor-drain and utility-drain traps stay filled to hold their seal, fixing leaks, drying out damp basements, and addressing any sewer-side entry point. A persistent American roach presence is usually a signal about the condition of a basement or a drain, not the start of a kitchen infestation.

## How infestations start — and why it is not about being dirty

One of the hardest parts of a cockroach problem, especially German roaches, is the shame people feel, as if it were a verdict on their housekeeping. It is not, and it helps to understand how these insects actually arrive, because they are hitchhikers above all else.

German cockroaches get into homes by being carried in. They ride in on **cardboard boxes and grocery bags** — cardboard is a favorite harborage, warm and full of tight seams, and it comes into the house constantly. They come in the folds, insulation, and motor housings of **used appliances**: a secondhand refrigerator, microwave, or dishwasher is one of the most common ways an infestation is introduced, because a warm appliance is exactly where German roaches like to live and breed. They arrive in **secondhand furniture and electronics**, in returning **luggage** after a stay somewhere infested, and in **deliveries and packages**. Once even a single pregnant female is inside, the reproduction described above does the rest.

And in any building with shared walls — apartments, duplexes, condos, senior housing — German roaches also arrive by simply **traveling between units** through the hidden voids around plumbing, electrical, and heating penetrations. In that situation an infestation next door becomes yours through no action of your own, and no amount of cleaning on your part will keep them out while the neighboring unit goes untreated.

The cleanliness point is worth being clear about. A spotless home is easier to treat, because there is less competing food to distract roaches from the bait, and good sanitation is a real part of control. But cleanliness alone does not prevent German cockroaches and does not eliminate them once established — they will live on the grease film behind an immaculate stove and the crumbs no one can reach. Having roaches does not mean you failed to keep a clean house. It means an insect built to infest found its way in, which happens to careful, tidy households all the time.

## The real health risks: asthma, allergies, and contamination

Cockroaches are not only unpleasant; they are a legitimate health concern, and this is the part that most justifies taking an infestation seriously rather than living with it.

The best-documented risk is **respiratory**. Cockroach droppings, shed skins, saliva, and the fragments of dead roaches contain proteins that are a recognized trigger for **asthma and allergies**. In a home with a heavy German cockroach population, these allergens accumulate in household dust — concentrated in the kitchen but spreading through the living space over time — and they can drive asthma attacks and allergic reactions, with the strongest effect on **children** and on anyone with existing respiratory sensitivity. For a household with kids or an asthmatic family member, a cockroach infestation is a genuine indoor-air-quality problem, not just an aesthetic one. This is the same allergen-and-asthma concern we flag for other droppings-heavy pests, and with German roaches it is central rather than incidental.

The second risk is **contamination**. Cockroaches travel between filthy and clean environments as a matter of routine — moving from drains, garbage, decaying matter, and the grimy spaces behind appliances directly onto counters, dishes, silverware, and stored food. Along the way they can mechanically carry bacteria, including organisms associated with food-borne illness, depositing them on the surfaces where you prepare and eat. They also foul food and surfaces with droppings and their musty, oily odor, which in heavy infestations becomes noticeable in the kitchen. None of this is cause for panic, but taken together — the asthma and allergy trigger, the potential to spread bacteria onto food surfaces, the contamination of stored food — it is the reason cockroaches rank among the pests most worth resolving properly and promptly.

## Why store sprays and foggers make German roaches worse

If there is one thing to take away from this page, it is this: reaching for a can of spray or a fogger is often the single worst move against German cockroaches, and it is the reason so many infestations spiral before anyone calls a professional.

The problem is how these products interact with how German roaches live. The roaches spend nearly all their time deep inside cracks, voids, and appliance housings, so a surface spray applied to open areas mostly misses them — it hits the countertops and floors they avoid, not the tight harborages where the colony actually is. Worse, most over-the-counter sprays are **repellent**: roaches can detect the chemical and flee from it. So instead of killing the population, the spray **scatters** it. Roaches driven out of one harborage by a repellent do not leave the building; they relocate — into other cabinets, other rooms, into wall voids, and in multi-family buildings straight into the neighboring units through the shared voids. A problem that was contained under one sink becomes a problem spread across the kitchen, the bathroom, and beyond.

**Foggers and bug bombs** are worse still. They release a cloud that settles on exposed surfaces but barely penetrates the cracks and voids where roaches hide, so they kill a few stragglers while driving the core population deeper into the walls — and they spread residue across the entire home, including food-prep surfaces, for very little control benefit. On top of all this, heavy repeated use of the same repellent products over time can favor roaches that are less bothered by them, making future control harder.

Professional control turns this logic around by using **non-repellent baits** instead of a spray the roaches run from. A gel bait is something the roaches actively feed on; they carry it back to the harborage, and through their droppings and the feeding of other roaches on those, the active ingredient moves through the population — reaching roaches that were never directly treated, including in the hidden voids. Bait works *with* roach behavior; repellent spray works against it. That single difference is why the professional approach succeeds in exactly the situations where the store spray made everything worse.

## Prevention: denying food, water, and entry

Once an infestation is eliminated — or to keep one from starting — prevention comes down to making your home a poor place for roaches to find food, water, and shelter. None of this substitutes for professional treatment of an active infestation, but after the roaches are gone, these habits are what keep them from coming back.

**Deny food.** Store pantry staples and dry goods in sealed containers rather than open packaging. Clean up crumbs and spills promptly, and pay special attention to the hidden grease and food film that builds up **behind and under the stove, refrigerator, and range hood** and inside small appliances — the places roaches feed that rarely get cleaned. Do not leave dirty dishes in the sink overnight, wipe down counters before bed, take out the garbage regularly and keep it covered, and pick up pet food and water bowls overnight rather than leaving them out.

**Deny water.** Roaches, and American roaches especially, depend on moisture. Fix dripping faucets and leaking pipes and traps under sinks, wipe down wet sinks and tubs at night, run a dehumidifier in a damp basement, and make sure **floor-drain and seldom-used drain traps stay filled** so they hold their water seal and block roaches from coming up the drain. Correcting a chronically damp basement does more against American roaches than any amount of spraying.

**Deny entry and shelter.** Break down and recycle **cardboard boxes** promptly instead of storing stacks of them, since cardboard is both a prime hiding place and a common way German roaches arrive. Inspect **grocery bags, secondhand appliances, used furniture, and returning luggage** before bringing them inside, since these are the classic hitchhiking routes. Seal gaps and cracks around plumbing and utility penetrations, along baseboards, and in cabinets, which removes harborage and — in shared buildings — the routes roaches use to travel between units. Reduce clutter, especially in kitchens, bathrooms, and basements, so there are fewer undisturbed places to hide.
