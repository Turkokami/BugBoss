## What general pest control really is — and who needs it

Every home has a baseline of insect pressure, and in rural Northeast Wisconsin that baseline runs high. You are surrounded by the exact habitat that produces bugs: fields, fencerows, woodlots, pasture, mulch beds, damp basements, and the moist edge where lawn meets foundation. General pest control is the service that manages that baseline. It is not a cure for one dramatic infestation; it is the steady, practical work of keeping the everyday nuisance insects out of your living space and knocking them down when they slip through.

The pests it covers are the ones almost everybody deals with at some point: [ants](/pest-library/ants/) trailing across the kitchen counter, [spiders](/pest-library/spiders/) webbing up the corners of the porch and basement, [crickets](/pest-library/crickets/) chirping somewhere behind the water heater, [silverfish](/pest-library/silverfish/) darting out of a stored box, [centipedes](/pest-library/centipedes/) racing across the bathroom floor at midnight, [earwigs](/pest-library/earwigs/) turning up under the welcome mat, and the [flies](/pest-library/flies/) and [gnats](/pest-library/gnats/) that gather around windows, drains, and houseplants. None of these is a crisis on its own. Left unmanaged, though, they are a constant low-grade nuisance that makes a home feel less like yours.

Who needs it? Realistically, most rural households benefit from some level of it, because the pressure never stops. A brand-new home in an open subdivision gets it. A century-old farmhouse ringed by cropland gets it worse. A lake cabin that sits empty half the year gets a version of it timed to the seasons. The question is rarely whether you have household insects — you do — but how much pressure your specific property is under and how light a plan will keep it comfortably in check. That is what a good general service figures out, and it is where a local company that actually knows the building stock and the landscape earns its keep over a national brand running a fixed spray route.

## The common household insects around here, and how to tell them apart

Getting the identification right is the whole game, because the fix follows the species. Half the calls we get start with a misidentification — a centipede called a silverfish, a camel cricket called a spider, a fungus gnat called a fruit fly — and the wrong name points to the wrong solution. Here is the honest field guide to the everyday pests this service covers.

### Ants

Most ant problems around here are nuisance species — pavement ants, odorous house ants, and the like — trailing indoors after food and moisture. You will see them in lines along counters, baseboards, and foundations, and especially around sinks and other damp spots. The critical thing to understand is that the trail you see is a scent path from a hidden nest to a food source, and spraying the trail only kills the foragers while the colony keeps producing. Effective control targets the nest, often with baits the ants carry home to the queen. There is one important exception: large black ants showing up indoors, particularly in winter, are usually [carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/), which excavate wood and cause structural damage. Those are a different problem handled under our [ant and carpenter ant control](/residential-pest-control/ant-control/) service, not general pest work.

### Spiders

Spiders are predators, which means a spider problem is almost always a symptom of another insect problem — they follow their food. The overwhelming majority of Wisconsin spiders are harmless nuisances; the state has very few medically significant species, and bites are rare. You will notice webs in corners, basements, and eaves, egg sacs tucked in sheltered spots, and a jump in sightings in late summer and fall as the insects they hunt move toward the house. Because spiders track prey, the most effective control is reducing that prey — which is exactly what a general exterior and harborage treatment does. Knock down the insects and the spiders that were living off them thin out too.

### Crickets

As nights cool in late summer and fall, crickets migrate toward warm structures. Field crickets are the familiar chirpers you hear behind the water heater or in the garage; camel crickets — also called cave crickets — are humpbacked, silent, and can gather in surprising numbers in damp basements and crawlspaces. Both enter at ground level through foundation gaps and window wells. They do not bite or spread disease, but in numbers they can damage fabrics and paper, and the chirping and clustering wear on your patience fast. Control comes down to sealing the ground-level entry points, reducing basement moisture and clutter, and treating exterior harborage before the fall push.

### Silverfish

Silverfish are teardrop-shaped, wingless, silvery insects that live in humid, dark places — bathrooms, basements, and boxes stored in damp areas — feeding on the starches in paper, cardboard, book bindings, wallpaper paste, and dry goods. If you are finding them regularly, the real message is that you have a humidity problem. They leave irregular holes and grazed surfaces on paper and cardboard, along with occasional yellowish stains. The durable fix is moisture control: dehumidify, ventilate, fix leaks, and store papers and dry goods in sealed containers. Treating harborage handles the current population, but controlling humidity is what keeps them from coming back.

### Centipedes

Fast, many-legged, and genuinely startling to find, the house centipede is a predator that lives where it is damp and where other insects are available to eat. They turn up in basements, bathrooms, and crawlspaces, usually at night. Like spiders, they are essentially a symptom: finding them regularly points to dampness plus a supply of prey insects, not to a centipede problem on its own. They are harmless to people and rarely bite. The fix is the same two-part approach — reduce moisture and control the prey insects — and the centipedes decline on their own once their food and their damp shelter are gone.

### Earwigs

Earwigs are the pincered insects that gather in damp mulch, under pots and boards, in leaf litter, and along the moist zone at the foundation. They occasionally wander indoors, especially during hot, dry spells when they are hunting for moisture, entering at ground level near doors. Despite the menacing pincers and the old folklore, they are harmless to people — the pincers are for defense, and they do not crawl into ears in any real sense. Because they are an outdoor harborage pest, the control is exterior: pull mulch and debris back from the foundation, improve drainage, seal gaps around doors, and treat the harborage zone where they congregate.

### Flies and gnats

These two get grouped together but they are different problems. Nuisance [flies](/pest-library/flies/) — house flies and other filth flies — breed in moist decaying matter like garbage, manure, compost, silage, and spilled feed, which makes them a special concern on farms and rural properties. A fly problem is really a breeding-source problem, so control starts with finding and eliminating the source, not just swatting adults. The small flies people call [gnats](/pest-library/gnats/) are almost always one of three things, each with its own breeding site: fungus gnats develop in overwatered houseplant soil, fruit flies in ripe or fermenting produce and recycling, and drain flies in the organic film inside drains. Identifying which one you have points straight at the fix, because removing the breeding site is what actually stops them — no amount of spraying the air does.

## Why source-targeting beats blanket spraying

Here is the philosophy that separates real pest control from the spray-and-go routine, and it is worth understanding because it is the reason the work lasts.

Most household insects do not live in the middle of your living room. They live in a nest, a breeding site, or a harborage zone, and they travel from there to the places you notice them. The ants on your counter come from a colony in the wall void or the mulch bed. The gnats around the sink come from a film in the drain. The crickets in the basement came up through a specific gap in the foundation. When a company blankets your baseboards with spray, it kills the individuals crossing that surface for a week or two, but it never touches the colony, the breeding site, or the entry point. ## The interior-plus-exterior approach

A complete general service always has two halves, and skipping either one is why so many pest problems linger.

The **interior treatment** handles what is already inside and the places pests shelter and breed indoors. That means treating harborage — the cracks, voids, under-sink cabinets, basement corners, and baseboards where insects hide — and addressing indoor breeding sites like the drain film that produces drain flies or the damp storage that supports silverfish. Interior work is targeted, not a whole-house fogging; we treat the specific problem areas so exposure in your living space stays minimal while the treatment goes where the bugs actually are.

The **exterior barrier** is the more important half for keeping a home clear over time, because most of these pests originate outdoors and only wander in. The barrier is applied around the foundation, the entry points, and the ground-to-wall band that nearly every household insect has to cross to get inside. It intercepts the ants trailing up from the soil, the crickets and earwigs coming through ground-level gaps, the spiders following their prey toward the warmth of the house. Stopping pests at the perimeter is far more efficient than chasing them once they are indoors and scattered through the house.

The two halves work together. The interior treatment clears the current occupants; the exterior barrier keeps the next wave out. Do only the interior and fresh bugs keep coming through the open perimeter. Do only the exterior and the population already inside keeps going. Done together, they break the cycle — which is the whole point.

## The seasonal rhythm of household pests in Northeast Wisconsin

Household pest pressure is not constant. It swells and recedes with the seasons, and knowing the pattern lets you get ahead of it instead of always reacting.

**Spring (March through May)** is the wake-up. As the ground thaws and warms, ants become active and start foraging indoors, overwintering insects that spent the cold months in wall voids stir and move toward light, and the whole outdoor insect world comes back to life. This is the first big window for treatment, because knocking down colonies and laying the exterior barrier early heads off the summer build-up before it starts.

**Summer (June through August)** is peak activity outdoors. Populations of nearly everything are booming in the fields, lawns, and mulch beds, and pressure on the building is steady. Flies peak in the warm months, especially around farms and animal areas. Earwigs and other harborage pests are at their thickest in the damp zones around the foundation. This is prime time for the exterior barrier to earn its keep, intercepting the constant probing at the perimeter.

**Fall (September through November)** is the migration. As nights turn cold and the fields are harvested, insects that spent the summer outdoors go looking for somewhere warm — and your heated house is the obvious target. Crickets push toward basements and garages, spiders follow their prey inward, and this is also when the separate fall-invader complex hits hardest. This is the second critical treatment window; sealing entry points and reinforcing the barrier before the push keeps the bulk of the fall migration outside.

**Winter (December through February)** is the quiet season for most general pests, but not silent. Insects that got into wall voids and basements in fall are overwintering there, and the ones that establish indoors — silverfish in a humid basement, the occasional carpenter ant colony living in the heated structure — carry on through the cold. Warm winter days can even rouse overwintering insects into the living space. Winter is a good time to address the moisture and harborage conditions that sustain indoor pests, so you go into spring with fewer weak points.

This rhythm is exactly why so many rural homes settle on a recurring seasonal plan built around the two big pushes — a spring visit and a late-summer-to-fall visit — rather than a single treatment that inevitably fades before the next wave arrives.

## Prevention and exclusion you can do yourself

The most effective thing any homeowner can do is make the house less attractive and harder to get into. None of this replaces treatment, but all of it lowers the pressure and makes the treatment last longer.

**Deny them moisture.** So many household pests — silverfish, centipedes, earwigs, camel crickets — are moisture-driven. Run a dehumidifier in a damp basement, fix plumbing leaks, improve drainage away from the foundation, and ventilate humid spaces. Drying out the damp zones removes the single biggest draw for a whole category of these insects.

**Deny them food.** Keep kitchen food sealed and surfaces clean to cut off ants, store dry goods and papers in sealed containers to protect them from silverfish, empty and clean recycling to starve fruit flies, let houseplant soil dry between waterings to stop fungus gnats, and clean drains to remove the film that breeds drain flies. On farms and rural properties, managing garbage, manure, compost, and spilled feed does more against flies than any spray.

**Seal them out.** Close the ground-level gaps that let crawling insects in — around doors and windows, at foundation cracks, around utility and pipe penetrations, and at window wells. Fit tight door sweeps and repair damaged screens. Pull mulch, leaf litter, woodpiles, and dense vegetation back from the foundation so you are not maintaining a harborage zone right against the house that funnels pests to the wall.

Do these things and you have removed the moisture, the food, and the doorways that household insects depend on. Combined with a targeted treatment and an exterior barrier, it is the difference between a home that stays comfortably clear and one that fights the same bugs every season.
