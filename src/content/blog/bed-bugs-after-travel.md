## The one that has nothing to do with how you keep house

Most of what we deal with in Northeast Wisconsin has a logic you can act on. Mice come in from the field because it got cold. Cluster flies come out of the earthworms in the pasture. Wasps build where there is shelter. All of it maps to the property.

Bed bugs do not work like that. **They arrive in luggage.** They feed on blood, so your kitchen, your trash, your dishes, and your housekeeping are irrelevant to them. The cleanest house in Waupaca County gets bed bugs exactly as easily as any other, and the only variable that really matters is whether somebody carried them in.

Which is good news, in a way, because a thing that arrives one specific way can be intercepted one specific way. That is what this post is about — the twenty minutes at the end of a trip that stop the whole problem before it starts.

It is worth saying the other part plainly too: **there is no shame in this.** The stigma is the single most expensive thing about bed bugs, because it is what makes people wait. Three months of hoping it is something else turns a one-room problem into a whole-house problem, and by then it costs several times as much to fix. Nobody at BugBoss thinks less of a customer for calling about bed bugs. It is a common problem in a mobile world.

## At the hotel: two minutes, before you unpack

You do not need to strip the room. You need to check four places.

**First, park your bags somewhere hard.** The bathroom floor or the tub is ideal. The luggage rack is second best, pulled away from the wall. What you are avoiding is the bed and the upholstered chair, which are the two places bed bugs actually live.

**Then, phone light on:**

1. **The mattress seams and piping**, particularly at the head end. Pull the fitted sheet back at the corner.
2. **The box spring**, especially the corners and along the underside of the fabric edge.
3. **The headboard** — the crevice where it meets the wall, and the back of it if it lifts off the bracket. This is the number one hiding spot in a hotel room and the one nobody checks.
4. **The upholstered chair or couch**, in the seams and under the cushion.

**What you are looking for:** live insects, about apple-seed size, flat and reddish-brown; **pale translucent cast skins**, which look like empty shells of the insect and are often more visible than the bugs; and **fecal spotting** — small dark spots like ink dots that have bled slightly into the fabric, usually clustered along a seam.

If you find any of it, go back to the desk and ask for a room **in a different part of the building**. Not the room next door and not the one directly above or below, because bed bugs move between adjacent rooms.

## During the trip: keep the suitcase off the floor and closed

Simple habits, no effort:

- **Keep the case on the rack or a hard surface**, zipped shut when you are not in it.
- **Do not use the dresser drawers.** Live out of the case. Drawers are a classic harborage.
- **Keep dirty laundry in a sealed bag** inside the case rather than loose.

That is it. The point is to give a bug that is in the room no easy route into your belongings.

## Getting home: the twenty minutes that matter

This is the part that actually protects your house, and it happens **before the suitcase crosses the bedroom threshold.**

**Unpack somewhere that is not the bedroom.** The garage, the porch, the mudroom, the laundry room, the entry — anywhere with a hard floor and no upholstery.

**Everything fabric goes straight into the dryer on high for thirty minutes.** Not the hamper. Not the floor. The dryer. Sustained heat kills bed bugs at every life stage, eggs included, and a household dryer on high reaches it. Wash afterward if you like, but the heat is doing the work — a cold or warm wash on its own is not reliable.

This covers the things people forget: the jacket you never wore, the clean clothes you did not touch, the shoes, the backpack, the stuffed animal the kids took. If it will survive a hot dryer, it goes in the dryer.

**Vacuum the empty case, inside and out.** Concentrate on the seams, the zipper track, the piping, and the pockets. Then take the canister or bag outside and empty it into the outdoor trash immediately.

**Store the case away from the bedroom.** Basement, garage, a closet that is not the bedroom closet. A large sealed plastic bag or bin is better still.

Twenty minutes. Compared with the cost, the disruption, and the sheer misery of an established infestation, this is the best trade in pest control.

## The other ways they get in

Travel is the main one, but not the only one, and out here in the country the others matter more than people expect.

**Secondhand furniture.** Roadside finds, rummage sales, auctions, estate sales, thrift stores, marketplace pickups. Upholstered furniture and bed frames are the high-risk categories. Inspect anything before it goes in the vehicle — seams, undersides, the joints of a wooden frame, the staple line under a couch — and if you cannot inspect it properly, do not put it in a bedroom.

**Multi-unit buildings.** Apartments, duplexes, senior housing. Bed bugs move between units along plumbing chases, electrical runs, shared voids, and hallways, which means a neighbor's problem can become yours through no action of your own. This is why treating a unit in isolation so often fails and why building-wide coordination matters. It is also why we wrote up the [Wisconsin rental responsibility question](/compliance/bed-bugs-in-rentals/) as its own page.

**Guests, students home for the summer, kids back from camp, and workers who move between job sites.** Not a reason to be suspicious of people — just a reason for the dryer routine to apply to more than vacations.

**Hospitals, care facilities, and repeated overnight stays.** Anyone doing regular overnights somewhere they do not control is worth building the routine around.

## Do not diagnose this from your skin

People spend weeks on this and it almost never resolves anything, so it is worth being direct: **bite patterns are not diagnostic.**

Reactions to bed bug bites vary enormously between individuals. Some people welt up dramatically, some show a delayed reaction days later, and a substantial share of people show **no visible reaction at all** — which means an absence of bites proves nothing either. Meanwhile plenty of things that are not bed bug bites look exactly like bed bug bites: [flea](/pest-library/fleas/) bites, [mosquito](/pest-library/mosquitoes/) bites, [mite](/pest-library/mites/) reactions, contact dermatitis, dry-skin irritation, and simple anxiety-driven attention to marks that were always there.

The bites are a prompt to look. They are not the evidence.

**The evidence is physical**, and there are three kinds:

- **Live insects.** Adults are about the size and color of an apple seed, flat unless recently fed. Immatures are smaller and paler.
- **Cast skins.** Bed bugs molt five times, leaving a translucent shell each time. These persist and are often the first thing found.
- **Fecal spotting.** Dark, ink-dot marks along mattress seams, box spring corners, behind the headboard, in the bed frame joints, and along the baseboard behind the bed.

Where to look, in order of likelihood: mattress seams and piping, box spring corners and underside, the bed frame and slat joints, behind and under the headboard, the nightstand, the baseboard and carpet edge nearest the bed, and any upholstered furniture someone regularly sleeps in.

**Take a photo of anything you find and keep the specimen if you can** — tape it to an index card or drop it in a sealed bag. It makes the identification instant instead of speculative, and it matters if you rent and need a record.

## Why this one needs a professional

We say a lot on this site that some things do not need us. Bed bugs are the other case, and here is the honest reasoning.

**They hide where you cannot reach.** In the frame joints, inside the box spring fabric, under carpet edges, behind baseboard, inside electrical outlets, in the seam of a chair, behind a picture frame. A visible cluster is a fraction of the population.

**They survive a long time without feeding**, so waiting out an empty room does not work on the timescale anyone can actually manage.

**Over-the-counter resistance is real.** Bed bug populations in many areas have documented resistance to the pyrethroid insecticides that most consumer aerosols rely on. The practical result of spraying one is that you kill some, fail to kill others, and **scatter the survivors** — out of the bed and into the adjacent room, or in an apartment, into the next unit. Dispersal is how a one-bedroom problem becomes a whole-floor problem.

**Total-release foggers are the worst version of this.** They do not penetrate harborage, they push insects deeper into voids and outward into other rooms, and there is a genuine fire and explosion risk with them.

What does help, and is worth doing while you arrange treatment:

- **Zippered bed bug encasements** on the mattress and box spring. Anything sealed inside dies inside, and it removes the two biggest harborage sites from the equation permanently. Leave them on.
- **Laundering everything on high heat**, as above.
- **Vacuuming** the bed frame, edges, and floor, then emptying outdoors into sealed trash immediately.
- **Pulling the bed away from the wall** and keeping bedding off the floor, so the bed is an island.
- **Not moving furniture, mattresses, or bedding to another room** — this is the single most common way a contained problem gets spread.

## What treatment actually looks like

A real bed bug job is not one visit with a sprayer. It is an inspection to establish the extent, a prepared room, a treatment appropriate to the situation, and **at least one follow-up** — because eggs hatch after the first treatment and something has to be there when they do.

Preparation matters and we will walk you through it, because a job done in an unprepared room mostly fails. Expect to launder, to encase the mattress, to reduce clutter around the bed, and to leave the bed in place rather than moving it.
