## The ten-below paradox

It is the third week of January, it has not been above zero in four days, and there is a ladybug walking across your kitchen ceiling. A week later the sun comes out, the thermometer climbs to the high thirties, and by afternoon there are nine of them at the south window along with a couple of sluggish flies.

Nearly everybody's first thought is the same: *how did they get in?* And the answer is the thing that reframes the whole problem — **they didn't.** Not this week. They got in during the last two weeks of September and the first half of October, and they have been sitting in your walls, soffits, and attic ever since.

Understanding that is the difference between a frustrating winter of spraying at the window and a much better fall next year.

## The four you are probably looking at

Northeast Wisconsin has a standard cast for this. If something is at your window in January, it is very likely one of these.

**[Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/)** — the orange-to-red domed beetles that are not the native ladybug. They mass on sun-warmed south and west walls in fall by the thousands, work their way into any gap, and overwinter in aggregations in wall voids and attics. They are the ones that stain: disturbed, they reflex-bleed a yellow fluid that smells sharp and marks curtains and pale paint.

**[Boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/)** — flat, half-inch, black with red-orange lines. Same behavior, same walls, same gaps. They also leave a stain if crushed, and they do exactly nothing else — no bites, no damage, no interest in food.

**[Brown marmorated stink bugs](/pest-library/brown-marmorated-stink-bugs/)** — shield-shaped, mottled brown, with the banded antennae that separate them from our native stink bugs. Newer to this part of the state than the other three and spreading. The smell is the whole reputation, and it is well earned; do not vacuum them with the same machine you use for the carpet unless you like the result.

**[Cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/)** — slow, dark, slightly larger than a house fly, with golden hairs on the thorax, and the tendency to bumble at a window rather than fly properly. Their larvae parasitize earthworms in the soil, which is why farmhouses on old pasture and lawn get them so badly and why it has nothing whatever to do with the kitchen. We have a [full guide to cluster fly control](/guides/cluster-fly-control/) and a [longer post on why farmhouses get hit hardest](/blog/cluster-flies-wisconsin-farmhouses/).

What all four have in common is the important part: **none of them feed indoors, breed indoors, bite, sting, or damage anything.** They are a nuisance, full stop.

## Why they wake up in the middle of winter

The insects in your wall are not dead. They are in **diapause** — a dormant, low-metabolism state that lets them sit out the winter on stored reserves. Diapause is temperature-driven, and it is reversible.

So on a February thaw, or on any bright afternoon when the sun loads heat into a south or west wall, the cavity behind that siding warms up well past the outside air temperature. The insects in it rouse. Having no calendar, they read warmth as spring and start moving — and they move **toward light**, because that is how an overwintering insect finds its way back out to the world.

The tragedy, from the bug's point of view, is that in a heated house the brightest thing is usually on the wrong side of the wall. It finds a gap in the interior finish instead of the exterior one — around a window frame, a recessed light, a bath fan, a poorly sealed electrical box, the top plate above a wall — and ends up in your living room.

This is why:

- They appear at **south and west windows** first.
- They show up **on warm days and disappear on cold ones**.
- They gather at **ceiling fixtures and lamps** at night.
- The same house produces a handful **over and over** all winter rather than all at once.

## The one genuinely good piece of news

**They cannot multiply in there.** There is no food and no breeding site in a wall cavity for any of these four. Whatever number got in during October is the total number you will ever deal with, and every one you vacuum is permanently gone from the pool.

That is worth holding onto in February, because the drip-drip of two here and five there feels endless when it is actually a fixed and shrinking population.

## What to do right now

**Vacuum, don't squash.** This is the whole indoor strategy and it is genuinely the right one. Crushing an Asian lady beetle or a stink bug releases the exact chemical you do not want on your paint or in your nose.

A useful trick for the lady beetles: drop a knee-high nylon stocking into the vacuum wand and fold the open end back over the outside of the tube, secured with a rubber band. The insects collect in the stocking rather than the machine, and you can pull it out, tie it off, and take it outside. Empty a shop vac outdoors, well away from the house.

**Deal with the ones in light fixtures.** Enclosed ceiling fixtures collect dead insects all winter — it is often where people first realize how many there are. Wait for the bulb to cool, take the cover down, tip it out, wash it.

**Leave the pesticide alone.** We will say this plainly because it is the most common thing people do and the least useful: an indoor aerosol at the window kills the six insects you can see and does nothing about the several hundred still in the wall. It puts material where your family lives to solve a cosmetic problem. It is not what we would do in our own house.

**Do not seal the interior gaps with the insects still behind them.** Caulking around a window stop in January mostly means the insects die in the cavity instead. Dead insects in a void draw [carpet beetles](/pest-library/carpet-beetles/) and other scavengers, which is a real and worse problem. Interior sealing is a summer job, once the wall is empty.

## Winter is for making the list

Here is the genuinely productive thing to do in January and February, and hardly anybody does it: **work out where they are coming in, while the evidence is fresh.**

Note which rooms produce insects and which do not. Which windows. Which side of the house. Whether they come from the ceiling — which points at the attic and the top plate — or from around a window frame, which points at the fenestration itself. On a bright day, look at the outside of the walls where you are getting them and look hard at:

- The **soffit** — the vent strips, the joint where soffit meets fascia, and anywhere the panel has pulled or torn.
- The **frieze board** where the siding meets the soffit. On a lot of older houses out here that joint is simply open.
- **Gable and ridge vents** with damaged or missing screening.
- **Window and door frames** — worn weatherstrip, failed caulk at the trim, gaps at the sill.
- **Utility penetrations** — the electrical service, the dryer vent, the hose bibs, the AC line set, the satellite mount.
- **Attic access** from outside, and the chimney flashing.

Write it down. That list is next August's work order, and having it in hand in the summer — when nobody is thinking about ladybugs — is what makes the difference between planning to do something and actually doing it.

## Why the fix is a September fix

There is no honest way around this: **the intervention that works happens before the insects get in.** Two parts, both timed.

**Exclusion.** Seal the gaps on the list. This is permanent, it works on all four species at once, and it also handles [mice](/pest-library/mice/), [bats](/pest-library/bats/), and [wasps](/residential-pest-control/stinging-insects/) using the same openings. It is the part with lasting value.

**A properly timed exterior treatment.** Late August into September, on the walls the insects actually mass on — the sunny south and west faces, the soffit line, around windows and doors — *before* they start piling up on the siding. Timing is most of the effect. The same treatment applied in late October, when they are already in the wall, does very little.

Both together is what a fall invader program is, and it is one of the more satisfying jobs we do out here because the difference the following winter is obvious to the customer without anyone having to explain it. ## The farmhouse problem specifically

If you are in an older farmhouse on open ground around [Clintonville](/service-area/clintonville/), [Marion](/service-area/marion/), [Bear Creek](/service-area/bear-creek/), or anywhere out in the county, you get a harder version of this than a house in the village, for structural reasons rather than housekeeping ones:

- A **tall pale wall standing alone in open country** is the most conspicuous warm surface for a mile, so the insects find it first and in larger numbers.
- **Balloon framing** in pre-1940s houses leaves the wall cavities running open from sill plate to attic. An insect that enters at the foundation has an unobstructed route to the second-floor bedroom.
- **A century of settlement** has opened joints, trim, and masonry in ways no amount of maintenance fully closes.
- **Big attics with original venting** and generous soffit runs give a lot of entry area to defend.

None of that is fixable in the sense of making it go away. It is manageable, and the management is the same two-part fall program done consistently — but it is worth knowing why your house gets ten times what your cousin's ranch in town gets, so you can stop taking it personally.
