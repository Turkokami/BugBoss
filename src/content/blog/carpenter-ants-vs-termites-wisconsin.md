## The spring panic: "I think I have termites"

It happens every April. The weather warms, and one afternoon there's a cloud of winged insects at a sunny window, or a little pile of them on the sill. The first word out of most people's mouths is *termites* — because that's the word everyone knows, and it's the scary one. Then they spend a nervous evening online convinced the house is being eaten out from under them.

Here's the reassuring part and the important part, both at once: in northern Wisconsin, those spring swarmers are almost always **carpenter ants, not termites.** That's genuinely better news — but it doesn't mean nothing's wrong, because an indoor carpenter ant swarm points to a nest in your house. This post is about telling the two apart with confidence and understanding what each one actually means. For the full detail on carpenter ants and how we handle them, our [carpenter ant pest page](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) is the deep reference.

## Why termites are rare up here

Start with the geography, because it settles most of the worry. **Eastern subterranean termites** do exist in Wisconsin, but they're concentrated in scattered, localized pockets in the warmer southern part of the state. This far north — around Clintonville and up through the northern counties — established termite infestations are uncommon. The cold, the soil, and the shorter warm season all work against them getting a foothold.

Carpenter ants, on the other hand, are everywhere up here. They're a native, cold-hardy insect perfectly at home in the woods, woodpiles, and damp older homes of the northern counties. So when the odds say "winged insect swarming indoors in spring, in northern Wisconsin," the answer is carpenter ant far more often than not. That's not a reason to shrug it off — but it does mean you can take a breath and identify carefully rather than assuming the worst.

## The three-second identification

You can tell a carpenter ant swarmer from a termite swarmer by looking at three things. Catch one on a piece of tape or in a jar and check:

**The waist.** This is the giveaway. A carpenter ant has the classic ant shape — a **pinched, narrow waist** between the thorax and abdomen. A termite has **no waist at all**; its body is straight and broad from front to back, more like a grain of rice with wings.

**The antennae.** Carpenter ant antennae are **elbowed** — clearly bent, like a jointed arm. Termite antennae are **straight**, made of tiny bead-like segments in a straight line.

**The wings.** Both have two pairs of wings, but a carpenter ant's **front wings are noticeably longer than the back pair**. A termite's four wings are all **about equal in length**, and often extend well past the body.

Any one of these tells you a lot, but the waist is the fastest. Pinched waist and bent antennae? It's an ant. Straight body and straight antennae with equal wings? Only then are you looking at something in the termite family — and up here, that's the rare case worth a professional confirmation.

## Eat vs. excavate: what they do to wood

The biggest practical difference between the two isn't how they look — it's what they do to your house.

**Termites eat wood.** They digest the cellulose, consuming the wood itself, and they typically work in soil-connected galleries, leaving a mud-packed, dirty appearance and mud tubes along foundations.

**Carpenter ants don't eat wood at all.** This surprises people. Carpenter ants **excavate** wood to build their nest galleries — they chew it out and carry the debris away, but they don't consume it. That's why the two leave completely different evidence:

- Carpenter ant galleries are **smooth and clean**, almost sandpapered-looking, and the ants push out the excavated debris as **frass** — a fine, sawdust-like material, often mixed with bits of dead insects, that piles up below the nest.
- Termite damage looks the opposite: the wood is eaten away and often packed with soil and mud.

So if you find clean tunnels and little piles of what looks like sawdust under a baseboard or in the basement, you're looking at carpenter ants. That frass is one of the most reliable signs there is.

## What an indoor swarm actually means

Here's why the spring swarm matters even though it's "just ants." Swarmers are a colony's reproductive stage — winged males and females produced by a **mature** colony to fly off and start new ones. When they emerge *outdoors*, that's just nature. When they emerge *inside your living space*, tapping at the windows from the inside, it's a strong signal that there's an **established nest within the structure** — not ants wandering in from the yard.

And carpenter ants complicate this with their nesting habits. A colony often has a **parent nest outdoors** — in a stump, a tree, a woodpile, buried roots — plus one or more **satellite nests indoors**, connected by foraging trails. The satellite nests are what put ants and swarmers inside your walls. That structure is exactly why spraying the ants you see never works for long: you're killing foragers while the nest keeps producing more.

Other signs that point to an indoor nest: large ants active inside in late winter and early spring, when nothing should be foraging outdoors; a faint **rustling** in the walls on a quiet night as the colony works; and frass piles turning up repeatedly in the same spots.

## Moisture is the real story

Carpenter ants don't attack sound, dry wood at random — they go for wood that's been **softened by moisture**. That's the thread that ties every carpenter ant job together. The prime nest sites are wherever water has gotten into the structure:

- Sills and framing under a roof leak or an ice dam.
- Damp basements and crawlspaces.
- Rotting window and door frames.
- Deck ledgers, porch posts, and any wood in contact with soil.
- Wall areas around a chronic plumbing leak.

This is genuinely good news for control, because it means the fix has two halves, and the second half is under your control. You treat the nest, *and* you address the moisture that made that wood attractive in the first place. A dry, sound house is a far less appealing nest site. Ignore the water problem and you'll keep re-inviting ants no matter how many you kill — which is why any real carpenter ant job includes finding the water source, not just the nest.

## Why DIY spraying fails

The instinct is to spray the trail of ants on the counter or the swarmers at the window, and it fails for a consistent reason: it kills the **foragers you can see** while leaving the **nest you can't** untouched. With a parent nest possibly outdoors and satellites in the walls, the visible ants are a small fraction of the colony, and the colony simply replaces them. You knock down the trail, feel better for a week, and they're back.

Effective control means locating the actual nest — parent and satellite — following the trails and the frass and the swarmer emergence points to find where the colony really lives, and treating *there*, while dealing with the moisture feeding it. That's inspection work, and it's the difference between suppressing the symptom and ending the infestation. It's the same find-the-source discipline we bring to [mice](/pest-library/mice/) and [cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/) — the visible bugs are never the whole problem.

## What to do if you're seeing swarmers now

If you've got winged insects at the windows this April, work through it in order:

**First, identify.** Catch one and check the waist, antennae, and wings. Pinched waist, elbowed antennae, longer front wings — carpenter ant, the overwhelmingly likely answer up here. Straight body, straight antennae, equal wings — the rare possible termite, worth professional confirmation given how uncommon they are this far north.

**Then, look for the nest evidence.** Check for frass piles under baseboards, in the basement, around window frames and wooden features. Listen for rustling in the walls. Note where the swarmers are concentrated — that's often near the nest.

**Find the water.** Think about where the house has had leaks, ice dams, damp, or rot. The nest is very likely in or near moisture-damaged wood.

**Don't just spray and hope.** A can of ant spray on the swarmers accomplishes almost nothing against the colony. If there's an indoor nest, it needs to be located and treated properly.
