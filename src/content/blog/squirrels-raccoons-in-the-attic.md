## The noise is data

Almost every attic wildlife call starts the same way: *there's something up there.* And almost always, the caller already has most of the information needed to identify it — they just have not been told that the **timing** of the noise is the most diagnostic thing about it.

So before you call anybody, spend two nights paying attention to three things: **when** you hear it, **what kind** of sound it is, and **where** in the house it seems to come from. That narrows it to one or two species, and the species determines everything that follows — including whether this is legal to deal with right now, and whether sealing the hole would be a catastrophe.

## Reading the noise

### Dawn and dusk, scratching and rolling — gray squirrels

**Gray squirrels are strictly diurnal.** That single fact does more identification work than anything else. If the attic is busy at first light, quiet through the middle of the day, busy again in the late afternoon, and silent all night, you have squirrels.

The sound is scratching, scurrying, and often a distinct **rolling** noise — that is a nut or an acorn being moved across the ceiling joists, and it is close to definitive.

[Squirrels](/pest-library/squirrels/) get in at the roof edge: soffit and fascia joints, gable vents, ridge vents, the corner where two roof planes meet, and any spot where ice damming has already pulled the fascia loose. They can also gnaw a small gap into a large one, so a hole that was too small in September is not necessarily too small in November.

### After dark, heavy and deliberate — raccoons

If it sounds like a person is up there, it is probably a [raccoon](/pest-library/raccoons/). The signature is **weight**: thumping, dragging, a slow deliberate walk rather than a scurry. Activity starts at dusk and runs through the night. You may also hear **vocalizations** — chittering, growling, and in spring, the distinctive crying of kits.

Raccoons are the most destructive of the attic animals, because they are strong and they have hands. They tear soffit panels off, pull back roof edges, work shingles loose, rip open ductwork, and establish a **latrine** in the attic, which is a contamination problem in its own right.

They are also the animal most likely to injure a person or a pet if cornered, and they carry health considerations that make DIY handling a genuinely bad idea. Do not go up there to have a look.

### Fast, light skittering, mostly at night — mice

Quick, light, and constant, in the attic and inside the walls, with the sound often seeming to run along a line — that is [mice](/pest-library/mice/). Peak activity is after dark, and unlike squirrels it does not stop and start on a schedule.

Mice in the attic are the same problem as mice anywhere else in the building and they will be in the walls too. Fall is when the field population moves in, which is why this is an October call. Our [farmhouse rodent exclusion guide](/guides/farmhouse-rodent-exclusion/) covers the rural version of this properly.

### Rapid chatter at full dark, with soft landings — flying squirrels

Less common, frequently misidentified, and worth knowing about. [Flying squirrels](/pest-library/flying-squirrels/) are strictly nocturnal — activity starts at full dark, not dusk — and the sound is a fast, light, almost bird-like chatter with quick movement. You may hear a soft thump as one lands after a glide.

They also **den communally in winter**, which means you may not have one animal, you may have a dozen. People often mistake them for mice because of the sound and for squirrels because of the season.

### Squeaking and rustling at dusk, from a wall or soffit — bats

A high squeaking and a dry rustle at dusk, coming from a wall cavity, a soffit, or the peak, along with **staining at an exterior gap** and small dark droppings collecting below it, points to [bats](/pest-library/bats/).

Bats are the one case where the calendar is legally significant. During the summer maternity season, flightless pups are in the roost, and exclusion at that time both kills the pups and fails as a solution. We have written this up in full: [Wisconsin's bat maternity season, explained](/blog/wisconsin-bat-maternity-season-explained/), and the [Wisconsin bat exclusion law page](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/) covers the regulatory side.

### Nothing at all, but a smell — something died

Sometimes the call is a smell rather than a sound. A dead animal in a wall or attic produces a strong odor for a period of weeks and draws blow flies, which then show up in the living space and are usually how people discover it. Finding and removing the carcass is the fix; masking it is not.

## Why October

All of this concentrates in the fall for one simple reason: **everything is shopping for a winter den.**

From late September through November, squirrels, raccoons, mice, and bats are all evaluating shelter for the cold months, and a heated attic with a gap at the soffit is the best available option for a quarter-mile in any direction. Add the year's young dispersing to find their own space, and the fall is when your roofline gets tested.

The animal has usually been assessing your house for weeks before you hear anything. The noise starts when it moves in, not when it arrives.

## What the damage actually costs

**The wiring is the part to take seriously.** Squirrels and mice gnaw continuously to manage teeth that never stop growing, and in an attic the available material includes electrical cable. Chewed insulation on a live conductor is a fire risk. If you have had squirrels or mice in an attic, having the wiring looked at is not overcautious.

**Insulation is the quiet cost.** Animals compact it, tunnel through it, and soil it. Compacted insulation has lost much of its R-value, so you pay for it every month of the heating season without ever seeing a line item for it.

**Structural and mechanical.** Torn ductwork, chewed plumbing lines, damaged vent stacks, gnawed sheathing and rafters, and — with raccoons — soffit and roof-edge damage that then lets water in.

**Contamination.** Raccoon latrines and long-established bat roosts require proper cleanup rather than a shop vac and good intentions.

**And the next tenant.** A hole that let one animal in will let the next one in. Every attic job that ends without the roofline being properly closed is a job you will repeat.

## The three mistakes

**1. Sealing the hole first.** This is the big one, and it is almost always done with good intentions.

In spring and early summer, an adult female entering an attic is very often a mother with dependent young somewhere back in the insulation. Seal the entrance and she is locked out and they starve in your ceiling — inhumane, and practically speaking a severe odor problem plus a fly and dermestid beetle problem inside your insulation for months.

Seal a **raccoon** in, at any time of year, and it will simply make a new hole. Through soffit, through drywall, through ductwork. Raccoons are strong and highly motivated.

Confirm the space is empty — and that there are no young — before anything gets closed.

**2. Repellents and gadgets.** Ultrasonic devices have not been shown to drive wildlife out of structures, and animals habituate to noise and light within days. Mothballs and ammonia in an attic push fumes down through the ceiling into your bedrooms and do not reliably move anything. Every dollar spent here is a dollar not spent on the exclusion work that actually solves it.

**3. Going up there yourself.** Attics are dark, hot or freezing, full of things to fall through, and the animal is cornered and frightened. Raccoons in particular can injure a person. Add health considerations around droppings and roost material and it is simply not a reasonable place to go poking around.

## What a proper job looks like

**Inspect first — outside as well as in.** The entry point is found from the exterior, by reading the roofline and the sign around it: rub marks, staining, disturbed insulation visible through a gap, damaged vent screening, claw marks, droppings below an opening. There is often more than one, and the secondary ones are what make a first attempt fail.

**Identify the species and the season**, because that determines what is legal and what is humane. Bats have their own timing. Species with dependent young have theirs.

**Get the animals out, in the way that fits the case.** Frequently the right tool is a **one-way door** fitted over the confirmed entry: the animal leaves on its own to forage and cannot get back in. No handling, no trapping stress, and no relocation question. Where young are present, they have to be dealt with together with the mother, not left behind.

**Confirm the space is actually empty** before anything gets sealed.

**Exclude properly.** Not foam in a hole. Heavy-gauge hardware cloth and metal flashing mechanically fastened, soffit and fascia repaired, vents rescreened, the roofline closed along its whole length — because the next animal will test the same building. This is the part that determines whether the job holds. Our [attic wildlife removal guide](/guides/attic-wildlife-removal/) goes into the method.

**Clean up what needs cleaning up**, and give an honest assessment of what does not. Not every attic needs full insulation replacement, and you should be wary of a quote for one written before anyone has been up there.
