## The nest was always there — August is just when you notice it

Almost nobody calls about a wasp nest in May. In May the nest is a bare gray comb the size of a walnut with a single queen sitting on it, tucked up under the porch roof where nobody looks. By the second week of August the same nest is the size of a dinner plate with a couple of hundred workers on it, everybody is eating on the deck, and it has become the most urgent problem on the property.

That is the seasonal shape of stinging-insect work in Northeast Wisconsin, and it is why our [stinging insect calls](/residential-pest-control/stinging-insects/) cluster so hard into July, August, and September. The colony has been growing all summer on a curve that only becomes obvious at the top of it.

Understanding that curve is the whole basis for deciding what to do. So is knowing what you are actually looking at, because "wasp nest" covers four very different situations with four very different levels of risk.

## Know which one you have — it changes everything

### Paper wasps: the open umbrella comb

This is the nest most people picture. An **open, unwrapped comb of gray hexagonal cells**, hanging from a short stalk, usually under an eave, a porch ceiling, a deck rail, inside a grill you have not opened since spring, or in the frame of a swing set. You can see the cells and you can see the wasps standing on them — typically a modest number, often under thirty even in late summer.

[Paper wasps](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) are the least aggressive of the group. They defend the nest if you get close, but they do not patrol and they do not pursue far. Their nests stay comparatively small. A homeowner can reasonably deal with a small paper wasp nest in an accessible spot, at dusk, with an aerosol rated for the distance and a clear path to walk away.

The complication is location. A paper wasp nest inside the handle of a patio door, in a mailbox, or under the rail you grab going down the deck steps is a stinging incident waiting to happen regardless of the species' temperament, because you are going to put your hand on it eventually.

### Bald-faced hornets: the gray football in the tree

If you are looking at a **large, fully enclosed, teardrop or football-shaped gray paper envelope** with a single opening near the bottom, you have [bald-faced hornets](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) — which, despite the name, are a large black-and-white yellowjacket rather than a true hornet.

This is the one to leave alone. Bald-faced hornets build big — a mature nest can hold several hundred workers — they are strongly defensive of the nest area rather than just the nest itself, and they will pursue an intruder well away from it. They can sting repeatedly. Vibration alone, from a lawnmower or a ladder set against the same tree, is enough to bring them out.

There is no version of a homeowner successfully spraying a mature bald-faced hornet nest from a stepladder that we want to be part of.

### Yellowjackets in the ground: the nest you cannot see

[Yellowjackets](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) build both aerial and subterranean nests, and out here in farm and field country the ground nests are the ones that cause trouble. The colony takes over an old rodent burrow or a void under a stump, a slab, a bale, or the edge of a shed, and the only sign is wasps streaming in and out of a hole.

Every summer this is the same call: somebody was mowing, weed-whipping, tilling a garden edge, or moving firewood, and the ground erupted. Because the nest is invisible until it is disturbed, you get no warning and no chance to back off first.

Ground nests are also the worst candidate for DIY treatment, for a reason that is worth stating plainly: **you cannot see how big it is.** A garden-hose-and-gasoline approach — and people do try it — is dangerous, illegal to do with fuel, and frequently fails while enraging several hundred wasps at ankle height.

### Yellowjackets in a wall or soffit

The other yellowjacket problem is structural. The colony sets up in a wall void, a soffit, a box gutter, or behind fascia, with the workers using a gap in the siding or a weep hole as the door.

The mistake almost everyone makes here is to **seal the hole**. It seems obvious — block the door, problem over. What actually happens is that a few hundred wasps that cannot get out the way they came in start chewing, and the softest material between them and daylight is very often the drywall on the inside of your bedroom.

The entrance has to stay open until the colony is dead. Seal it after, not before.

## The late-summer aggression is real and it has a cause

People are not imagining that wasps get worse in August. Two things change at the same time.

First, the colony is at its peak. A nest that had a dozen workers in June has hundreds by late August, so the same amount of disturbance produces a much larger response.

Second, the food supply flips. Through most of the summer, adult workers hunt insects to feed the larvae, and in exchange the larvae produce a sugary secretion the adults live on. Late in the season the queen stops laying worker brood, the larvae run out, and that sugar supply disappears. The workers go looking for sugar somewhere else — and somewhere else is your can of pop, the sweet corn on the picnic table, the fallen apples under the tree, the garbage can by the garage, and the fruit in the orchard.

That is why late-summer wasps get in your face at a cookout when the same wasps ignored you in June. They are not defending anything. They are foraging, and you are the sugar.

## Which nests can actually wait until frost

A Wisconsin wasp or hornet colony is **annual**. It does not survive winter. Hard frost kills the workers and the founding queen, and only the newly mated queens live on, overwintering somewhere sheltered — under bark, in a woodpile, in a wall void, sometimes in an attic — to start over alone in spring. The nest is not reused the next year.

That fact is practically useful, because it means some nests genuinely can be left alone:

- **High in a tree, well away from the house, walkways, play areas, and the mowing line.** Nobody is going to bump it. It will be empty by November and can come down over winter.
- **In a fencerow or woodlot corner of a farm property** that nobody works close to for the rest of the season.

And some cannot wait, no matter how late in the year it is:

- **Anywhere within about ten feet of a door, a walkway, a deck, a patio, or a play area.** Proximity, not species, is the deciding factor here.
- **In the ground anywhere you mow, till, or work.** Equipment vibration is the trigger.
- **Inside the structure** — wall, soffit, attic, box gutter.
- **Anywhere on a property with someone who has a known sting allergy.** There is no acceptable level of risk in that case, and it is worth saying to us on the phone so it is treated as urgent rather than routine.
- **Around livestock, a horse paddock, or a kennel run**, where a disturbed ground nest can cause a serious animal injury and a panicked animal can hurt someone.

## What actually works, and what to skip

**The hardware-store aerosol.** These have a place: a small, open, accessible paper wasp nest, treated after dark when the colony is home and calm, from the maximum distance the can supports, with the wind at your back and a clear route inside. They are not a solution for an enclosed nest, a nest you have to climb to, or a ground nest.

**Do it at dusk or before dawn, never midday.** Foragers are out during the day, which means the ones you did not kill come home to a wrecked nest and no reason to be calm. At night the colony is together and quiet.

**Never on a ladder.** This is worth its own line, because the worst outcomes we hear about are not sting injuries. They are fall injuries. Being stung once at the top of an extension ladder is how people break bones. If it needs a ladder, it needs somebody else.

**Skip the traps as a control method.** Hanging yellowjacket traps can reduce the nuisance foragers around a specific spot — a deck, a dumpster, a serving area — and that is a legitimate use. What they do not do is kill the colony. Baited traps placed close to the house can also pull more wasps toward the area than they catch. Put them at the far edge of the space you are protecting, not next to the picnic table.

**Skip the fake-nest decoys and the gasoline and the burning.** The decoys have no reliable effect. Fuel in a ground nest is dangerous, contaminates soil, and is illegal to use as a pesticide.

## Prevention for next year, which starts in spring

The single highest-value thing you can do about wasps is not done in August at all. It is done in **late April and May**, when the founding queens are out looking for a site and the nests are the size of a walnut with one wasp on them.

- **Walk the eaves, soffits, porch ceilings, deck rails, and outbuildings once a week in May.** A nest at that stage is trivially removed.
- **Open and check the things that sat closed all winter** before you need them: the grill, the playset tubes, the mailbox, the meter box, the shed, the boat cover, the empty flowerpots stacked by the garage.
- **Seal the gaps that let them into voids** — torn soffit vents, gaps at fascia and frieze board, unscreened attic and gable vents, siding gaps at utility penetrations. Same work that keeps [fall invaders](/residential-pest-control/fall-invaders/) and [bats](/pest-library/bats/) out, done once.
- **Keep the sugar sources managed** through late summer: lidded garbage cans, cleaned recycling, fallen fruit picked up, spills rinsed off the deck.
- **Look before you mow.** Especially along field edges, banks, stumps, and the ground next to outbuildings.

Ten minutes with a broom handle in May is the cheapest stinging-insect control there is.
