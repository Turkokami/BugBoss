## In this town, commercial work mostly means buildings full of apartments

Menasha's older rental stock, its converted mill-era buildings, its duplexes and small apartment buildings are the bulk of the commercial pest work here. And they all share one feature that decides the outcome of every job: **the units are not separate buildings.**

That sounds obvious and it is routinely ignored, including by companies that should know better. It is the single reason bed bug and cockroach problems in a town like this run for years while everyone involved concludes that pest control does not work.

## The pattern, and why nobody in it is being unreasonable

A tenant in unit 4 reports [bed bugs](/pest-library/bed-bugs/). Unit 4 is treated. Six weeks later unit 3 reports them. Unit 3 is treated. A few months on, unit 4 has them again.

By the second year the owner believes the tenants keep re-introducing them and the tenants believe the owner is cheaping out on the treatment. Both are acting sensibly on what they can see. Both are wrong.

What is actually happening is that the population is **moving through the building** — along plumbing chases, electrical runs, shared wall voids and hallways — staying permanently ahead of a treatment that is always aimed at one unit at a time. Every single treatment worked. **The scope was wrong.**

The same mechanism drives [cockroaches](/pest-library/cockroaches/), which travel identical routes, and rodents, which add the unsealed gaps around old plumbing penetrations between floors.

## What a whole-building job actually involves

1. **An inspection that establishes which units are genuinely involved**, not which ones have complained. It is nearly always more than the ones you know about, because reporting is uneven and some people do not react to bites at all.
2. **Prepared units.** Laundering on high heat, mattress and box spring encasements, clutter reduced around beds, beds pulled off the walls. A job done in unprepared units mostly fails, so we will walk both you and the tenants through it.
3. **Treating the involved units together**, on one schedule, so there is nowhere to relocate to.
4. **At least one follow-up**, because eggs hatch after the first visit and something has to be there when they do. One visit does not finish a bed bug building, and anyone who says otherwise is selling.
5. **Coordinated access**, which on multi-unit work is genuinely the most common failure point. Not treatment — access.

## The responsibility question

Wisconsin has **no bed-bug-specific statute**. Responsibility comes from the landlord's duty to keep the property habitable, the terms of the lease, and who introduced the infestation — which, with bed bugs, is essentially never provable.

In practice the landlord normally has to arrange treatment and the tenant has to prepare the unit and allow access. Our [Wisconsin landlord and tenant pest control](/compliance/landlord-tenant-pest-control/) and [bed bugs in rentals](/compliance/bed-bugs-in-rentals/) pages go through it.

The practical point for an owner: **arguing about blame while it spreads is how a one-unit problem becomes an eight-unit one.** The cost of the job is largely a function of how long it ran before somebody scoped it correctly.
