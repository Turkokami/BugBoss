## Two kinds of wildlife work in one town

Manawa sits on the **Little Wolf River** with **Bear Lake** north of it, and that produces a split you do not get in a landlocked village.

There is the ordinary structural work — bats, raccoons and squirrels in attics, skunks and groundhogs under decks — which is the same job here as anywhere. And there is **water-corridor work**, which is a genuinely different discipline: muskrats undermining banks, beavers plugging culverts and flooding low ground, and the drainage and legal questions that come with both.

## The structural side

**[Bats](/pest-library/bats/)** — squeaking and a dry rustle at dusk from a wall, soffit or the peak; staining at an exterior gap where bodies pass repeatedly; small dark droppings collecting below it. Older village homes and lake cottages both. This one has a binding calendar (below).

**[Raccoons](/pest-library/raccoons/)** — heavy, deliberate movement after dark, dragging, sometimes vocal. Attics, chimneys, under decks and cottages. The most destructive of the group and the most likely to injure a person or a dog. Between March and June, assume young until ruled out.

**[Squirrels](/pest-library/squirrels/)** — scratching and a rolling sound at dawn and dusk, silence overnight. Roof edges and soffits, usually where an ice dam already lifted the fascia. Chewed wiring is the part to take seriously.

**[Skunks](/pest-library/skunks/) and [groundhogs](/pest-library/groundhogs/)** — under decks, stoops, sheds and cottages. Look at the entry, and look for young, before sealing anything.

The time of day the noise happens does most of the identification before anybody climbs: [reading the noise](/blog/squirrels-raccoons-in-the-attic/).

## The water-corridor side

**[Muskrats](/pest-library/muskrats/)** burrow into banks and under retaining structures with entrances below the waterline. The damage is invisible until a bank slumps or a soft spot appears near the water's edge — you rarely see the animal, because they are quiet and mostly nocturnal.

**[Beavers](/pest-library/beavers/)** are a water-management problem wearing an animal costume. A dam plugging a culvert can flood low ground, a field access, or a road approach, and taking the animal without addressing the dam, the culvert and the drainage solves nothing for long. Wisconsin regulates work on beavers and on waterways, so what we will do is tell you plainly what applies to your situation and what a realistic outcome looks like — rather than quote you a trap count and let you find out afterward.

## Bats: the calendar is binding

Wisconsin protects its bats, and exclusion is prohibited during the summer maternity season while flightless pups are in the roost. Sealing then kills the pups inside the structure — illegal, inhumane, and a far worse problem for the owner than the colony was, since what follows is odor and insects in the insulation for months.

A June or July call therefore gets an inspection, a full plan and a booked date rather than immediate work. Detail: the [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/) and [why you cannot evict bats in June and July](/blog/wisconsin-bat-maternity-season-explained/).

## Removal is the easy half

Take tonight's raccoon and the torn soffit it used is still open next week — and it is the same opening that gives you [squirrels](/pest-library/squirrels/) in October, [wasps](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) in July and [cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/) in September. One repair closes all of it.

**The sequence:** inspect from outside first, because the entry is found by reading the building rather than starting in the attic, and there is usually more than one. Identify species and season. Get them out, generally with one-way devices so the animal leaves to forage and cannot return. **Confirm the space is empty before closing anything.** Exclude with hardware cloth and flashing, mechanically fastened, along the whole roofline. Then clean up what needs it.

## Seasonal property

Lake and river cottages that sit empty get colonized precisely because nobody is there to notice the first week of it. Physical exclusion is the only thing that works on an unoccupied building, since it keeps working whether or not anybody visits. Pair it with a proper [closing routine](/blog/cabin-closing-pest-checklist/) in the fall.
