## We take this work selectively, and it is worth saying which work

Green Bay has plenty of commercial pest companies with shorter drives and bigger fleets. For a straightforward monthly service on a straightforward building, one of them is probably your answer, and we will say so on the phone.

What we take on here is narrower:

- **Multi-unit and property management** where the problem has run for years because it was scoped one unit at a time.
- **Buildings with a wildlife or exclusion component** — birds on the parapet, bats in an older structure, an animal in a roof void — which most metro pest routes will not handle.
- **Owners who want the person doing the work to be the person accountable for it**, rather than a technician who changes every season.

## The scope problem, which is the real problem in this city

A large share of Green Bay's commercial pest work is in **shared structures** — apartment buildings, converted older stock, mixed-use downtown blocks, senior housing. And in a shared building the boundary between "unit 4's problem" and "the building's problem" is not the wall. It is nothing at all.

The pattern repeats almost exactly. Unit 4 reports [bed bugs](/pest-library/bed-bugs/). Unit 4 gets treated. Six weeks later unit 3 reports them. Unit 3 gets treated. By the second year the owner believes tenants keep re-introducing them and the tenants believe the owner is using a company that cannot do the job.

Nobody in that story is being unreasonable and nobody is right. The population is **moving through the building** — plumbing chases, electrical runs, shared wall voids, hallways — staying ahead of a treatment always aimed at one unit at a time. Each treatment worked. The scope was wrong.

Same mechanism for [cockroaches](/pest-library/cockroaches/), and for rodents using the same routes plus the gaps around old plumbing penetrations.

We took an [eight-unit building with years of bed bugs to zero](/case-studies/eight-plex-bed-bug-elimination/) by treating it as one job. That is the whole method. See also [property management](/commercial-pest-control/property-management/), and the Wisconsin [landlord and tenant](/compliance/landlord-tenant-pest-control/) and [bed bugs in rentals](/compliance/bed-bugs-in-rentals/) pages for the responsibility question.

## Birds, and the legal question that comes first

Birds on **signage, parapets, ledges and loading canopies** are a routine commercial call in a city this size, and it is one of the few where the law is the first thing to settle rather than the last.

**Starlings, pigeons and house sparrows** are non-native and are not protected under the Migratory Bird Treaty Act, so they can be excluded and removed directly. **Native birds are protected**, and acting on a misidentification is a federal violation rather than a technicality.

So every bird job starts by confirming which species is actually causing the problem. After that it is exclusion and deterrence — netting, spikes, ledge modification — plus cleanup, and dealing with whatever is drawing them, which is often food waste handling. [Nuisance birds](/pest-library/nuisance-birds/).

**Every visit produces a work order** — what was inspected, what was found, what was treated and when. That is the record an inspector normally asks to see.

Finding that gap during an audit is avoidable in one conversation at the outset.

## What we will tell you not to buy

If your building's actual problem is a dock seal, a floor drain nobody scrubs, a cardboard storage habit, or a dumpster sited against the wall, a recurring treatment manages the symptom indefinitely and never ends it.

We would rather identify that and say so, even when the honest recommendation is a repair and a change of practice rather than a service agreement. There is [no contract](/no-contracts/) on the table, which makes that a much easier conversation than it is for a route company.
