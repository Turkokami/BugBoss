## Out here, wildlife work means outbuildings

In a village, a wildlife call is almost always about a house. Around Marion it is as likely to be about a **barn, a granary, a machine shed, a coop, or a milk house** — and those are a different problem from an attic.

The reason is structural. A house can be sealed. A century-old dairy barn, with open gables, board gaps, and a loft the size of a small warehouse, largely cannot — not economically, and not honestly. Anybody who quotes you full exclusion on an old barn is selling something they cannot deliver.

What works instead is **deciding what actually has to be protected** and sealing that: the feed room, the milk house, the tack room, the office, the part of the loft you use. The rest gets treated as what it is, which is a covered outdoor space. On the farmhouse itself, full exclusion is absolutely worth doing and it holds.

## The animals, and what tells you which

**[Bats](/pest-library/bats/)** — squeaking and a dry rustle at dusk, staining at an exterior gap, droppings collecting below a roost. Barn lofts and farmhouse attics both. This is the one with a legal calendar; see below.

**[Raccoons](/pest-library/raccoons/)** — heavy, deliberate movement after dark, dragging, chittering. In outbuildings, chimneys, and under floors. The most destructive of the group, the most likely to injure a person or a dog, and the most likely to have a litter somewhere behind it in spring.

**[Squirrels](/pest-library/squirrels/)** — scratching and rolling at dawn and dusk, silence overnight. Soffits and roof edges, usually where the fascia has already been opened by an ice dam.

**[Skunks](/pest-library/skunks/) and [groundhogs](/pest-library/groundhogs/)** — under decks, stoops, granary floors and shed slabs. Look at the entry before sealing anything: a burrow closed with young inside is the outcome nobody wants.

**[Woodpeckers](/pest-library/woodpeckers/)** — on wood-sided buildings, and worth knowing that native woodpeckers are federally protected, so the toolkit is deterrence, exclusion, insect control and repair rather than removal. Often the bird is there because there are insects in the siding, which is the real thing to fix.

Longer version of the identification: [reading the noise before you call anyone](/blog/squirrels-raccoons-in-the-attic/).

## Bats: the calendar is binding

Wisconsin protects its bats, and exclusion is prohibited during the summer maternity season while flightless pups are in the roost. Seal a roost then and the pups die inside the structure — illegal, inhumane, and a far worse problem for the owner than the colony was.

So a June call around here gets an inspection, a full plan, and a booked date rather than immediate work. We would rather say that and lose the momentum than do it wrong. Detail: the [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/) and [why you cannot evict bats in June and July](/blog/wisconsin-bat-maternity-season-explained/).

## Removal is the easy half

Almost every call arrives as "get this animal out," and almost every one is really "close this hole."

Remove tonight's raccoon and the torn soffit it used is still open next week — and it is the same opening that gives you [squirrels](/pest-library/squirrels/) in October, [wasps](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) in July, and [cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/) in September. One repair closes all of it, which is why we quote the whole job rather than the removal alone.

The sequence: inspect from outside first, identify species and season, get them out with one-way devices where possible, **confirm the space is empty before closing anything**, exclude with hardware cloth and flashing that is mechanically fastened rather than foam in a hole, then clean up what needs it.

## Why we do this at all

Most pest control companies do not do wildlife. They treat insects and rodents and refer an animal in a wall out — to a trapper who removes it and leaves, or to nobody. Out here in Waupaca and Shawano County there frequently is nobody, so we learned to do the animal and the building as one job.

That is also why we can handle a farmstead as a whole: the bats in the barn, the raccoon in the chimney, the [rodents in the pole barn](/blog/mice-in-the-pole-barn/) and the fall invaders on the farmhouse are one property and one visit, not four contractors.
