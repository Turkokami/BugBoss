Clintonville is where BugBoss is based — the shop is at **600 S. Main St.** — so this is the one town where we are not driving in. For mosquito work that turns out to matter more than it does for most pests, and the reason is timing.

## The river is not the problem

Start here, because it saves money.

**Mosquitoes do not breed in moving water.** The larvae need standing water that sits undisturbed for several days. The **Pigeon River** running through the village is not producing them. What it does is raise local humidity and provide shade, both of which let adult mosquitoes live longer — so river-adjacent properties genuinely do have a higher baseline. But the controllable part is almost entirely inside your own lot line.

On a village property the productive water is a short and depressingly familiar list:

- Clogged gutters — the most common single source we find, in Clintonville and everywhere else
- Corrugated downspout extensions, which hold water in the ribs even on a slope
- A tarp over a boat, trailer or woodpile with a pocket in it
- Plant saucers, buckets, wheelbarrows, bin lids, kids' toys
- Birdbaths that nobody changes
- Tires behind the garage
- The low spot in the lawn that holds for a week after a heavy rain

A bottle cap's worth of water is enough for some species. This is why the twenty-minute walk-round decides the outcome and the spray does not.

## Where the adults actually spend the day

The second thing people get wrong is aiming at the open lawn.

Adult mosquitoes do not sit in the middle of a mown yard in daylight. They rest in **cool, shaded vegetation** — under shrub foliage, in dense ground cover, in tall grass at the yard edge, against the shaded north side of the house and the garage, and along any wooded margin. That is where a barrier treatment belongs, which is why the area we treat often looks nothing like the patio where you were being bitten.

## Three kinds of Clintonville property

**The older blocks near downtown and the river.** Mature trees, deep shade, and a lot of gutter. Shade is the asset and the liability at once: it keeps the house cool and it keeps adult mosquitoes alive through the afternoon.

**The mid-century neighborhoods.** Generally the easiest. Open lawns, less shaded harborage, and the sources are usually three or four containers and a downspout.

**The farm properties on the edge.** Here the walk takes longer. Ditches, tile outlets, stock tanks, the tire pile behind the machine shed, and the corner of the yard that never drains — plus fence rows and treelines giving the adults somewhere to sit out the day. Work that stops at the lawn edge gets undone by the field margin.

## Timing beats frequency

This is the part being in town lets us actually deliver.

Significant rain fills every container and low spot in the village simultaneously, and roughly a week later you get a **synchronised hatch**. A treatment timed to that is worth several applied on a fixed calendar regardless of weather. A company routing a truck up from Appleton fits you into a schedule; we can look at the forecast and move.

The season runs from sustained spring warmth to the first hard frosts, with the heaviest pressure through midsummer and a reliable surge after every real rain.

## What we will tell you honestly

That there is a ceiling. A property backing onto a wet field margin or a marshy low area will never be as quiet as a house in the middle of the village, no matter what is applied. We would rather set that expectation in June than have you feel misled in August.

And that if the lot is wooded, **[ticks](/pest-library/ticks/)** are frequently the more consequential problem of the two.
