Appleton's tick problem is a city problem, and people are routinely surprised by that because Lyme disease gets filed mentally under "up north".

## The corridors bring it into town

Appleton is cut by **ravines and creek corridors**, and they run right through residential, school and commercial ground.

Those corridors are continuous strips of shaded, humid, unmown vegetation with leaf litter underneath — which is exactly what a deer tick needs to survive between hosts. They are also travel routes for the deer and small mammals that carry ticks around.

The result is that the risk people associate with a weekend in the woods is available half a mile from a grocery store, at the back of an ordinary lot.

## The band, which is narrower than you think

This is the single most useful thing on this page.

Ticks quest from vegetation at roughly **ankle to knee height**, in shaded humid cover. They cannot survive long in open sun on a mown lawn — it is too hot and too dry. They are also not spread evenly through deep woods.

They are concentrated in the **yard or two where mown turf meets brush**, leaf litter, or unmown margin.

That concentration is the whole basis of a workable program. A defined band is treatable in a way an entire landscape is not.

## What a program actually does

1. **Walk the property line** and find the real boundary band. On a lot backing onto a ravine it is the back fence; on an apartment property it may follow a creek corridor or a buffer strip between buildings.
2. **Treat that band**, timed to the nymph window rather than applied on a fixed interval across the season.
3. **Do the habitat work**, which is the durable half.
4. **Say where the limit is.** A property backing onto a protected corridor carries a permanently raised baseline, and we are not going to treat a greenway that is not yours.

## The habitat half, which costs almost nothing

Worth doing whether or not anybody treats:

- **Mow the margin** instead of letting it go to brush
- **Clear leaf litter** back from the lawn edge
- **Move the woodpile and the play equipment** away from the boundary
- **Put in a dry break** — three feet of gravel or wood chip between turf and brush. Ticks desiccate crossing it, and it works every year without reapplication

## The deer tick, and the clock

The one that matters is the **deer tick** (blacklegged tick), and it is the small one: a nymph is 1–3 mm, an unfed adult 3–5 mm, with a solid dark shield and an orange-red body. The larger mottled **American dog tick** is far more noticeable and does not transmit Lyme.

In Wisconsin the deer tick transmits **Lyme disease and anaplasmosis**, and transmission risk rises sharply once a tick has been attached roughly **24 to 36 hours**.

That number is why the daily check matters more than any treatment we can sell. A tick found in a few hours is a different situation from one found on the third day, and that part is entirely in your hands. Check behind the knees, the waistband, the hairline, behind the ears — and the same on children and dogs after any time in the boundary band.

**Nymphs transmit most Lyme cases**, because at 1–3 mm they go unnoticed long enough to matter. They peak through late spring and early summer, and that is the window worth timing work to.
