Bear Creek is small enough that the mosquito problem is not the village's. It belongs to the farmland around it.

## The honest version first

On most lots in a village this size, the answer is a bucket and twenty minutes.

Clear the gutters, tip the wheelbarrow, drain the tarp on the woodpile, empty the plant saucers, deal with the downspout extension that holds water in its ribs. On a small lot that is frequently the whole problem, and it costs nothing.

We would rather say that than sell a program. A treatment earns its place on a property with genuine shaded harborage, on a lot backing straight onto wet field margin, or where somebody has already done all of the above and is still being driven indoors.

## What the farmland contributes

The productive water around Bear Creek is agricultural and almost none of it is treatable:

- Field drainage ditches and tile outlets holding water after rain
- The low corner of a field that never dries out
- Stock tanks, and the churned pooled ground around them
- Tires weighting bunker covers — individually small, collectively one of the most productive containers in Wisconsin agriculture
- Equipment left out with cavities that hold rain

It is within easy flying distance of the village, it belongs to other people, and it is the reason a place this size sees pressure that a larger town with less surrounding agriculture would not.

## The creek is not breeding them

Worth stating because it is the first assumption everybody makes. **Flowing water produces nothing** — larvae cannot complete development in current.

What produces is the still water beside it: sheltered pockets out of the flow, low ground along the bank that holds after a rise, anywhere vegetation shelters standing water from the wind.

## Why the back of the lot is worse

Because the shade is there.

Adults spend daylight in **cool shaded vegetation** — the brushy boundary, the fence line, tall grass, whatever trees are on the lot edge. They are not in the open lawn. You walk into the harborage and the harborage responds, which is why the back corner feels like a different property from the patio.

It is also exactly where a barrier treatment goes, when one is warranted. If your lot has no shaded edge at all, a treatment will do very little, because there was nothing to treat and the mosquitoes are arriving from the fields at dusk.

## Follow the rain

Not the calendar. A significant rainfall fills every ditch, container and low spot simultaneously, and about a week later a **synchronised hatch** arrives. A treatment landing in that window is worth two applied on a schedule regardless of weather.

For most properties out here that means a handful of applications across a season, not a standing monthly visit.

And in this landscape, **[ticks](/pest-library/ticks/)** are usually the more serious of the two — same field edges, same treated band, same visit.
