Howard has two mosquito problems running at once, and they come from opposite directions: an old creek corridor and new construction.

## The Duck Creek corridor

The flowing channel breeds nothing — **larvae cannot complete development in current.**

The corridor around it is a different matter. Low wet margins, backwater pockets, ground that holds after a rise, and a long continuous run of dense shaded vegetation. That last part is the one people underestimate: it is where adults spend the daylight hours before coming out at dusk.

A property backing onto the corridor gets production and harborage together, which is why it runs worse than a lot two streets inland, and why the treated band on those lots is the back boundary rather than the patio.

## Why the new subdivisions are worse

This genuinely surprises people, and it is consistent.

A recent subdivision built on former farmland is frequently a heavier mosquito environment than a fifty-year-old street, for the first several seasons:

- **Grade has not settled.** Compacted construction soil sheds water into low spots that were not designed to be low spots.
- **Drainage is unestablished**, so water sits where it will eventually run.
- **Retention basins** may be holding a permanent shallow vegetated pool rather than draining down.
- **Builder-grade gutter and downspout work** nobody has yet discovered is holding water.

It generally improves as the grading settles and the planting matures. In the meantime it is a problem and it is worth treating as one.

## Retention basins: it depends

Not all of them produce. A basin that drains down within a couple of days is not a significant source. One holding a shallow, still, vegetated pool with no fish and no flow is an excellent nursery sitting in the middle of your neighborhood.

It is also common ground, which makes it an association or municipal conversation rather than something a homeowner can address. Worth identifying which kind yours is before anybody treats anything.

## What you can still do when the source is next door

More than it sounds.

Even where the production is in the basin down the street or on the corridor behind you, the adults have to spend daylight **somewhere**. If your property offers no cool shaded vegetation, they will largely be somewhere else.

That is the entire mechanism behind a barrier treatment, and it is why it works on lots where source reduction alone cannot.

## The on-lot list, newer-house version

- Gutters — on a newer house everyone assumes they are fine, and they are the most common source we find
- The corrugated downspout extension holding water in its ribs
- The low spot where the builder's grade did not quite work
- Plant saucers, buckets, wheelbarrows, bin lids
- A birdbath nobody empties, and tires
- The tarp over whatever is stored at the side of the garage

A week of undisturbed water is the whole life cycle.

## Where the treatment goes, and when it is not worth it

Into **shaded vegetation**: shrub undersides, dense ground cover, tall grass at the lot edge, the shaded sides of the house and garage, the creek or wooded margin.

On a newer lot with young planting there is sometimes very little harborage to treat. When that is the case we will tell you the treatment is not worth much and the source reduction is — which is a cheaper answer and the correct one.
