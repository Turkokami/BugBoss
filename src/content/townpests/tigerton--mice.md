Tigerton is a small Shawano County village on the **Embarrass River**, ringed by farmland and woodlots. It is a short run from Clintonville and one of the towns we cover most regularly, and the work here is dominated by one thing: **rodent exclusion**.

## Pressure from two sides

What makes Tigerton properties distinctive is that they often sit between two different sources.

**The farmland** supplies the agricultural version — grain, feed, pole barns, machine sheds, spillage, and the year-round population those support.

**The woodlots** supply the woodland version — mice moving in from timber and brush as the weather turns, with cover right up to the property line.

A house with a field on one boundary and woods on another is getting both, which is common here. It is also why Tigerton properties frequently need more thorough sealing than the raw numbers suggest: you are not closing one approach, you are closing the building.

The **Embarrass River** corridor adds to it. River banks give rodents cover and a travel route, and the vegetation supports populations that move into buildings when it cools.

## Sealing beats baiting, and it is not close

Bait removes some of the animals that are already inside. It does nothing about the gap they came through, so the space refills from an outside population that does not run out.

That is the whole reason people bait the same farmhouse every autumn for a decade and conclude this is just what rural life is like.

Both have a role — control to knock down what is in, exclusion so it does not refill — but if only one gets done, sealing is the one that still matters in three years.

## Where they get in

On an older Tigerton farmhouse it is a consistent list:

- The **sill plate seam** on a stone or block foundation, often gapping along a whole run
- **Utility penetrations** — well line, electrical, plumbing — rarely sealed, often enlarged over the years
- **Basement window frames** shifted in old masonry
- The **outside cellar entry**
- The join where a **later addition or enclosed porch** meets the original structure — the single most productive place to look

A mouse needs a gap about the width of a pencil, so most of these are invisible until someone gets underneath with a light.

## The pole barn is managed, not sealed

Worth being straightforward about. Pole buildings are hard to seal completely — the base of the sheeting, the header gaps, the sliding door tracks all defeat a perfect job.

What is achievable: close the worst routes, get feed and stored goods into hard sealed containers, pull stacked material off the walls so the perimeter can be seen and cleaned, and run control on the remainder. The **farmhouse** is where the sealing genuinely pays off, and that is where the effort goes.

## Timing, and the follow-up

**September, not November.** Sealing ahead of the influx is worth more than any product choice, and almost nobody calls then — the calls come once the noise starts in the walls.

And exclusion on an older rural property is rarely one visit. There is a seal, a control period, and a **follow-up to check the work**. Tigerton being twenty minutes from the shop makes that easy to actually do, which matters more than it sounds: exclusion that never gets checked is exclusion with a hole in it.
