Marion straddles the Waupaca–Shawano county line in the middle of dairy country, and that shapes the mouse work here more than anything about the houses themselves.

## The population is not in the house

This is the thing most people have backwards. On a Marion farmstead the mice are not visitors that came in for the winter — they are residents of the property, and the house is simply one building among several that they use.

The population lives where the food is: **pole barns, grain storage, feed rooms, and the equipment sheds** where spillage collects and stacked machinery gives endless cover. It persists year-round because nothing about that food supply is seasonal. What is seasonal is your awareness of it, which peaks in October when the cold pushes some of them into the farmhouse and you start hearing them in the walls.

Treat the kitchen and you have removed a handful of animals from a population being replenished from a building fifty yards away. That is why people set traps in the same farmhouse every autumn for years and conclude that mice are just part of living out here.

They are not. But the work has to happen on the property, not in the pantry.

## Where they get into a Marion farmhouse

The older farmhouses around Marion share a fairly consistent set of routes, and once you have crawled a few of them you know where to look:

- The seam where the **sill plate meets a stone or block foundation** — often a continuous gap under the whole run
- **Well lines, electrical conduit and plumbing penetrations**, rarely sealed properly and often opened up further over the years
- **Basement and cellar windows** whose frames have shifted in old masonry
- The **bulkhead or outside cellar entry**
- The **later addition or enclosed porch**, built on a different foundation, which nearly always leaves a seam where the two meet

A mouse gets through a gap about the width of a pencil. Most of these are invisible from standing height.

## What actually works out here

**Secure the feed first.** Hard sealed containers, pallets off the wall so behind them can be seen and cleaned, and prompt cleanup of spillage around bins and augers. Nobody gets a working farm spotless and we do not pretend otherwise — but the loose grain is usually the single biggest food source on the property and reducing it changes what the buildings can carry.

**Cut the harbourage.** Stacked material against walls, old bedding, disused equipment full of nesting sites.

**Seal the buildings**, farmhouse first. This is the part that lasts, and it is what separates a solved problem from an annual expense.

**Then control** what remains, rather than starting there.

**And do it in September**, not November. Getting ahead of the influx is worth more than any product choice.

## The wiring, and the cleanup

Two things worth saying plainly on a farm. Mice **gnaw insulation off wiring** — in barns, machine sheds, and inside vehicles and equipment left standing — and that is a fire risk and an expensive repair.

And a good proportion of what we find in Marion pole barns and closed-up outbuildings is [deer mice](/pest-library/deer-mice/) rather than house mice. That matters for the cleanup rather than the trapping: droppings and nesting material in an enclosed space should not be dry-swept. We handle that properly.
