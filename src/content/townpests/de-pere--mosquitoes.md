De Pere is built across the **Fox River**, with the **East River** joining just downstream and a dam in the middle of the city. That is a lot of water edge for a city this size, and water edge is what mosquito work turns on.

First, the thing worth saying at the top: **we are not local to De Pere.** The shop is in Clintonville and this is a drive. For scheduled seasonal work that is fine and we do plenty of it. For a same-afternoon emergency, a Brown County company will beat us and you should call one.

## Moving water is not the problem

The Fox channel produces nothing. Larvae cannot develop in current.

What produces is the still water the river creates around itself:

- **The pool above the dam**, where flow slows and the margins go quiet
- Vegetated edges sheltered from both wind and current
- Backwater pockets off the main channel
- Low ground that holds after the river rises and drops

A slow, weedy, sheltered margin is close to ideal larval habitat precisely because it is none of the things open flowing water is. It is also public water that nobody should be treating — which means on a riverfront De Pere property, your own lot and your own harborage carry more weight, not less.

The river does one other thing: it raises humidity and supplies shade. Neither breeds anything, but both let adults live longer.

## East side, west side

People ask which is worse and the honest answer is that the city boundary is not the variable.

The **East River corridor** adds slow water and a long run of shaded vegetation, so lots near it start higher. Parts of the west side sit further from any slow margin and start lower. But within either half, what actually predicts a bad property is the same three things: distance to a slow-water margin, how much shaded vegetation is on the lot, and how many containers are quietly holding water in the yard.

## Your own lot

Still where most of the controllable production is, even in a river city:

- Gutters — the single most common source we find anywhere in Wisconsin
- Corrugated downspout extensions, which hold water in the ribs on any slope
- A tarp over the boat, the trailer, the woodpile
- Plant saucers, buckets, wheelbarrows, bin lids, tires
- A birdbath nobody changes
- The low patch of lawn that stays soft a week after a storm

On a riverfront lot, add the boat cover and the kayak stored open side up. A week of undisturbed water is the whole life cycle, and some species manage in a bottle cap.

## Where a treatment actually goes

Not the lawn. Adults spend daylight in **shaded vegetation** — shrub undersides, dense ground cover, tall grass at the lot line, the north side of the house and garage, the wooded margin.

Anyone leading with fogging the open yard is selling you a repeat visit. The barrier goes where the adults actually are, which is why the treated band looks nothing like the patio.

## Scheduling from an hour away

We run De Pere in grouped visits rather than dropping them into any open slot, and we time to weather rather than to an interval.

A significant rain refills everything at once and produces a **synchronised hatch** roughly a week later. One treatment landing in that window beats two on a fixed schedule. In practice: call with some notice if you need a particular date, and expect us to occasionally suggest moving a visit because the forecast moved.
