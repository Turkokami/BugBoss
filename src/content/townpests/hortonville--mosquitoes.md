Hortonville is small, and the mosquito problem here is bigger than the village is. Two things explain it.

## A shallow impoundment in the middle of town

**Black Otter Lake** sits in the village, and a shallow impoundment behaves differently from a deep lake.

The open water is not the issue — wind and fish make poor larval habitat. The **vegetated margin** is: the weedy, sheltered edge where water stands undisturbed out of the wind for weeks. That edge genuinely produces, and properties near it carry a higher baseline than ones a few streets away.

It also supplies the other half of the equation, which people forget about: shade and humidity. Adults live longer near water regardless of where they hatched.

## Farmland on every side

The second factor is agricultural, and it works the same way everywhere in this corridor.

Field **drainage ditches**, tile outlets, the low corner of a field that never dries, stock tanks and the churned pooled ground around them — all of it holds water long enough to produce a cohort. And then the **fence rows, treelines and unmown margins** give the adults somewhere cool to sit out the daylight hours before coming back at dusk.

A Hortonville property backing onto a field is dealing with production and harborage at the same time, which is why the treatment band on those lots runs along the back of the property rather than around the patio.

## Your own lot still decides most of it

None of the above means the village lot is innocent. On the properties we walk, the controllable sources are almost always the same handful:

- Gutters, clogged — the most common single source anywhere
- Corrugated downspout extensions holding water in the ribs
- A tarp over the woodpile, the boat, the trailer
- Plant saucers, buckets, wheelbarrows, bin lids
- A birdbath nobody empties
- Tires
- The low patch of lawn that holds for a week after a storm

A bottle cap is enough for some species, and a week of undisturbed water is the entire life cycle. Clearing these costs nothing, and on a small village lot it is frequently the whole answer.

## What a treatment actually targets

Not the lawn. Adults rest through the day in **shaded vegetation**, so the application goes into shrub undersides, ground cover, the tall grass at the lot line, the north side of the house and the shed, and the field or wooded margin.

The treated band usually looks nothing like the spot where you were getting bitten, and that is the point.

## Timing over frequency

Rain is the trigger. A real rainfall refills everything simultaneously and you get a **synchronised hatch** about a week later. One treatment landing in that window beats two applied on a fixed interval.

For most properties in the village that means a handful of well-timed applications across the season rather than a standing monthly visit — which is a cheaper program, and we would rather sell you the one that works.
