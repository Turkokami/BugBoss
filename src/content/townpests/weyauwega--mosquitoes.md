Weyauwega is a small place built around still water, and that is the whole story of mosquito work here.

## A millpond is not a lake

The **millpond** on the **Waupaca River** is shallow, and shallow impoundments behave differently from deep lakes.

The open middle is not the problem. Wind across the surface and fish underneath make poor larval habitat. The **weedy margin** is the problem: water standing undisturbed among vegetation, sheltered from wind, out of reach of fish, warm and shallow. That is near-ideal.

It is also public water, and nothing anybody should be treating. What it means for a homeowner is simply that a property near the pond starts from a higher baseline than one three streets up the hill.

## The river is doing less than you think

Flowing water breeds nothing. Larvae cannot complete development in current.

What matters along the **Waupaca** is the still water beside it — backwater pockets, the quiet inside of a bend, and low ground that holds after the river rises and falls. Those produce, they are mostly not on your land, and that is why work on river-adjacent lots leans toward harborage treatment rather than source reduction.

## Why the valley is worse than the ridge

Three effects stacking, none of them anybody's fault:

- **Water collects** in low ground, supplying breeding sites
- **Humidity and cool air settle** there overnight, so adults survive longer
- **Vegetation is denser and more shaded** in a river valley, which is where adults spend the day

A property up on the higher ground will be quieter than one down by the water, and no amount of treatment fully closes that gap. Worth knowing before you compare notes with a friend across town and conclude somebody is doing something wrong.

## Your own lot, which is the part you control

The on-lot sources in Weyauwega are the same ones as everywhere, and they are still producing regardless of what the pond is doing:

- Gutters, clogged — the most common single source we find
- Corrugated downspout extensions, which hold water in the ribs even on a slope
- A tarp over the woodpile, the boat, the trailer
- Plant saucers, buckets, wheelbarrows, bin lids
- A birdbath nobody empties, and tires behind the shed
- The low patch of lawn that holds for a week after a storm

Some species need a bottle cap's worth. A week of undisturbed water is the whole life cycle.

## Where the treatment actually goes

Not on the lawn. Adults spend daylight in **shaded vegetation** — shrub undersides, dense ground cover, tall grass at the lot line, the north side of the house and the garage, the wooded margin.

The treated band usually looks nothing like the patio where you were being bitten, which is the most common surprise on a first visit and the reason the work holds.

## Timing, not frequency

Rain is the trigger. A significant rainfall refills everything at once, and about a week later you get a **synchronised hatch**. One treatment landing in that window beats two on a fixed schedule.

Most properties here do well on a handful of well-timed applications across the season rather than a standing monthly visit — which costs less, and is the program we would rather sell.
