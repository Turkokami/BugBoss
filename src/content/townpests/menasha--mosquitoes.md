Menasha gets two insect problems off the water, they look superficially similar, and they need opposite answers. Getting the distinction right is most of the value on this page.

## The hatch you cannot treat

**Midges and mayflies** come off Lake Winnebago in enormous synchronised clouds. They swarm lights and walls, they coat a building after a still warm night, and they do not bite.

They also cannot be treated. They develop in the lake sediment across thousands of acres of open water on their own schedule, and **nothing applied to a building affects a hatch emerging from the lake.** Anyone offering you a treatment for lake flies is selling something that cannot work.

What genuinely helps: screening, adjusting exterior lighting so your building is less of a beacon, and cleaning up afterward.

## The one you can

Mosquitoes are a different animal with a different answer. They **breed on land**, in standing water that sits undisturbed for several days. That immediately rules out the open lake, which is too big, too windy and too full of fish, and the moving channel, where larvae cannot develop at all.

It rules in everything on your property:

- Gutters, clogged — the most common source we find anywhere
- Corrugated downspout extensions holding water in the ribs
- A tarp over the woodpile, the boat, the trailer
- Plant saucers, buckets, wheelbarrows, bin lids, tires
- A birdbath nobody empties
- The low patch of lawn that stays soft a week after a storm

And the **sheltered weedy margins** nearby where water sits out of the wind. Some species need a bottle cap's worth; a week is the entire life cycle.

## The channel and the locks

The moving water produces nothing. The still edges beside it do — quiet vegetated pockets out of the flow, low ground that holds after a level change.

Most of that is not your property, which is why work on a near-channel lot leans toward **harborage treatment** rather than source reduction.

## Multi-unit buildings

Menasha has a lot of shared structure, and mosquito production on a multi-unit property is almost always in common ground rather than any one unit: the building's gutters, the dumpster surround, the planters, the low corner of the parking area, whatever is stored behind the building.

That makes it an owner or manager conversation rather than something a tenant can solve alone. Our [landlord and tenant pest control](/compliance/landlord-tenant-pest-control/) page covers how responsibility falls out in Wisconsin.

## Where a treatment goes

Into the **shaded vegetation** where adults spend daylight: shrub undersides, dense ground cover, tall grass at the lot edge, the shaded side of the building, any wooded margin.

Not the open lawn, and obviously not the lake. The treated band is routinely nowhere near the spot where people were being bitten, and that is what a correct application looks like.

## Timing

Rain is the trigger. A significant rainfall fills everything at once and a **synchronised hatch** follows roughly a week later. One treatment landing in that window beats two applied on a calendar.

Most properties here do well on a small number of well-timed applications rather than a standing monthly visit.
