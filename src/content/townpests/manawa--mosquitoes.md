Manawa sits on the **Little Wolf River**, with **Bear Lake** just outside town and a mix of river homes, lake properties, village housing and surrounding farms. Water defines the place, and it defines the mosquito work here.

But not in the way most people assume.

## The river is not breeding them

This is the reframe that makes mosquito control workable. **Mosquitoes do not breed in moving water.** They need water that stands undisturbed for several days — long enough for eggs to hatch and larvae to develop. The Little Wolf, flowing past the bottom of your garden, is not producing them.

What produces them is standing water within a short flight of your back door, and on a Manawa property that is almost always a short and depressingly familiar list:

- Plant saucers and pot trays
- A tarp over a boat, trailer or woodpile, holding a pocket of rain
- Clogged gutters — probably the most common single source we find
- Corrugated downspout extensions that hold water in the ribs
- A wheelbarrow, a bucket, a bin lid, a kids' toy
- Birdbaths nobody empties
- Old tyres
- Low spots in the lawn that hold for a week after heavy rain

A bottle cap's worth is enough for some species. This is why the walk-round matters more than the spray.

River and lake proximity does raise the baseline — humidity, shade, and still backwater pockets all help — but the controllable part is nearly all on your own land.

## Where the adults actually are

The second reframe. Adult mosquitoes do not spend the day in the open lawn, which is exactly where people aim a fogger.

They rest in **cool shaded vegetation**: the underside of shrub foliage, dense ground cover, tall grass at the yard edge, the shaded side of the house and outbuildings, and the wooded margin of the lot. That is where a barrier treatment belongs, and it is why the treated area often looks nothing like the patio where you were being bitten.

## What we actually do on a Manawa property

**Walk it first.** Every container, every low spot, the gutters, the tarps, the boat. This takes twenty minutes and it is the part that determines whether anything else works.

**Reduce the sources** — most of which costs nothing, just tipping things over and clearing a gutter.

**Treat the harbourage**, not the open yard.

**Time it to the weather** rather than to a fixed calendar. Significant rain fills everything at once and produces a synchronised hatch roughly a week later; treatment is worth far more timed to that than applied on a schedule regardless of conditions.

**Be honest about the ceiling.** A lakeside lot backing onto a marshy backwater will never be as low-pressure as a house in the middle of the village. We would rather set the expectation than have you feel misled in August.

## Event treatments, and ticks

Two things that come up constantly here.

Treating a few days before a **specific event** — a graduation, a wedding, a family weekend, the rodeo — works well and is a perfectly reasonable thing to want. It is not permanent and we will not pretend it is.

And on wooded or riverside lots, **ticks** are frequently the more consequential problem. They overlap with mosquito work because the yard edge where mown grass meets brush is both prime tick habitat and mosquito resting harbourage, so combining the two is efficient.
