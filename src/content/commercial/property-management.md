In a single-family home, a pest problem stops at the walls. In an apartment building, the walls are the highway. That one difference is the whole story of multi-family pest control: [bed bugs](/pest-library/bed-bugs/) and [German cockroaches](/pest-library/cockroaches/) don't respect unit boundaries, they travel through the shared walls, floor and ceiling gaps, and utility chases that tie a building together, and a program that treats one unit at a time is a program that never finishes. Property managers who learn this the hard way spend a year chasing the same infestation around a building, unit to unit, while it stays one step ahead.

## Multi-family is a building problem, not a unit problem

The central reality of multi-family pest control is spread. When a tenant reports [bed bugs](/pest-library/bed-bugs/) in unit 3, the honest assumption is that the bugs are already testing the walls into units 1, 5, and the units directly above and below. Bed bugs migrate along baseboards, through the gaps where plumbing and electrical penetrate shared walls, along the cable and utility chases that run vertically through a building, and behind the outlet and switch plates that are effectively open doorways in a demised wall. [German cockroaches](/pest-library/cockroaches/) do the same, moving through the plumbing chases that connect stacked kitchens and bathrooms, drawn along warm, humid runs from one unit to the next.

Treat the reported unit in isolation and one of two things happens: the population you didn't kill simply shifts next door and waits, or a neighboring source unit you never inspected re-seeds the one you just cleared. Either way, the manager ends up in a loop — a callback, another single-unit treatment, another callback — that burns money and tenant goodwill while the infestation entrenches. Breaking that loop requires inspecting and treating the affected unit **and the ring of units around it**, sealing the migration paths, and tracking findings across the whole building so a persistent source gets identified rather than chased. That is whole-building [Integrated Pest Management](/commercial-pest-control/), and it is the only thing that resolves a multi-family infestation for good.

## The pests that define multi-family housing

### Bed bugs: the reputation-and-turnover killer

[Bed bugs](/pest-library/bed-bugs/) are the pest that costs property managers the most, in dollars and in headaches. They ride in on a new tenant's belongings, on secondhand furniture, on a resident who picked them up traveling — never a cleanliness issue, always a transport issue — and once in a unit they hide in mattress seams, box-spring framing, headboards, baseboards, outlet boxes, and the cracks of furniture. A mated female cements her eggs into those cracks where sprays don't reach, which is why "spray the baseboards" fails and why every serious bed bug job has to account for eggs and stragglers with a follow-up inspection.

In a multi-family building the danger is spread. Every week an active unit goes untreated is another week the bugs push into neighboring units through the shared wall, converting a one-unit problem into a five-unit problem and a manageable cost into a building-wide project. The workhorse where it fits the unit is **heat treatment** — whole-unit heat that raises the space to a temperature lethal to every life stage, eggs included, in a single treatment with no residue on furniture tenants keep. For early or scattered activity we may use targeted products and monitors with a mandatory follow-up. Either way, in multi-family the job includes inspecting the surrounding units and sealing the paths between them; clearing the source unit while leaving the highway open just relocates the problem. Our full [bed bug pest page](/pest-library/bed-bugs/) covers the biology and signs in depth.

### German cockroaches: fast, chase-driven, and unit-hopping

[German cockroaches](/pest-library/cockroaches/) are the other signature multi-family pest, and they're relentless. They breed fast — a single female and her offspring can produce hundreds in a few months — in the warm, humid voids behind kitchen and bathroom fixtures: under the sink, behind the fridge and stove, in the motor housing of appliances, around the dishwasher and hot-water lines. They spread unit to unit through the plumbing and utility chases that connect stacked kitchens and baths, and they rebound almost immediately if you treat one unit and leave a connected one alone.

The control is gel baiting and IPM applied across the whole affected block of units, not a spray-and-pray on the one that complained. Broadcast spraying actually makes German roaches worse — it scatters them (and can trigger "bait aversion" if used carelessly) instead of eliminating the colony. We bait the harborage, address the sanitation and moisture conditions feeding them (a leaking trap under the sink is a roach nursery), and monitor to confirm the population is genuinely gone rather than lying low. Because roaches are a contamination and allergen concern — their droppings and shed skins trigger asthma, a real issue in occupied family housing — clearing them is a habitability matter, not just a nuisance.

### Rodents: shared walls, utility chases, and old rural stock

[Mice](/pest-library/mice/) are a constant in multi-family, especially in the older converted homes and aging apartment stock common across rural Wisconsin. They enter through quarter-inch gaps around utility penetrations, unsealed thresholds, and foundation cracks, then travel the same wall voids and chases the insects use, moving between units and floors out of sight. Fall — September through November — brings the seasonal surge as mice move indoors ahead of winter. [Rats](/pest-library/rats/) show up around dumpster corrals, in overgrown building edges, and in the crawlspaces and basements of older properties. The fix is exclusion — sealing the actual entry points — plus mapped, monitored stations, run building-wide rather than reactively unit by unit.

### Ants, flies, and the shared-space pests

[Ants](/pest-library/ants/) forage from exterior nests into ground-floor units and common areas in warmer months, working kitchens and any shared trash room. [Gnats](/pest-library/gnats/) — drain flies and fruit flies — breed in floor drains, basement and laundry-room drains, and trash-room residue; [flies](/pest-library/flies/) come off the dumpster corral and pressure entries and common hallways. In multi-family these common-area pests are the manager's direct responsibility, and they're best handled at the source — the dirty drain, the dumpster, the trash room — as part of the whole-building program.

### The wildlife older rural rentals bring with them

Much of the multi-family and rental stock out here is older — converted farmhouses and village homes cut into units, with balloon framing, soffit and fascia gaps, and attics that invite bats, squirrels, and birds. Because BugBoss does both pest control *and* wildlife exclusion, we can handle the attic and roofline intrusions that come with older rural rentals — including bat work, which Wisconsin law restricts during the June 1–July 31 maternity season and which most pest-only companies won't do at all. That combination is rare among rural competitors and it saves managers from juggling two vendors.

## The rules: ATCP 134, entry notice, and landlord responsibility

Multi-family pest work in Wisconsin runs alongside landlord-tenant law, and managers have to get the compliance right or a good treatment can still turn into a dispute.

**Entry notice.** Under **ATCP 134.09(2)** and **Wis. Stat. 704.05**, a landlord must give a tenant at least **12 hours' advance notice** before entering a rented unit, may enter only at reasonable times, and must announce their presence — except in a genuine emergency or when the tenant consents to shorter notice. Pest treatment is a planned entry, so it needs proper notice. We schedule treatments so your notice window is respected and units are prepped and accessible on the day, and we document access — including any unit that was refused or not prepped — so you have a clean record.

**Responsibility.** As a general matter, Wisconsin landlords are responsible for maintaining rental housing in habitable condition, and controlling a building-wide infestation — bed bugs and cockroaches especially — is an owner responsibility, not something you can push onto a single tenant, because those pests spread beyond any one unit regardless of that tenant's housekeeping. Exact responsibility for a given incident can depend on the lease and on the cause, and this isn't legal advice — confirm the specifics with your attorney. But the operational reality is simple: a pest that uses the shared walls is a building pest, and building pests are the owner's to solve.

**Documentation is your protection.** In multi-family, records aren't just for inspections — they're your evidence in a tenant dispute, your handoff at turnover, and your reporting line to owners. Every visit produces a work order covering what was inspected, treated and found. On a multi-unit job the useful extra is a unit-by-unit record of which units were prepped, treated, refused or outstanding — agree that with us at the start and it becomes part of the routine.

## Turnover is your best defense — and your biggest blind spot

The vacant unit between tenants is the one moment a manager has full, uncontested access, and it's the cheapest time to catch a problem. A **turnover inspection** at move-out — before you clean, paint, and re-rent — is worth more than almost any other single service in multi-family. An empty unit lets us inspect the mattress-less bed frame, the baseboards, the outlet boxes, and the kitchen and bath voids without a tenant's belongings in the way, and without the notice-and-access friction that comes with an occupied unit. Finding bed bugs or roaches in a vacant unit means you treat it clean before a new resident moves in and inherits a problem you then own.

Skipping that inspection is how a lot of building-wide infestations start: a departing tenant leaves bed bugs behind, the unit gets a quick turn, a new tenant moves in, the bugs spread to the neighbors, and now it's a five-unit project that a fifteen-minute vacant-unit inspection would have caught. For managers with steady turnover, building a quick pest inspection into the make-ready checklist is one of the highest-return habits in the business.

## Tenant communication and the blame problem

Bed bugs and cockroaches carry a stigma, and that stigma is the enemy of control. Tenants who fear being blamed — or charged — for an infestation hide it, and a hidden infestation is one that spreads through the shared walls before anyone reports it. The managers who stay ahead of multi-family pests are the ones who make it safe and easy for residents to report early: a clear, no-blame reporting process and a fast, professional response teach a building that speaking up gets the problem solved rather than getting them in trouble.

## A multi-family pest calendar for Wisconsin

**Spring.** Ants begin foraging into ground-floor units and common areas as the ground warms. Overwintered invaders emerge from wall voids in older buildings. It's a strong window for exterior exclusion and for humane bat work on older rental stock — before Wisconsin's June 1 maternity-season restriction closes it.

**Summer.** Bed bug activity runs high with peak moving season — leases turning over, secondhand furniture changing hands, residents traveling — so it's a high-introduction window. Small flies breed fastest in warm laundry-room and basement drains. Roach populations grow quickly in the heat.

**Fall (rodent surge).** September through November, mice move indoors, and older multi-family stock with many entry points gets hit hard. Exclusion and building-wide monitoring take priority before winter.

**Winter.** German cockroaches thrive year-round in heated buildings, congregating in the warmest units and mechanical spaces. Rodents already inside keep working the wall voids and chases. Monitoring continues through the cold months.

## Common areas and the exterior: the shared-cost pests

Beyond individual units, a multi-family property has spaces every resident shares and every owner is responsible for — lobbies and hallways, laundry and trash rooms, basements and mechanical spaces, the dumpster corral, and the grounds. These are where building-wide pressure originates and where a whole-building program earns its keep. Trash rooms and laundry-room drains breed [gnats](/pest-library/gnats/) and feed [rodents](/pest-library/mice/); the dumpster corral drives [flies](/pest-library/flies/) and [rats](/pest-library/rats/) for the entire property; basements and utility rooms are the highways that connect units vertically. Sanitation in these shared spaces — closed dumpster lids, clean drains, cleared harborage against the foundation — does more to protect every unit than any single in-unit treatment, and it's squarely the owner's to manage. We fold the common areas and exterior into the program so the building is treated as the connected system it actually is.
