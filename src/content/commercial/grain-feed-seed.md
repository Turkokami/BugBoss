## Stored grain and feed is a pest magnet under real regulation

A grain elevator, a feed mill, a seed-cleaning and storage operation — from the road these are some of the most ordinary-looking buildings in rural Wisconsin, and from a pest-control standpoint they are among the most demanding. They exist to concentrate and hold exactly what pests want: tons of grain, feed, and seed in one place, plus the warmth, the dust, and the spillage that come with handling it. A single facility can hold enough food to support an insect population running into the millions and a rodent population that treats the structure as a heated pantry. And unlike a backyard shed, these facilities operate under federal food-safety regulation and, for most, under buyer audits that examine the pest program in detail.

## The pests that matter: know exactly what you have

Stored-product pest control begins with correct identification, because the insects that attack grain and feed fall into groups that behave very differently, and the response depends on which you have. This is monitoring-driven work — you cannot manage what you haven't identified.

### Internal feeders: the weevils

The **granary weevil** (*Sitophilus granarius*) and the **rice weevil** (*Sitophilus oryzae*) are internal feeders: the female lays an egg inside a whole kernel, the larva develops entirely within the grain, hidden, and emerges as an adult. That concealment is what makes weevils dangerous — by the time you see adults, a generation has already developed unseen inside the grain mass, and infested kernels can move through the system looking sound. The granary weevil is flightless and a classic problem of long-term bin storage in temperate climates like Wisconsin's; the rice weevil flies and can infest grain in the field and in storage. Because weevils develop inside the kernel, controlling them leans hard on prevention — clean incoming grain, cool dry storage, and sanitation — since surface treatments don't reach the larvae inside.

### External feeders: the beetles

The **sawtoothed grain beetle** (*Oryzaephilus surinamensis*) and the **red flour beetle** (*Tribolium castaneum*) are external feeders that live in the grain mass, the fines, and processed product. They don't bore into whole sound kernels but thrive on broken grain, dust, meal, and the residue that accumulates in equipment and dead spaces — which ties them directly to sanitation. The sawtoothed grain beetle is small, flat, and able to work into packaging and cracks; the red flour beetle is a signature pest of mills and processed feed. Their presence is very often a sanitation signal: they're telling you there's accumulated product somewhere feeding them.

### The Indian meal moth

The **Indian meal moth** (*Plodia interpunctella*) is the most commonly seen stored-product pest, and its larvae are the culprits behind the **silken webbing** on the surface of stored grain and feed and in the head space of a bin. The larvae feed at the grain surface and in fines, then crawl — often up walls and across ceilings — to pupate, and the grayish, two-toned adult moths fly in the evening. Because it concentrates at the surface and in spillage and rides in on incoming product, it's controlled largely through sanitation, monitoring with pheromone traps, and cleaning out residue before refilling. See our [moths](/pest-library/moths/) page for more on the family, and our [carpet beetles](/pest-library/carpet-beetles/) page for a related fabric-and-residue pest that turns up in facility offices and storage.

### Rodents

A facility full of grain is the single strongest [rat](/pest-library/rats/) and [mouse](/pest-library/mice/) attractant in agriculture. Rodents contaminate far more than they eat with droppings and urine, gnaw wiring and create fire risk, and breed year-round in a warm, well-stocked structure. And rodent evidence in a food facility is a fast way to fail an audit or draw an FDA observation. Rodent control here is exterior interception, interior monitoring, and — the permanent fix — exclusion and spillage control, covered in detail below.

## The regulatory reality: FDA animal-food rules and audits

### FSMA and 21 CFR Part 507

A facility that manufactures, processes, packs, or holds animal food is covered by the FDA's Food Safety Modernization Act rule for animal food — the **Preventive Controls for Animal Food** regulation, codified at **21 CFR Part 507** and overseen by the FDA's **Center for Veterinary Medicine (CVM)**. The rule has two main parts that touch pest control. First, **current good manufacturing practices (CGMPs)** that apply broadly and require, among other things, that the plant and grounds be kept in a condition that protects against pest contamination — pests excluded from manufacturing, processing, packing, and holding areas, and the facility and surroundings maintained so pests don't become a source of contamination. Second, for covered facilities, a **hazard analysis and risk-based preventive controls** program with a written food-safety plan.

The practical upshot for pest management is straightforward: a covered feed mill or grain facility must actively keep pests out and be able to show it. A documented IPM program — monitoring, sanitation, exclusion, and targeted control with records — is how a facility demonstrates it's meeting the CGMP expectation rather than simply reacting after an infestation appears.

### Buyer audits and GFSI schemes

On top of the FDA baseline, facilities selling into larger supply chains often carry a **third-party audit** — a GFSI-benchmarked scheme or a specific buyer's food-safety audit — that examines the pest program closely: the device map, the trend data, corrective actions, applicator licensing, and evidence the facility acts on what monitoring finds. As on the [dairy and cheese processing](/commercial-pest-control/dairy-cheese-processing/) side, a program built to satisfy an auditor comfortably satisfies the regulatory baseline beneath it, and that's the standard BugBoss builds to.

## An audit-ready IPM program for stored product

Integrated Pest Management is the right framework for a grain, feed, or seed facility precisely because the pests live in conditions — residue, spillage, warm grain, structural gaps — that you manage rather than spray away. Here's what the program looks like.

### Monitoring: know before you can see

The program runs on **monitoring that produces trend data**. **Pheromone traps** draw and count specific insects — Indian meal moth lures, beetle lures — so rising numbers flag a building problem before it's visible in the grain. **Grain probe traps** sample inside the grain mass, where surface inspection can't reach. **Rodent devices** are mapped and numbered around the perimeter and interior. The value isn't any single catch; it's the record over time that tells you where and when pressure is building, so sanitation or targeted treatment happens early, at a corner or a bin, instead of at load-out with a whole lot infested.

### Sanitation: the foundation

In a grain and feed facility, **sanitation is the core of pest control**, because the insects survive in what gets left behind — residual grain in "empty" bins, fines and dust in the **boot pit and leg**, spillage under equipment, and old product in dead corners. The most common way an operator reseeds an infestation is filling a bin over old residue that's already harboring beetles or moth larvae. So the program prioritizes **cleaning out bins and equipment before refilling**, keeping the facility swept and the fines managed, running **first-in-first-out rotation** so nothing sits indefinitely, and eliminating the dead spaces where residue accumulates unseen. Every sanitation gap we find gets documented as a corrective-action recommendation, because correcting the condition is what actually ends the problem.

### Aeration and grain condition

Stored-grain insects are **temperature- and moisture-driven** — they develop fast in warm, damp grain and slow to a near-stop when grain is cold and dry. Wisconsin's climate is an advantage here: **aerating grain to cool and then near-freezing temperatures through fall and winter, and keeping it dry**, dramatically slows or halts insect development and mold. Managing grain condition is a genuine pest-control tool, not just a quality measure, and it works hand in hand with sanitation and monitoring — cold, dry, clean grain is grain that isn't building an insect population.

### Exclusion: keep them out of the structure

A facility this attractive lives under constant pressure from the surrounding fields and farms, so **exclusion** does heavy lifting. That means tight **door and dock seals**, sealed utility and pipe penetrations, intact screens and vents, weathertight bins and headhouses, and constant attention to **spilled grain** on the grounds and pads, which draws rodents, insects, and [nuisance birds](/pest-library/nuisance-birds/) straight to the building. It's the same seal-it-out discipline BugBoss brings to [wildlife exclusion](/wildlife-removal/), scaled to an industrial structure.

### Targeted treatment, by the label

Only after monitoring, sanitation, aeration, and exclusion does product come into it, applied by a licensed applicator strictly to label. That includes **empty-bin and structural treatments** applied to clean bins before refilling, **crack-and-crevice work** in non-product areas, **residual treatments** on surfaces and equipment where the label allows, and **exterior rodent bait stations**. Active ingredients are chosen for the setting and **rotated** to manage resistance. The restraint is deliberate: in a facility handling animal food, treatment is precise and documented, never broadcast over product.

### Documentation

The documentation is part of the deliverable. A BugBoss facility program keeps current and on-site: a **device and trap map**, **service reports** for every visit, a **trend log** of insect counts and rodent activity with analysis, **corrective-action records** on sanitation and exclusion, **product labels and safety data sheets**, and the **applicator's license and insurance**. When an FDA investigator, a buyer's auditor, or a GFSI scheme asks to see the pest program, the answer is a complete file — not a stack of receipts.

## Fumigation: a specialized, licensed job — and often avoidable

Operators are often told, or assume, that fumigation is the go-to answer for a grain insect problem. It's worth being clear and honest about what fumigation actually is. **Fumigation uses restricted-use fumigants** — phosphine-generating products and, for some structural work, sulfuryl fluoride — that fill a sealed space with toxic gas to penetrate the grain mass and structure and kill insects at all life stages, including the ones hidden inside kernels. It is highly effective and sometimes genuinely necessary. It is also **highly regulated and hazardous**: it requires **specific applicator certification** for fumigation, strict sealing to hold gas concentration, aeration and clearance monitoring, placarding, and detailed safety and recordkeeping requirements. It is not a routine spray, and it is not something to hand to a general applicator or to attempt casually.

For most facilities, **a strong sanitation, aeration, and monitoring program prevents infestations from ever reaching the point where fumigation is required** — which is the far cheaper and safer path. Where a grain or structural fumigation genuinely is warranted, we'll tell you honestly and help you bring in a properly **licensed fumigation specialist** for that specific job, rather than pretend it's a routine service or let a facility gamble with a hazardous procedure done wrong. Prevention first, fumigation only when it's truly the right tool and done by the right, licensed hands.

## Rodents: the constant siege

Because a grain facility is the strongest rodent attractant in agriculture, rodent control is never "done" — it's a constant siege held by three lines. **Exterior tamper-resistant bait stations** intercept rodents before they reach the structure. **Interior monitoring and mechanical traps** catch what gets in, mapped and numbered for the audit trail. And **exclusion and spillage control** — sealing the structure and keeping spilled grain cleaned up so there's nothing drawing rodents to the walls — is what actually reduces the pressure over time. Grain stored tight, pads kept clean, and vegetation and clutter cleared off the building all cut the draw at the source. ## Facility types weight the problem differently

A commercial **grain elevator** is about volume and turnover — large bins, receiving and load-out, and the boot pits and legs that accumulate fines. The pest emphasis is bin sanitation between fillings, aeration and grain condition, weevil and beetle monitoring in stored lots, and holding rodents off a structure that's essentially a mountain of food. A **feed mill** adds processing, grinding, and mixing, which means more dust, more fines in equipment, and processed feed that favors external feeders like the red flour and sawtoothed grain beetles and Indian meal moth — so equipment sanitation and the residue in dead spaces matter even more. **On-farm storage** — the bins and hopper tanks on a working farm — is a smaller version of the same fight, tied into the farm's overall rodent and stored-product pressure (covered on our [farm and agricultural page](/commercial-pest-control/agricultural-farm/)). And a **seed facility** carries the added constraint that its product has to stay viable, which pushes the whole program toward prevention rather than treating the product.

### Seed facilities and viability

Seed deserves its own note because you can't manage it like feed grain. Treated or coated seed carries specific handling and labeling requirements, and seed intended for planting has to keep its **germination viability**, so the emphasis leans heavily on protecting the storage environment rather than treating the seed itself: cool, dry, clean storage; tight exclusion; sanitation of bins and equipment before storing; and monitoring incoming and stored stock. Stored seed is attacked by the same weevils and beetles as grain, so keeping incoming lots clean and the storage structure sealed and monitored is the core of a seed-facility program — prevention first, because a treatment that protects grain isn't necessarily appropriate for viable seed.

## The stored-grain year in Wisconsin

Stored-product pest pressure tracks the calendar and the grain's temperature, and a program that anticipates the year beats one that reacts. **Harvest and fill (fall)** is the highest-risk window for reseeding a problem — this is when bins get filled, and filling a bin over old residual grain is the fastest way to carry an infestation into the new crop, so cleaning out empty bins and equipment before they're filled is the priority. **Late fall into winter** is when Wisconsin's climate becomes an ally: aerating grain down to cool and then near-freezing temperatures and keeping it dry slows insect development to a near-stop and holds mold in check, so this is the season to get grain cold and keep it that way. **Spring** is the turn — as outside temperatures climb, the top of the grain mass and the bin surface warm first, and any surviving insects begin developing again, so this is when surface monitoring and pheromone-trap counts matter most. **Summer** is peak insect-activity season in warm grain and in the warm dust of a mill, when Indian meal moth and beetle populations move fastest, spillage ferments, and rodent pressure builds ahead of the next harvest. The program shifts its emphasis across those windows rather than doing the same thing year-round.
