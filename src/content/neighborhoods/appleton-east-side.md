## Old homes, old mills, and the Fox River

Appleton's east side is the oldest, most river-shaped part of the city. Appleton grew up along the **Fox River** — the first paper mill went in on the water in 1853, the nation's first hydroelectric central station, the Vulcan Street Plant, started generating on the river in 1882, and Lawrence University has anchored the near-east side since the city's earliest days. The result is a stretch of older neighborhoods where the housing runs from the **Victorian homes of the Old Third Ward** — home to the Hearthstone Historic House Museum, itself the first residence in the world lit by a central hydroelectric station — through the **College Avenue Historic District** to the mid-twentieth-century riverside streets of areas like Colony Oaks. It's genuinely old, genuinely close to the water, and both of those things drive the pest picture.

Two forces shape pest pressure on the east side, and they compound each other. The first is **age**: Victorian and early-1900s homes were built with no modern air-sealing and have had a century-plus to settle, opening gaps at the foundation, the sills, the soffits, and the additions. The second is **the river**: the Fox River corridor keeps the ground and the lower framing damp, feeds a strong summer mosquito population, and gives insects and wildlife a wet, wooded travel route right through the neighborhood. An old home is a leaky home; a damp old home is a leaky home with a moisture problem; and a damp old home next to a river is all of that with a mosquito season on top.

## The pests of an old riverfront neighborhood

**[Carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/)** are the headline pest on the east side, and the river is the reason. Carpenter ants excavate galleries in moisture-softened wood, and the damp river corridor keeps the ground, the lower sills, and the porch and deck wood of these old homes exactly wet enough. The parent colony usually lives outdoors in a tree, stump, or woodpile along the corridor, sending satellite nests into the wet framing of the house. You see the big black ants trailing indoors or a scatter of winged swarmers at a spring window; large black ants indoors in winter mean a nest is living inside the heated structure. A [carpenter-ant problem in a deck](/pest-problems/carpenter-ants-in-deck/) — especially a riverside deck near damp ground — is one of the most common calls the east side makes. Spraying the visible trail never reaches the nest or fixes the moisture; that's the whole job.

**[Mosquitoes](/pest-library/mosquitoes/)** are the second signature, and they're what makes the east side different from a landlocked neighborhood. Standing and slow water is prime breeding habitat, and the Fox River, its backwaters, and the low, damp yards along it produce mosquitoes all summer. A riverfront lot can be unusable at dusk in July. We can't drain the river, but a seasonal program that treats the resting vegetation where adult mosquitoes shelter and knocks down the standing-water breeding sites on and near the property makes a real difference for a yard, patio, or event space. Low, shaded lots close to the water benefit the most.

The **fall invaders** round out the older-home roster. **[Cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/)**, **[boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/)**, and **[Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/)** mass on the warm south and west walls in September and October and push into the wall voids and attics of these old homes to overwinter. Cluster flies appear even in immaculate homes because their larvae parasitize earthworms in the soil — and the damp river-corridor ground is heavy with earthworms, so the east side produces them in force. On a warm winter day the walls heat up and the crowd wakes and heads for the [windows](/pest-problems/cluster-flies-in-windows/); the fix is an early-fall exterior treatment plus sealing, laid out in the [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/). **[Mice](/pest-library/mice/)** exploit the same aged foundations and damp, sheltered ground, pushing in through dime-sized gaps as the weather cools.

## Entry points in a Victorian, and why exclusion is the real fix

The older east-side homes have the full catalog of entry points that come with age. Many sit on **fieldstone, rubble, or early block foundations** that develop mortar gaps and settlement cracks — harborage and travel routes for rodents, ants, and overwintering insects. The oldest homes were built with **balloon framing**, leaving the wall cavities open from the basement sill to the attic with no fire-stops, so a mouse or a cluster fly that enters low can end up in the attic without ever showing itself — which is why pests in these homes seem to appear from nowhere. Worn **soffit and fascia joints**, settled **gable trim**, and gaps around old **chimney flashing** open the attic to flies, bats, and squirrels. The enclosed porches, additions, and dormers common on these homes almost always have a poorly sealed seam where new framing met old. And the river corridor keeps the whole lower structure damp enough to soften wood and draw carpenter ants and springtails.

None of this responds to a spray. The work these homes need is **exclusion** — finding the actual gaps and closing them with the right materials, sealing at both the foundation and the roofline in a balloon-framed house, and correcting the moisture that softens the wood. That's careful, hands-on work, the opposite of a quick route stop, and it's the core of what we do.

## Bats, squirrels, and raccoons along the river

The east side is strong **wildlife** territory, and this is where a company that does both pest and wildlife work earns its keep. The mature trees along the river corridor put branches against old rooflines, and Victorian and mid-century homes have the gable, soffit, and chimney gaps that structural wildlife exploits.

**[Bats](/pest-library/bats/)** are the most common and the most legally sensitive. Wisconsin protects its bats — four cave species are state-threatened and white-nose syndrome hit the state in 2014 — so exclusion is regulated by season. The **DNR requires that exit points stay open June 1 through July 31**, the maternity window when flightless pups are in the roost; sealing then traps and kills them inside your walls. The right process is to find the exits at dusk, install one-way doors outside that window, wait at least a week for the colony to clear, then seal and clean the guano. The full legal timeline is on the [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/), and [bats in the attic](/pest-problems/bats-in-attic/) is routine work for us. **[Squirrels](/pest-library/squirrels/)** chew into attics through weak roof edges and old louvers, and **[raccoons](/pest-library/raccoons/)** den in uncapped chimneys and torn soffits — spring mothers the usual culprit. Both leave contamination: chewed wiring, soiled insulation, latrines. Every one is a removal-plus-exclusion-plus-cleanup job; see the [wildlife removal](/wildlife-removal/) page and the [attic wildlife removal guide](/guides/attic-wildlife-removal/). The pest-only franchises won't do any of it.

## What an east-side homeowner can do, and where it stops

We'll tell you straight what you can handle. Near the river, the biggest lever is moisture: keep gutters and downspouts running water away from the foundation, correct grade that pools against the sill, and keep firewood and mulch off the damp lower wall — wet wood is what draws carpenter ants. Empty anything that holds standing water in the yard weekly to cut the local mosquito brood, trim tree limbs back from the old rooflines so bats and squirrels lose their bridge, and knock down the first spring wasp nests before the colonies build. On an aged home, sealing obvious foundation and sill gaps with the right materials helps too.

Where it stops is the point most homeowners reach: carpenter ants whose parent nest is out along the river corridor and whose satellites are in wet framing you can't reach, mosquitoes off moving water that no yard spray alone will hold without a real program, cluster flies that need a properly timed exterior treatment, mice refilling through hidden gaps in a balloon-framed wall, or any bat or wildlife issue with legal timing and contamination cleanup. DIY clears the visible layer and lets the source rebuild. That's where calling BugBoss saves money — and with no contract, you call for the one job over your head and nothing more.
