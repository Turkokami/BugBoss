## Preble: an east side of two eras

Preble isn't a single subdivision — it's a broad stretch of Green Bay's **east side**, and understanding its pest picture starts with its history. The former **Town of Preble** was created on **July 13, 1859**, and covered the land east of the **East River** out toward Humboldt and Eaton. On **November 3, 1964**, Preble's residents voted to consolidate with the city of Green Bay, and those **12,576 acres** nearly doubled the city's size and opened the door to decades of easterly development. That single fact explains why "Preble" today spans such different housing.

The **older core**, closer to the East River and the Bay Beach area, filled in through the mid-twentieth century — ranches, Cape Cods, split-levels, and story-and-a-half homes from the 1950s and 60s. Push farther east and you hit the **newer subdivisions**, much of it built from the late 1980s onward on former farmland, with plenty of construction dated well into the 2000s and 2010s. Ranch, split-level, and modern-farmhouse and Colonial-Revival builds line curved streets and cul-de-sacs. One name, two building eras — and each brings pests in its own way.

**BugBoss The X-Terminator** is not an east-side Green Bay company. We run out of **Clintonville**, in rural Waupaca County, and Preble sits at the eastern edge of the metro area we reach. We won't pretend we're the closest truck to a Green Bay address. What we are is the rural exclusion and wildlife specializt worth calling when the problem is one we're built for — **fall invaders, rodents, carpenter ants, and attic wildlife** — the kind of work we do every week on old farmhouses and lake cabins out in the country, and the kind the metro franchises won't do without a contract.

## Two housing eras, two sets of entry points

The mistake homeowners in Preble's newer subdivisions make is assuming a modern house is pest-proof. It isn't. It just leaks in different, more hidden places than an old one.

**Mid-century east-side homes** near the East River have the weak points of any settled sixty-year-old house: **block foundations** that have cracked and shifted, soffit and fascia gaps that have loosened, roof-edge openings, and utility penetrations that were never sealed tight. These are visible problems once you know where to look, and they reward a careful exclusion inspection.

**Newer subdivision homes** are tighter on paper but predictable in practice. Fall invaders and mice exploit the **construction junctions**: soffit returns and ridge vents, **weep holes** in brick veneer, gaps around HVAC and plumbing penetrations, garage-door corners, and unsealed sill plates hidden behind the siding. A complex roofline with multiple planes — common on the newer custom builds here — gives *more* of these gaps, not fewer. New construction changes where you seal, not whether you have to.

And the newer subdivisions have geography working against them. Much of eastern Preble was **farmland** until recently, so plenty of homes still back onto open ground, fencerows, and green corridors. That matters more than the build date, as the next section explains.

## The fall invaders: why new houses get them too

Every September and October, Preble gets the same wave every rural home in our territory gets. **Cluster flies**, **boxelder bugs**, and **Asian lady beetles** mass on the warm south- and west-facing walls, then work into wall voids and attics to overwinter. On a warm day in late winter they wake up and turn up at the windows, looking like a brand-new invasion.

Homeowners in Preble's newer subdivisions are often shocked to get hammered by [cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/), assuming it's an old-house problem. It isn't. Cluster fly larvae **parasitize earthworms** in the soil, so a two-year-old house sitting on former farm ground produces the same fall population as a mid-century home near the river — it just lets them in through soffit returns and weep holes instead of a foundation crack. [Boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/) feed on boxelder and maple seeds from the trees around every neighborhood, and [Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/) pile in beside them.

The fix is the same regardless of build date: a **timed early-fall exterior treatment** on the sunny walls, plus sealing the seams. Our [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/) covers the timing, and if they're already stacking up on the glass, see [cluster flies in windows](/pest-problems/cluster-flies-in-windows/).

## Mice and carpenter ants across the east side

**Mice** peak from September through November and don't care how new your house is. With open ground and the **East River corridor** close by, a dime-sized gap is all it takes. Newer homes leak at the construction junctions; older homes leak at block foundations and soffit gaps. Trapping clears what's inside, but sealing the building is what stops the supply — see the [mice](/pest-library/mice/) page for where they get in.

**Carpenter ants** show up in both eras. Older homes have aged wood and decades of small leaks; newer homes get them through moisture around decks, window and door flashing, and roof-edge leaks people assume new construction is immune to. Swarmers at the windows in spring, or large black ants active indoors in winter, mean the nest is inside the heated structure. Control means locating the nest and correcting the moisture, not spraying the trail — the [carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) page explains why. And [wasps and hornets](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) round out the east-side summer, nesting under eaves and deck structures and turning aggressive in August.

## Wildlife along the East River corridor

The **East River** and the green space around **Bay Beach** give wildlife a highway right up to east-side rooflines, and this is where a rural specializt genuinely earns the trip from Clintonville. Homes backing onto that corridor see more attic and chimney intrusions than a home buried in a dense block.

**Bats** roost in attics and behind fascia on both the mid-century homes and the larger new builds with complex rooflines. Exclusion is legally restricted — Wisconsin protects its bats, and the **DNR keeps one-way exits open June 1 through July 31** during the maternity season, when flightless pups are in the roost. We do the work outside that window: find the exits, install one-way doors, wait at least a week, then seal and clean up the guano. The rules are in our [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/), and the process is on the [bats](/pest-library/bats/) and [bats in attic](/pest-problems/bats-in-attic/) pages. We never seal bats in.

**Squirrels** chew into attics at roof edges and gable vents — a [squirrels in attic](/pest-problems/squirrels-in-attic/) job that ends with sealing every access point. **Raccoons** den in chimneys and torn soffits, especially females in spring; that's a [raccoon in chimney](/pest-problems/raccoon-in-chimney/) situation calling for humane removal and a proper cap. This is all [wildlife removal](/wildlife-removal/) work, detailed in our [attic wildlife removal guide](/guides/attic-wildlife-removal/), and it's the capability the metro pest franchises generally don't offer — see the [raccoons](/pest-library/raccoons/) and [squirrels](/pest-library/squirrels/) pages.

## Beyond the fall invaders: the rest of the east-side picture

Cluster flies and mice lead the Preble list, but a mixed-era east side carries a broader mix. **Nuisance ants** — pavement ants trailing out of driveway and patio cracks, odorous house ants in the kitchen — are a steady call from the newer subdivisions. These aren't the wood-damaging carpenter ants, and spraying the visible trail only scatters them; the fix is treating the colony at the source.

**Spiders** follow their food, so they gather wherever the insect supply is good — well-lit newer homes, garages, and porches that pull in flying insects at night. The durable control is an exterior program that knocks down the insects they feed on, not sweeping webs off the eaves.

**Ticks** matter on the eastern edges where the newer subdivisions push against woodlots, the East River green space, and old field margins. Deer ticks carry Lyme disease and wait in the tall grass along those edges, so families with kids and dogs backing onto green space are the ones who see them. A perimeter treatment targets the brushy transition where lawn meets cover.

And winter on the east side is mostly about what already got in during fall — **mice** breeding in a warm wall void, and the overwintering flies and beetles waking at the windows on a mild day. Sealing in September is what keeps January quiet, which is why we push exclusion in fall rather than chasing individual bugs all winter.
