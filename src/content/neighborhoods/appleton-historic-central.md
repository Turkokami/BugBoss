## The oldest housing in Appleton, and what that means for pests

Historic Central is the band of older housing that sits just north of downtown Appleton, above College Avenue. It's the closest thing the city has to a founding neighborhood: Appleton was settled in 1847 and incorporated in 1857, its first paper mill went up on the **Fox River** in 1853, and the streets north of the commercial core filled in with homes as the mills and Lawrence College drew people through the late 1800s. The result is a neighborhood where the **median home was built around 1885** — genuinely old housing stock, with Gothic-revival churches like Zion Lutheran anchoring whole blocks and a summer Porchfest that plays out on hundred-year-old front porches.

For a pest technician, the age of the housing is the whole story. A home built in the 1880s or 1890s was not built to any modern standard of air-sealing, and it has had a hundred and forty years to settle, shift, and shed water. Every one of those years opened gaps. That's not a knock on the neighborhood — these are some of the best-built and best-loved homes in Appleton — it's just the reality of the building science. Old homes have more ways in, and the pests that matter here are the ones that exploit age, moisture, and the open cavities of pre-war construction.

## The pests that come with 1880s housing

Four pests define pest control in this neighborhood, and all four track directly to the age of the homes.

**[Carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/)** are at the top. These are the large black ants that excavate galleries in wood that has been softened by moisture — they don't eat it, they hollow it out to nest. A neighborhood of hundred-year-old homes is a carpenter-ant buffet: old porch columns and deck ledgers, sills that have wicked groundwater for decades, roof edges and valleys that have leaked slowly for years, and the damp basements common under fieldstone foundations. The parent colony is often outdoors in a tree, stump, or woodpile, sending satellite nests into the wet framing of the house. Winged swarmers show up at windows in spring; large black ants indoors in the dead of winter mean a nest is living inside the heated structure. Seeing a carpenter-ant problem on a [deck](/pest-problems/carpenter-ants-in-deck/) is one of the most common calls these homes produce.

The **fall invaders** are the second signature. **[Cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/)**, **[boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/)**, and **[Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/)** mass on the warm south and west walls of these homes in September and October and push into any gap they can find, heading for the wall voids and attic to overwinter. Cluster flies in particular confuse people because they appear in clean, well-kept old homes — the reason is biology, not housekeeping. Cluster fly larvae parasitize earthworms in the soil, so any home surrounded by lawn, boulevard trees, and old yards produces a local population regardless of how spotless it is. On a warm winter day the wall cavities heat up and the whole overwintering crowd wakes and heads for the [windows](/pest-problems/cluster-flies-in-windows/). We cover the full approach in the [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/).

**[Mice](/pest-library/mice/)** are the third. Old homes leak mice the way they leak air — through gaps as small as a dime at the foundation, around old utility penetrations, under later porch and mudroom additions, and wherever a century of settlement has cracked the sill open. In these homes the mice rarely stay where they got in, which brings us to the fourth factor.

## Balloon framing, fieldstone, and the entry points of an old home

The single most important structural fact about pre-1930s Appleton homes is **balloon framing**. In balloon-framed construction, the wall studs run continuously from the foundation sill all the way up to the roofline, with no fire-stops closing off the cavity at each floor. That leaves the wall interior as one open vertical shaft from basement to attic. A mouse that enters at the sill can climb inside the wall to the second floor or attic without ever showing itself. Cluster flies and boxelder bugs that get behind the siding funnel down into those same cavities. It's why pests in these homes seem to erupt from nowhere, far from any visible entry. Exclusion in a balloon-framed home means sealing at **both** the foundation and the roofline, and it's the first thing we inspect for.

The foundations add their own doors. Many Historic Central homes sit on **fieldstone, rubble, or early block foundations**, which develop mortar gaps and settlement cracks over the decades — perfect harborage and travel routes for rodents, ants, and overwintering insects. Old **basement and crawlspace moisture** feeds the carpenter ants and springtails. Worn **soffit and fascia joints**, settled **gable-end trim**, and gaps around old **chimney flashing** open the attic to flies, bats, and squirrels. Later additions — the enclosed back porch, the dormer, the mudroom — almost always have a poorly sealed seam where new framing met old.

None of this is fixed with a spray. The work these homes need is **exclusion**: finding the actual gaps and closing them with the right materials, correcting the moisture that softened the wood, and treating the source rather than the symptom. That's slower, more careful work than a franchise route allows, and it's the core of what we do.

## Bats, squirrels, and raccoons in the attic

Historic Central is prime **wildlife** territory, and this is where being a company that does both pest control and wildlife removal matters most. Mature street trees put branches against old rooflines, and homes from the 1880s through the 1920s have the exact gaps that structural wildlife exploits — worn fascia, settled soffits, gable louvers with failing screen, and chimneys that have lost their caps.

**[Bats](/pest-library/bats/)** are the most common and the most legally sensitive. Wisconsin protects its bats — four cave species are state-threatened, and white-nose syndrome hit the state in 2014 — so exclusion is regulated by season. The **DNR requires that exit points stay open June 1 through July 31**, the maternity window when flightless pups are in the roost; sealing during that stretch traps and kills the pups inside your walls. The right process is to find the exits at dusk, install one-way doors outside the closed season, wait at least a week for the colony to clear, then seal and clean the guano. We walk through the full legal timeline on the [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/), and handling [bats in an attic](/pest-problems/bats-in-attic/) is a routine job for us.

**[Squirrels](/pest-library/squirrels/)** chew into attics through weak roof edges and old louvers, especially in fall and late winter, and **[raccoons](/pest-library/raccoons/)** den in uncapped chimneys and torn soffits — females looking for a place to raise young in spring are the usual culprits. Both leave contamination: chewed wiring, soiled insulation, latrines. The fix is the same discipline as bats — humane removal, then exclusion so the next animal can't reuse the opening, then cleanup. We cover it on the [wildlife removal](/wildlife-removal/) page and the [attic wildlife removal guide](/guides/attic-wildlife-removal/). The pest-only franchises dispatching out of the Fox Valley won't do any of it.
