## East De Pere: the original city on the river

De Pere is split down the middle by the **Fox River**, and the two sides have different histories and different housing. The **east side** is the original De Pere. When the city was first platted in 1836 by John Penn Arndt and Charles Tullar, and when the 1839 division created separate towns, **De Pere sat on the east bank** while the west side remained part of Howard until the two consolidated in 1890. The name itself comes from **"Rapides Des Pères"** — the rapids of the fathers — after the St. Francis Xavier Mission that Father Claude-Jean Allouez established at the rapids in 1671. Today the city holds about **25,410 people** across both banks.

For pest control, the east side reads as two zones. Near the historic **downtown and the riverfront** you have the older, settled housing — homes from the late 1800s and early 1900s with the entry points that come with age. Extend away from the river and you reach the mid-century and newer east-side neighborhoods. Both get pests; they just let them in differently.

**BugBoss The X-Terminator** is not a De Pere company. We run out of **Clintonville**, in rural Waupaca County, and the east side of De Pere sits at the edge of the metro area we reach. We won't pretend we're the nearest truck to a riverfront address. What we are is the rural exclusion and wildlife specialist worth calling when the problem is one we're built for — **fall invaders, rodents, carpenter ants, and attic wildlife** — the work we do every week on old farmhouses out in the country, and the work the metro franchises won't take without a contract.

## Older riverfront homes: where the pests get in

An older home near downtown De Pere has weak points a builder today would seal automatically. The **foundations** — early poured concrete, block, and some stone — have settled and cracked over a century of Wisconsin freeze-thaw, and a mouse needs only a dime-sized gap. **Soffit and fascia** boards have loosened, aging windows have gapped at the sash, and older masonry **chimneys** near the river have lost their caps. Many of these homes have real, spacious **attics** — good harborage for overwintering insects and wildlife.

The newer east-side neighborhoods are tighter but predictable. Fall invaders and mice exploit the **construction junctions**: soffit returns and ridge vents, weep holes in brick veneer, gaps around HVAC and plumbing penetrations, garage-door corners, and unsealed sill plates behind the siding. New construction changes where you seal, not whether you have to.

Exclusion — physically sealing the openings — is the durable fix on either type, and it takes someone who understands the building. That's the core of what we do.

## The fall invaders: the east side's signature problem

Every September and October, east-side De Pere gets the wave that hits every riverfront, tree-lined neighborhood in our territory. **Cluster flies**, **boxelder bugs**, and **Asian lady beetles** mass on the warm south- and west-facing walls, then work inside to overwinter. On a mild day in February they wake in the wall voids and turn up at the windows, looking like a fresh invasion.

[Cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/) are the worst of them, and they have nothing to do with housekeeping. Their larvae **parasitize earthworms** in the soil, so any home with established lawns near the Fox River corridor produces them regardless of how clean it is. [Boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/) feed on boxelder and maple seeds — trees De Pere has in abundance — and [Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/) pile in beside them. All three overwinter in the same wall voids and attics.

What works on the east bank is a **timed early-fall exterior treatment** on the river-facing and south walls that catch the afternoon sun, together with sealing the seams the flies actually use. Late August into early September, before they mass on the siding — treat after they are in the wall and you have missed the year. Our [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/) covers the timing, and if they are already stacking up on the glass, see [cluster flies in windows](/pest-problems/cluster-flies-in-windows/).

## Carpenter ants and mice near the river

**Carpenter ants** thrive in older riverfront housing. They don't eat wood — they excavate it to nest — and they target wood already softened by moisture. Decades of small roof leaks, gutter overflow, ice dams, and drips around aging plumbing leave soft spots in sills, window bays, and framing. Big black ants active indoors in winter mean a nest inside the heated structure; winged **swarmers** at the windows in spring mean the same. Fixing it means locating the nest, treating it, and correcting the moisture — the [carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) page explains why humidity near the river makes it worse.

**Mice** peak September through November, and settled foundations near the water are open doors. Trapping clears what's inside; sealing the building — foundation, penetrations, garage corners, and soffit gaps — is what stops the flow. The [mice](/pest-library/mice/) page walks through where they get in. And [wasps and hornets](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) round out the warm season, nesting under eaves and deck structures and turning aggressive in August.

## Wildlife along the Fox River and the dam

The **Fox River** and the greenway around the **De Pere dam** bring wildlife right up to east-side rooflines, and this is where a rural specialist earns the drive from Clintonville. Homes near the water see more attic and chimney intrusions than an interior lot.

**Bats** roost in the older attics and behind loose fascia. Exclusion is legally restricted — Wisconsin protects its bats, and the **DNR keeps one-way exits open June 1 through July 31** during the maternity season, when flightless pups are in the roost. Sealing then traps and kills the pups, which is illegal and a worse mess. We do the work in the legal window: find the exits at dusk, install one-way doors, wait at least a week, then seal and clean up the guano. The rules are in our [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/), and the process is on the [bats](/pest-library/bats/) and [bats in attic](/pest-problems/bats-in-attic/) pages.

**Squirrels** chew into attics at aged roof edges and gable vents — a [squirrels in attic](/pest-problems/squirrels-in-attic/) job that ends with sealing every access point. **Raccoons** den in older chimneys and torn soffits, especially females in spring; that's a [raccoon in chimney](/pest-problems/raccoon-in-chimney/) situation needing humane removal and a proper cap. All of it is our [wildlife removal](/wildlife-removal/) work, detailed in the [attic wildlife removal guide](/guides/attic-wildlife-removal/), and it's the capability the metro pest franchises generally don't offer — see the [raccoons](/pest-library/raccoons/) and [squirrels](/pest-library/squirrels/) pages.

## Beyond the fall invaders: the rest of the east-side picture

Cluster flies, mice, and wildlife lead the list, but the east side carries a broader mix. **Nuisance ants** — pavement ants trailing out of driveway, sidewalk, and patio cracks, and odorous house ants in the kitchen — are a common warm-season call, especially in the newer neighborhoods. These aren't the wood-damaging carpenter ants, and spraying the visible trail only scatters them; the fix is treating the colony at its source.

**Spiders** follow their food. They gather where the insect supply is good — porches, garages, and eaves that pull in flying insects at night, and the well-lit doorways of the older downtown homes. Sweeping webs off the soffit does nothing lasting; an exterior program that knocks down the insects they feed on is what actually thins them out.

**Ticks** matter along the river greenway and the wooded edges of the newer east-side subdivisions, where lawns meet cover. Deer ticks carry Lyme disease and wait in the tall grass along those margins, so families with kids and dogs backing onto green space are the ones at risk. A perimeter treatment targets that transition zone.

Downtown De Pere near the dam is a genuine draw — a walkable business district, riverfront events, restaurants and taverns — and warm-weather crowds and food concentrate stinging insects and rodents around venues and the buildings near them. And winter on the east side is mostly about what already got in during fall: **mice** breeding in a warm wall void, and overwintering flies and beetles waking at the windows on a mild day. Sealing in September and October is what keeps the winter quiet, which is why we push fall exclusion instead of chasing individual insects through the cold months.
