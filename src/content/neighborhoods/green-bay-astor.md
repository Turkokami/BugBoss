## Astor: Green Bay's oldest neighborhood, and its oldest pest problems

The Astor Historic District covers roughly **39 square blocks** in southeast Green Bay, running from E. Mason Street down to Grignon Street and from Webster Avenue west to the **Fox River**. It was platted in **1835** by A.G. Ellis, and in 1838 it merged with the neighboring Town of Navarino to form what became the city of Green Bay. The oldest structures cluster on the streets nearest the river — the natural ridge along the Fox drained better than the swampy ground to the north, so this is where the first settlers built. The district went on the National Register of Historic Places in 1980, and its greatest run of building came between **1895 and 1929**, when Astor housed the city's business and civic leadership.

For a pest company, that history is the whole story. Astor's homes span roughly **1835 to 1920** and run the full catalog of nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century construction: Greek Revival, Gothic Revival, Italianate, Queen Anne, and the châteauesque and Prairie-style houses that came later. Beautiful buildings — and every one of them was framed and founded before anyone thought about air-sealing, weatherstripping, or a continuous foundation. That's exactly what pests exploit.

## Why old homes leak pests: the Astor entry points

A century-old house has weak points a builder today would never leave open. In Astor we see them in the same places over and over.

The **foundations** are rubble stone or early block, and after a hundred-plus Wisconsin freeze-thaw cycles they've cracked, shifted, and lost mortar. A mouse needs a gap the size of a dime; an Astor foundation offers dozens. Above the foundation, many of these homes use **balloon framing** — the wall studs run in one uninterrupted vertical channel from the sill plate all the way to the attic. That means a mouse or a cluster fly entering low at the sill can turn up two floors higher, inside a bedroom wall or the attic, with nothing to stop it along the way. It's why an Astor homeowner finds flies at an upstairs window when the actual entry point is down at the ground.

The **rooflines** on Queen Anne and Italianate houses are a maze of dormers, turrets, bays, and decorative trim, and every junction is a seam. Soffit and fascia boards have pulled away, original wood-frame windows have gapped at the sash, and the tall old **masonry chimneys** have lost their caps. Add the deep, spacious attics these homes were built with, and you have ideal harborage for everything from overwintering flies to a bat colony.

Exclusion — physically sealing these openings — is the durable fix, and on an Astor home it takes someone who understands the building, not just someone with a sprayer. Screening a weep hole or a chimney without wrecking the historic character is part of the job.

## The fall invaders: Astor's signature problem

If you own an old home near the Fox River, you know the September-October ritual. **Cluster flies**, **boxelder bugs**, and **Asian lady beetles** mass by the hundreds on the warm south- and west-facing walls, then start finding their way inside. On a warm day in February they reappear at the windows, sluggish and confused, and it feels like a fresh infestation — but it's the fall population waking up in your wall voids.

[Cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/) are the worst of them here, and they have nothing to do with how clean your house is. Their larvae **parasitize earthworms** in the soil, so any home near the river corridor, old lawns, and mature plantings produces them regardless of housekeeping. [Boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/) feed on boxelder and maple seeds — trees Astor has in abundance — and [Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/) pile in right alongside them. All three overwinter in the same wall voids and attics.

The control that actually works is a **timed early-fall exterior treatment** on those sunny walls, combined with sealing the seams they use to get in. Do it in late August or early September, before the masses arrive, and you head off the whole winter's problem. Wait until they're already inside and you're left vacuuming. Our [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/) lays out the timing, and if you've already got them stacking up at the glass, the [cluster flies in windows](/pest-problems/cluster-flies-in-windows/) page covers what to do about it.

## Carpenter ants and mice in a hundred-year-old house

**Carpenter ants** love Astor. They don't eat wood — they excavate it to nest — and they go for wood that's already been softened by moisture. A century of small roof leaks, gutter overflow, ice dams, and drips around old plumbing has left soft spots in sills, window bays, and framing throughout these homes. Big black ants active indoors in the dead of winter mean a nest inside the heated structure, not foragers wandering in. Winged **swarmers** at the windows in spring mean the same thing. Fixing it means locating the nest, treating it, and correcting the moisture that invited it — see the [carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) page.

**Mice** are the other constant. They peak from September through November, and an old foundation full of settlement cracks is an open door. Because balloon-framed walls let them travel vertically, a mouse that enters at the sill can nest in the attic insulation two floors up. Trapping clears the ones already inside, but the fix is sealing the building — every gap, at the foundation, the utility penetrations, and the old coal-chute and basement-window openings these homes tend to have. The [mice](/pest-library/mice/) page walks through where they get in.

## Wildlife in Astor: bats, squirrels, and old chimneys

Astor's old attics and tall masonry chimneys make it some of the best wildlife habitat in the city, and this is where a rural specialist genuinely earns the drive from Clintonville.

**Bats** roost in the big open attics and behind loose fascia on these Victorians. Getting them out is not a spray job and it's not a job you can do whenever it's convenient — Wisconsin protects its bats, and four cave species are state-threatened. The **DNR requires one-way bat exits to stay open June 1 through July 31**, during the maternity season when flightless pups are in the roost. Sealing the building then traps and kills the pups, which is both illegal and a far worse mess. We do exclusion in the legal window: find the exits at dusk, install one-way doors, wait at least a week, then seal and clean up the guano. The details are in our [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/), the [bats](/pest-library/bats/) page, and the [bats in attic](/pest-problems/bats-in-attic/) walkthrough.
