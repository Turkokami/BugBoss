## New homes on old fields: the signature problem

The defining reality of North Appleton's growth areas is simple and it surprises new homeowners every fall: **a new house does not mean a pest-free house.** When a subdivision goes up on a former field, the animals and insects that lived in that ground don't vanish — they move into the new structures. The **[mice](/pest-library/mice/)** and voles that overwintered in the field edges push into the fresh houses as the weather cools. The **[cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/)** whose larvae parasitize earthworms in the field soil produce a heavy local population that masses on the new homes' sunny walls every September. It has nothing to do with how new or clean the house is; it's about what was in the ground first.

New construction also isn't sealed the way people assume. A modern home still has **utility and plumbing penetrations, garage-door corners that don't meet tight, brick-veneer weep holes, dryer and HVAC penetrations, and gaps at the sill plate** — every one of them an open door for a mouse or a fall invader. The mice enter through a dime-sized gap; the cluster flies and boxelder bugs work in behind the siding and settle into the wall voids to overwinter, then reappear at the [windows](/pest-problems/cluster-flies-in-windows/) on warm winter and spring days. The single most valuable thing a new north-side home can do is a **thorough exterior seal in its first fall** — it pays for itself across every winter after. We cover the full cluster-fly approach in the [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/) and the rodent side in the [farmhouse rodent exclusion guide](/guides/farmhouse-rodent-exclusion/), which applies directly to homes on old ag ground.

Mice in a new home are the most common call, and the most misunderstood. Trapping clears what's already inside, but if the entry points stay open the field keeps resupplying — a handful in November becomes an infestation by February. The real fix is sealing the building, especially the garage and the sill. Handling [mice in the kitchen walls](/pest-problems/mice-in-kitchen-walls/) usually traces back to exactly those unsealed penetrations.

## The established streets: older homes, older problems

The settled parts of North Appleton behave like any aged neighborhood. **[Carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/)** are the top concern — the mature trees hold parent colonies, and satellite nests move into moisture-softened wood on the house: wet deck ledgers, leaking sills, damp garage walls. You see the big black ants on the counter or swarmers at a spring window; the nest is hidden in wet wood or an old tree. A [carpenter-ant problem in a deck](/pest-problems/carpenter-ants-in-deck/) is a routine call here. **[Boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/)** and **[Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/)** join the cluster flies on the fall-invader roster, massing on warm walls and overwintering in the voids of older homes.

Then there are the year-round background pests that show up on any north-side street. **[Ants](/pest-library/ants/)** trail in from the beds and foundations, especially after rain. **[Spiders](/pest-library/spiders/)** collect in garages, basements, and window wells — and they're really a symptom, following the insect supply drawn to exterior lights and moisture, so knocking down the food source and sealing the perimeter fades them out. And **[ticks](/pest-library/ticks/)** wait along the edges where mowed lawn meets the brush and tall grass near Plamann Park, Memorial Park, and the field margins at the city's edge; the blacklegged ticks here carry Lyme disease.

## Wasps in summer, wildlife in the attic

Summer on the north side is **[wasp and hornet](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/)** season. The mix of open, sunny new-subdivision lots and mature established yards gives them everything they want — paper wasps under eaves and deck rails, yellowjackets in the ground and in wall voids, bald-faced hornets in the trees. Colonies peak in size and aggression by August, which is when the stings and most of the wasp calls come. We remove the nest and seal the void so it isn't reused.

The **wildlife** side is concentrated on the established streets and the park-and-field edges, where homes back up to trees and open ground. **[Bats](/pest-library/bats/)** slip behind fascia and through gable and soffit gaps; Wisconsin protects its bats, and the **DNR requires exit points stay open June 1 through July 31** during the maternity season, so exclusion has to be done outside that window — one-way doors, wait a week for the colony to clear, then seal and clean the guano, never sealing bats inside. ## What a north-side homeowner can do, and where it stops

There's real value in the basics, and we'll tell you straight what you can handle yourself. On a new subdivision home, keep mulch and decorative stone from burying the sill, check that the overhead garage door seals at the corners, screen the dryer and HVAC penetrations, and get an exterior seal done in the first fall before the field population moves in. On an established home, keep firewood off the foundation, trim tree limbs back from the roofline so squirrels and raccoons lose their bridge, keep gutters clear so the wood by the fascia stays dry, and knock down the first paper-wasp nests in spring before the colonies build. These steps genuinely cut pressure.

Where it stops is the point most homeowners hit: a mouse problem that keeps refilling because the real entry is a buried utility penetration or the sill plate, cluster flies that need a properly timed exterior treatment, a carpenter-ant colony whose parent nest is out in a tree, or any bat or wildlife issue that carries legal timing and contamination cleanup. DIY knocks down what you can see and lets the source rebuild. That's the line where calling BugBoss saves money instead of spending it — and with no contract, you can call for the one job over your head without signing up for anything.
