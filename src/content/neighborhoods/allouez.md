## Allouez: a settled village between two rivers

Allouez is one of the older established communities in the Green Bay metro, and its character shapes its pest picture. The village sits **between Green Bay to the north and De Pere to the south**, with the **Fox River forming its western border** and the **East River running down its eastern side**. Soldiers first called the spot Shanttown in the early 1800s; Judge James Doty arrived in 1824 and renamed it Menomineeville, filing the plat in 1829. In **1874, Allouez separated from the town of Bellevue** to become its own entity — it marked its 150th anniversary in 2024 — and it takes its name from **Father Claude-Jean Allouez**, the French Jesuit missionary.

Today Allouez is home to about **14,156 people** across **5.15 square miles**, and it's essentially built out: 100% urban, with roughly **5,575 housing units** and a very low **3.0% vacancy rate**. That's the key fact for pest control. Allouez isn't new subdivisions on former farmland — it's a **mature, settled village** of established homes, deep lawns, and big old trees, much of it dating from the first half and middle of the twentieth century. Settled housing means settled pest problems.

We won't pretend we're the closest truck to a village address. ## Older, settled homes: where the pests get in

An established village home has weak points a builder today would seal automatically. In Allouez we see them repeat from street to street.

The **foundations** — older poured concrete and block — have settled and cracked over sixty, eighty, a hundred years of Wisconsin freeze-thaw, and a mouse needs only a dime-sized gap to use one. **Soffit and fascia** boards have loosened and pulled, original or aging windows have gapped at the sash, and older masonry **chimneys** have lost their caps. Many of these homes have real, spacious **attics** rather than the shallow trussed cavities of new construction — good harborage for overwintering insects and for wildlife.

Then there are the **mature trees and established plantings** Allouez is known for. Big boxelders and maples feed fall-invader populations directly, overhanging branches hand squirrels a bridge to the roof, and old root systems and rich soil keep the cluster fly supply steady. A leafy, settled neighborhood is beautiful — and it's exactly the setting these pests evolved to exploit.

Exclusion — physically sealing the openings — is the durable fix on a home like this, and it takes someone who understands the building. That's the core of what we do.

## The fall invaders: Allouez's signature problem

Every September and October, Allouez gets the wave that hits every settled, tree-lined neighborhood in our territory. **Cluster flies**, **boxelder bugs**, and **Asian lady beetles** mass on the warm south- and west-facing walls, then find their way inside to overwinter. On a mild day in February they wake up in the wall voids and turn up at the windows, sluggish and confused, and it looks like a fresh infestation.

[Cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/) are the worst of them, and they have nothing to do with housekeeping. Their larvae **parasitize earthworms** in the soil, so any home with established lawns near the Fox and East River corridors produces them regardless of how clean it is. [Boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/) feed on boxelder and maple seeds — trees Allouez has in abundance — and [Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/) pile in beside them. All three overwinter in the same wall voids and attics.

The control that works is a **timed early-fall exterior treatment** on those sunny walls, combined with sealing the seams. Do it in late August or early September, before the masses arrive, and you head off the whole winter's problem. Our [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/) covers the timing, and if they're already stacking up on the glass, see [cluster flies in windows](/pest-problems/cluster-flies-in-windows/).

## Carpenter ants and mice in a settled village

**Carpenter ants** are at home in Allouez. They don't eat wood — they excavate it to nest — and they target wood already softened by moisture. Decades of small roof leaks, gutter overflow, ice dams, and drips around aging plumbing leave soft spots in sills, window bays, and framing throughout these homes. Big black ants active indoors in winter mean a nest inside the heated structure, not foragers wandering in; winged **swarmers** at the windows in spring mean the same. Fixing it means locating the nest, treating it, and correcting the moisture — see the [carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/) page.

**Mice** are the other constant, peaking September through November. Settled foundations full of cracks are open doors, and with two river corridors bounding the village the supply is steady. Trapping clears what's inside; sealing the building — foundation, penetrations, garage corners, and soffit gaps — is what stops the flow. The [mice](/pest-library/mice/) page walks through where they get in. And [wasps and hornets](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/) round out the warm season, nesting under eaves and in wall voids and turning aggressive in August.

## Wildlife along the Fox and East River corridors

The **Fox River** on the west and the **East River** on the east both bring wildlife right up to Allouez rooflines, and this is where a rural specialist genuinely earns the drive from Clintonville. Homes backing onto the river greenway see more attic and chimney intrusions than an interior lot.

**Bats** roost in the older attics and behind loose fascia. Exclusion is legally restricted — Wisconsin protects its bats, four cave species are state-threatened, and the **DNR keeps one-way exits open June 1 through July 31** during the maternity season, when flightless pups are in the roost. Sealing the building then traps and kills the pups, which is illegal and a worse mess. We do the work in the legal window: find the exits at dusk, install one-way doors, wait at least a week, then seal and clean up the guano. The rules are in our [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/), and the process is on the [bats](/pest-library/bats/) and [bats in attic](/pest-problems/bats-in-attic/) pages.

**Squirrels** chew into attics at aged roof edges and gable vents — a [squirrels in attic](/pest-problems/squirrels-in-attic/) job that ends with sealing every access point, using the mature trees as their bridge. **Raccoons** den in older chimneys and torn soffits, especially females in spring; that's a [raccoon in chimney](/pest-problems/raccoon-in-chimney/) situation needing humane removal and a proper cap. All of it is our [wildlife removal](/wildlife-removal/) work, detailed in the [attic wildlife removal guide](/guides/attic-wildlife-removal/), and it's the capability the metro pest franchises generally don't offer — see the [raccoons](/pest-library/raccoons/) and [squirrels](/pest-library/squirrels/) pages.

## Beyond the fall invaders: the rest of the Allouez picture

Cluster flies, mice, and wildlife lead the list, but a settled village carries a broader mix. **Nuisance ants** — pavement ants trailing out of driveway, walkway, and patio cracks, and odorous house ants in the kitchen — are a common warm-season call. These aren't the wood-damaging carpenter ants, and spraying the visible trail only scatters them; the real fix is treating the colony at its source.

**Spiders** follow their food. They gather where the insect supply is good — porches, garages, and eaves that pull in flying insects at night, and the well-lit doorways of Allouez's older homes. Sweeping webs off the soffit does nothing lasting; an exterior program that knocks down the insects they feed on does.

**Ticks** matter along the village edges where lawns meet the wooded river greenways of the Fox and East rivers. Deer ticks carry Lyme disease and wait in the tall grass and brush along those margins, so families with kids and dogs backing onto the greenway are the ones at risk. A perimeter treatment targets that transition zone rather than the open lawn.

And winter in Allouez is mostly about what already got in during fall — **mice** breeding in a warm wall void, and the overwintering flies and beetles waking at the windows on a mild day. Sealing in September and October is what keeps the winter quiet, which is why we lean on fall exclusion instead of chasing individual insects through the cold months.
