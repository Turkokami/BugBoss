## An established west-side neighborhood, and how mid-century homes behave

The Highlands is a settled, tree-lined neighborhood on the **west side of Appleton**, in Outagamie County, built out largely through the mid-twentieth century around what is today Highlands Elementary School and the **Memorial Drive** corridor. The housing here is the classic post-war Fox Valley mix: **ranches, Cape Cods, and bungalows**, with the odd Tudor-revival detail, sitting on mature lots with the deep boulevards and wide verges that make the west side comfortable to walk. Linwood, Lutz, and Jones parks are close, and Lutz Park carries the neighborhood down to the **Fox River**. That maturity is exactly what shapes the pest picture. A mid-century home is not an old balloon-framed house with a fieldstone foundation, and it's not a brand-new subdivision build either — it's a sixty-year-old structure with sixty years of settlement, a generation or two of roof and window work, and, most important, a **mature landscape** that has grown up around it. The big shade trees, the established beds, the long-standing woodpiles and fence lines — all of it is habitat, and all of it puts pests right up against the house. The specific weak points of a ranch or Cape Cod are the long low **soffit line**, the **attached garage**, the shallow **attic**, and, on some of these homes, a **slab-on-grade** section where the living space meets the ground with no basement between.

## The pests a mature landscape brings

**[Carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ants/)** lead the list in an established neighborhood, and the mature trees are why. Carpenter ants keep their parent colony in dead and dying wood outdoors — a hollow limb, a stump, an old woodpile — and the Highlands has decades of that. From the parent colony they run satellite nests into any moisture-softened wood on the house: a deck ledger that has wicked water, a window sill under a slow leak, a damp garage wall, the wet wood around a failing gutter. You see the big black ants trailing on the counter or a scatter of winged swarmers at a spring window; the nest is somewhere you can't see. A [carpenter-ant problem in a deck](/pest-problems/carpenter-ants-in-deck/) is one of the most common calls these homes make. Spraying the visible trail does nothing — you have to find the nests and correct the moisture.

The **fall invaders** are the second signature. **[Cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/)**, **[boxelder bugs](/pest-library/boxelder-bugs/)**, and **[Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/)** gather on the sunny south and west walls in September and October and push into the soffits and wall voids to overwinter. Mid-century ranches, with their long low soffit lines and shallow attics, give the fall invaders an easy path in and a warm cavity to spend the winter. Cluster flies show up even in immaculate homes because their larvae parasitize earthworms in the soil — a home surrounded by mature lawn produces them no matter what. On a warm winter day the walls warm up and the whole crowd wakes and heads for the [windows](/pest-problems/cluster-flies-in-windows/). The real fix is an early-fall exterior treatment plus sealing, laid out in the [cluster fly control guide](/guides/cluster-fly-control/).

**[Wasps and hornets](/pest-library/wasps-hornets/)** are the summer pest here. Paper wasps love the deep eaves and soffit returns of these homes, yellowjackets nest in the ground and in wall voids, and bald-faced hornets build their big gray aerial nests in the mature trees. Colonies peak in size and aggression in August, which is when the stings and the calls come. And the everyday background pests — **[ants](/pest-library/ants/)** trailing from the beds and **[spiders](/pest-library/spiders/)** in the garage and window wells — round it out; spiders in particular are a symptom, following the insect supply drawn to exterior lights and moisture.

## Ranches, Cape Cods, and the entry points that matter

The mid-century home has its own distinct set of doors, and they're different from the ones an old downtown house has.

The **attached garage** is the biggest. It's a semi-conditioned space with an overhead door that rarely seals tight at the corners, a service door that gets left open, and a shared wall into the living space. Mice, ants, and overwintering insects use the garage as a staging area, then work through the shared wall and the utility penetrations into the house. Sealing the garage — the overhead-door corners, the service-door threshold, and the penetrations on the shared wall — is one of the highest-value exclusion steps on these homes.

The **long low soffit line** is the second. Ranches present a lot of horizontal soffit and fascia close to the ground, and sixty years of paint, weather, and the occasional ice dam open joints and pull screening loose. That's the path for cluster flies, boxelder bugs, bats, and squirrels into the shallow attic. The **slab-on-grade** sections found on some ranches and additions give ants and rodents a perimeter entry right at the slab edge, with no basement to intercept them. And the ordinary suspects — utility and dryer penetrations, the gap where siding meets foundation, worn weatherstripping — do the rest.

Exclusion on a Highlands home targets the garage, the soffit line, and the slab edge specifically. That's careful, hands-on work — finding the actual gaps and closing them with the right materials, correcting the moisture that softens wood, and treating the source rather than blanket-spraying the yard. It's the opposite of a fifteen-minute route stop, and it's what actually ends the problem.

## Bats, squirrels, and the wildlife side

The same features that make mid-century homes attractive — mature trees, long soffit lines, attached garages — make them good **wildlife** targets, and this is where hiring a company that does both pest and wildlife work pays off.

**[Bats](/pest-library/bats/)** get behind the fascia and through gable and soffit vents on these homes, using the mature trees as a highway to the roof. Wisconsin protects its bats, and the **DNR requires exit points stay open June 1 through July 31** during the maternity season, when flightless pups are in the roost — sealing then traps and kills them inside your walls. The correct process is to find the exits at dusk, install one-way doors outside that window, wait at least a week for the colony to clear, then seal and clean. We lay out the whole timeline on the [Wisconsin bat exclusion law guide](/guides/wisconsin-bat-exclusion-law/), and [bats in the attic](/pest-problems/bats-in-attic/) is routine work for us.

**[Squirrels](/pest-library/squirrels/)** chew into shallow attics through weak roof edges and vents, especially in fall and late winter, and **[raccoons](/pest-library/raccoons/)** den in chimneys and torn soffits, with spring mothers the usual cause. **[Skunks](/pest-library/skunks/)** den under the decks, porches, and sheds common in these established yards. Every one of these is a removal-plus-exclusion job — get the animal out humanely, seal the opening so the next one can't reuse it, and clean up the contamination. ## What a Highlands homeowner can do, and where it stops

We'll always tell you straight which parts you can handle yourself. Keep firewood, mulch, and decorative stone off the foundation and away from the wood sill. Trim the mature trees back from the roofline so squirrels, raccoons, and bats lose their bridge onto the shallow attic. Keep the gutters clear so the fascia and soffit wood stays dry — wet wood is what invites carpenter ants and softens the entry points. Weatherstrip the overhead garage door and its corners, since the attached garage is the biggest single door on these homes. And knock down the first paper-wasp nests in spring, before the colony builds through summer. These basics genuinely lower the pressure on a mid-century home.

Where it stops is where most homeowners land: carpenter ants whose parent nest is out in a tree and whose satellites are in wet framing you can't see, cluster flies that need an exterior treatment timed to early fall, a rodent problem that keeps refilling because the real gap is at the slab edge or a shared garage wall, or any bat, raccoon, or skunk issue that carries legal timing and contamination cleanup. DIY handles the visible layer and lets the source rebuild. That's the line where calling BugBoss saves money — and with no contract, you call for the one job that's over your head and nothing more.
