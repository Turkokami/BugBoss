faqs:
  - q: "Why had it gone on for years?"
    a: "Almost always for the same reason: the building was being treated one unit at a time. Bed bugs move through wall voids, along plumbing and electrical runs, and under doors, so treating the unit that complained while leaving its neighbours alone simply relocates the problem — and it comes back through the same wall a few weeks later. Every round of that costs money and buys nothing, which is how a building ends up years into an infestation with a file full of invoices."
  - q: "What makes a multi-unit job different?"
    a: "Scope and coordination, more than technique. Every unit has to be inspected, not just the ones reporting bites, because early infestations are easy to miss and a single untreated unit reseeds the building. Tenants have to be given real preparation instructions in advance and access has to be arranged for each unit, which needs the owner's involvement. And it always takes more than one visit — a treatment plan for a building this size is a sequence with follow-ups built in from the start, not a single pass."
  - q: "Who is responsible for bed bugs in a Wisconsin rental?"
    a: "Wisconsin has no bed-bug-specific statute, so responsibility comes from the landlord's duty to keep the property habitable, the lease, and who caused it — which with bed bugs is usually impossible to prove. In practice the landlord normally has to arrange treatment and the tenant has to prepare the unit and allow access. Our page on bed bugs in Wisconsin rentals covers this properly, because arguing about blame while the infestation spreads is how a one-unit problem becomes an eight-unit one."
  - q: "How do you know when a building is actually clear?"
    a: "By following up rather than assuming. Bed bug work is judged over weeks, through repeat inspection of every unit involved, not by the absence of complaints after a treatment — tenants stop reporting for all sorts of reasons. A building is finished when inspection stops finding activity across the whole structure, which is a different and much higher bar than a quiet fortnight in one apartment."
  - q: "What should a property owner do first?"
    a: "Call before treating one unit. The instinct is to deal with the apartment that complained and hope, and that instinct is what turns a manageable problem into a multi-year one. Getting the whole building inspected at the outset costs far less than a year of single-unit treatments, and it is the difference between a plan and a treadmill. For owners with several buildings we would rather agree a written response protocol before there is an outbreak."
