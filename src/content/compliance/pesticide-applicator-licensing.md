## The rule, stated plainly

**In Wisconsin, applying pesticides commercially requires the individual applicator to be certified by DATCP in the categories covering the work, and the business to be licensed.**

Certification is not paperwork. It means the person standing in your kitchen has passed examinations covering pesticide safety, label law, application technique, environmental protection, and the specific pest category they are certified in. It also means they are subject to record-keeping requirements and to enforcement action if they get it wrong.

The program sits under Wisconsin's pesticide statutes and administrative rules, administered by **DATCP's Bureau of Agrichemical Management**.

You are entitled to ask for the number before anyone treats your property. You are also entitled to verify it rather than take somebody's word.

## Who this applies to

**Anyone applying pesticides for hire** in Wisconsin — pest control operators, lawn and turf companies, mosquito services, agricultural applicators.

**The businesses employing them**, which need their own licensing.

**Homeowners hiring any of the above**, who are the people this page is actually written for.

Homeowners treating their own property with general-use retail products sit in a different position — though the label still binds them, which is covered below.

## What the certification actually covers

Wisconsin certifies commercial applicators **by category**, and this is the part almost nobody asks about.

Each category corresponds to a type of work. Structural pest control is one. Aquatic and mosquito is another. Turf, agricultural, right-of-way, and others exist separately. **Certification in one category does not qualify an applicator for another.**

The practical consequence: a company certified for lawn and turf work is not thereby certified to treat inside your home. If you hire a lawn company to deal with mice in the walls, ask what category covers that.

So the question to ask is not "are you licensed?" — to which the answer is always yes — but:

> **"What is your certification number, what categories does it cover, and when does it expire?"**

Three specifics, verifiable with DATCP, and a legitimate operator will give you all three without friction.

## The obligations

### On the applicator

- Hold current certification in the categories covering the work performed
- Work under a properly licensed business
- **Apply according to the label** — under federal and state law, the label is the law, and it governs sites, rates, re-entry intervals, and prohibitions
- Maintain required application records
- Renew certification before it lapses

### On you, before you hire

- Ask for the number, the categories, and the expiry
- Verify with DATCP rather than trusting the card
- Ask about insurance separately — licensing and insurance are different things
- Ask what records you will receive after treatment
- Get the scope in writing

## What happens if it is ignored

**For the applicator:** operating without valid certification or licensing exposes them to DATCP enforcement. Improper application — off-label use, wrong site, wrong rate — is separately actionable, and DATCP's Bureau of Agrichemical Management handles pesticide use complaints.

**For you, as the customer:** the real exposure. An uncertified operator is usually uninsured too, so damage to your property or harm to your household has no backstop. Off-label application in an occupied building is a genuine health hazard. And for a business, a treatment performed by someone who cannot produce compliant records is a finding waiting to happen at your next inspection or third-party audit.

**A practical note for businesses:** food processing, healthcare, schools, and any audited facility will be asked to produce pest control service records. An applicator who does not routinely supply them will fail you when it matters, regardless of how good the treatment was.
