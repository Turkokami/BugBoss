## Rural Wisconsin is in the Lyme disease hot zone, and it isn't close

If you live on a wooded lot, a lake cabin, or a farmstead in Waupaca or Shawano County, you are living inside one of the higher-risk regions in the country for Lyme disease. That is not an exaggeration meant to sell a service — it's the geography. Wisconsin is consistently one of the highest-incidence states for Lyme in the United States, the blacklegged tick that carries it is firmly established across the northern and central parts of the state, and the Wisconsin Department of Health Services reports that the average number of Lyme cases has roughly **tripled over the past fifteen years**. The ticks are not retreating. They're expanding, and the mild, brushy, wooded-edge country around here is exactly the habitat they like best.

BugBoss The X-Terminator runs tick prevention yard programs across that country from our base at **600 S. Main St. in Clintonville**. This guide is the straight version of what you actually need to know: which tick is the dangerous one, when the risk peaks, where the ticks live on your property, what a yard program does and doesn't do, and how landscaping and personal habits fit alongside treatment. Everything here is grounded in public-health guidance from DHS and the CDC — no fearmongering, no magic-bullet claims.

## The dangerous tick is the small one

The single most important thing to understand about ticks in Wisconsin is that the tick most people worry about is not the one that gives you Lyme.

The **American dog tick**, which most Wisconsinites grew up calling a "wood tick," is the larger, reddish-brown tick you'd find crawling up your pant leg after mowing. It's a real pest and it can transmit Rocky Mountain spotted fever and tularemia, but **it does not carry Lyme disease.** It's big enough that you usually find it and flick it off before it settles in.

The **blacklegged tick** — the "deer tick" — is the one that matters for Lyme, and it's much smaller. The adult female is about the size of a sesame seed, and the nymph, which is the life stage responsible for most human Lyme cases, is about the size of a **poppy seed.** That size is the whole problem. A poppy-seed-sized tick embedded behind your knee or in your scalp is easy to miss for the day or two it needs to transmit disease. The dangerous ticks are the ones you don't notice, which is why "I always check for ticks" is not the protection people think it is when they're picturing a fat wood tick.

|---|---|---|
| Size | Larger, ~apple-seed | Small; nymph ~poppy-seed |
| Color | Reddish-brown, patterned | Dark, blacklegged |
| Carries Lyme? | No | Yes |
| Also carries | RMSF, tularemia | Anaplasmosis, babesiosis, Powassan virus |
| Peak stage of concern | Adults, late spring/summer | Nymphs (spring/summer), adults (fall) |

## Lyme is not the only disease these ticks carry

Lyme gets the headlines, but the blacklegged tick carries a suite of infections, and cases of the others have been climbing in Wisconsin right alongside Lyme:

- **Lyme disease** — the classic infection, often (but not always) announced by an expanding "bull's-eye" rash (erythema migrans) that appears in roughly 70–80% of cases, along with fever, fatigue, and joint aches.
- **Anaplasmosis** — a bacterial infection causing fever, chills, headache, and muscle aches; increasingly common in northern Wisconsin.
- **Babesiosis** — a malaria-like infection of the red blood cells, which can be serious in older or immune-compromised people.
- **Powassan virus** — rare but very serious, a viral infection with no specific treatment that can cause encephalitis. Unlike Lyme, it can transmit within hours of attachment.

Because a single infected tick can carry more than one of these, a person bitten once can end up with a **co-infection.** The practical takeaway is simple: the goal is not to watch for the Lyme rash after the fact. The goal is to avoid the bite in the first place.

## The tick life cycle explains the whole problem

To understand why the risk peaks when it does, and why mice matter as much as deer, it helps to know how a blacklegged tick actually lives. The tick goes through four stages over roughly a **two-year** cycle: egg, larva, nymph, and adult. It takes a single blood meal at each of the three active stages, and — this is the key point — **a tick isn't born infected.** It picks up the Lyme bacterium by feeding on an infected host.

That host, for the larval and nymphal stages, is usually a small mammal — above all the **white-footed mouse**, which is the primary reservoir of Lyme bacteria in Wisconsin. A larva hatches uninfected, feeds on an infected mouse, and molts into an infected nymph. The following spring and summer, that infected nymph — poppy-seed small and easy to miss — goes looking for its next meal, and if that meal is you, it can transmit Lyme. The adults prefer larger hosts, especially **deer**, which don't transmit Lyme to the tick but are the animals that feed and transport the adult ticks and let the population reproduce.

This is why tick control has two levers beyond spraying: reducing the **mice and chipmunks** that infect young ticks (rodent control around buildings, removing woodpiles and brush that shelter them) and reducing the **deer** that sustain the adult population and carry ticks onto your land (fencing, plant choices, not feeding them). The whole ecology runs through your yard, which is exactly why yard-level management works.

## The two peaks: when Wisconsin tick risk is highest

Blacklegged ticks are active any time the ground is unfrozen and temperatures are above roughly freezing — they'll quest on a mild, snowless day in December or March. But the risk isn't flat across the year. There are two peaks that a smart prevention program is built around.

**Late spring into early summer (roughly May through July)** is the high season, and it's driven by the **nymphs.** This tiny, poppy-seed-sized stage becomes active as the weather warms, and because they're so easy to overlook and so abundant, nymphs cause the majority of human Lyme cases. This is the single most dangerous window of the year, and it lines up exactly with the season people are back outside gardening, hiking, and opening the cabin.

**Fall** brings the second surge, when **adult** blacklegged ticks become active and quest through the cooler months. Adults are larger and easier to spot, but they're still out in numbers hunting a host — often a deer, sometimes you or your dog — right through the first frosts and beyond.

A yard program is timed to these peaks: a **spring treatment** ahead of and into the nymph season, and a **fall treatment** for the adult surge, with the exact timing adjusted to the season.

## Where ticks actually live on your property

Here is the good news buried in the tick problem: ticks are not evenly spread across your yard, and they hate the places people spend most of their time. A blacklegged tick will dry out and die in the open, sunny, mowed middle of a lawn — it can't tolerate the low humidity there. So ticks concentrate in a predictable set of **edge and transition habitats:**

- **Where the lawn meets the woods** — the single biggest tick zone on a rural Wisconsin lot.
- **Tall grass, brush, and unmowed field edges.**
- **Leaf litter** under trees and along fence lines, where humidity stays high at ground level.
- **Stone walls, woodpiles, and rock edging**, which hold the mice and chipmunks ticks feed on as juveniles.
- **Shady, damp, ground-level cover** generally — ground cover plantings, dense shrubs, the north sides of buildings.

This is why a yard tick program is not a blanket spray of your whole lawn. It's a **targeted treatment of the edge and transition zones** where the ticks actually are, plus the immediate surroundings of the areas your family uses — the play set, the fire pit, the paths to the dock or the woods. Research on this approach is encouraging: focused treatment of these zones substantially reduces the number of ticks in the treated area. It's not a force field, and you can still bring a tick home from a hike or on the dog, but knocking the population down where you live cuts your everyday exposure meaningfully.

## Landscaping: making your yard hostile to ticks

Treatment works better, and lasts longer, when the habitat itself is working against the ticks. The through-line of every landscaping recommendation is the same: **ticks need shade and humidity, so drier and sunnier means fewer ticks.** The CDC and university extension guidance converge on a handful of high-value moves:

- **Mow regularly and keep grass short.** Short turf dries fast and offers no cover.
- **Clear leaf litter and brush**, especially along the wooded edge and around the house.
- **Build a barrier.** A strip of gravel or wood chips about three feet wide between the lawn and the woods (or between the lawn and a stone wall or field) creates a dry zone that ticks are reluctant to cross. This simple border is one of the most effective single steps.
- **Move the things people use into the sun.** Play sets, patios, benches, and the like belong away from the wooded edge, in the open where it's dry.
- **Relocate woodpiles and bird feeders** away from the house and out of shade — feeders draw the mice and squirrels that host young ticks and carry the pathogens.
- **Prune trees and shrubs** to let sunlight and airflow onto the ground, drying it out.
- **Discourage deer and rodents.** Deer carry adult ticks in; mice and chipmunks are the primary reservoir that infects young ticks with Lyme bacteria. Fencing, plant choices, and rodent control around the buildings all help reduce the tick supply.

None of these replace treatment, and treatment doesn't replace them. Together they compound.

## Cabins, woodlots, and the Chain O' Lakes problem

The Waupaca and Iola lake country presents the hardest version of the tick problem, because a wooded cabin lot is often **all edge habitat** — there's no sunny open lawn for the ticks to avoid, just woods, brush, and the humid ground layer they thrive in. You can't turn a forest into a golf course, and you shouldn't want to. The strategy shifts accordingly.

On a heavily wooded lot, a program focuses on the **used zones**: the immediate ring around the cabin, the paths to the dock and the outbuildings, the fire pit and gathering area, the kids' play space. Those get treated and, where possible, cleared and opened to sunlight. Beyond that, the emphasis leans harder on **personal protection** — permethrin-treated clothing, repellent, tucking pants into socks, staying on cleared paths — and on **rigorous tick checks** every time people come in from the woods. Seasonal cabins also tend to sit unused for stretches, so the surrounding vegetation creeps in; a spring visit to open the place up and knock back the immediate habitat is well worth it.

## What a yard tick treatment actually is

People sometimes picture tick treatment as fogging the whole property, and that's not it. A professional yard program applies a targeted residual product to the **specific zones where ticks quest** — the lawn-woods edge, the transition strip, the ground-level vegetation and leaf-litter margins, and the borders of the areas people use. Ticks sitting in that treated vegetation waiting for a host contact the material and die, which is why treating the right narrow zones is far more effective per gallon than blanketing open lawn where there are no ticks to begin with.

Timing is matched to the two activity peaks — a treatment as the nymphs come active in late spring, and another for the fall adult surge, with the exact schedule adjusted to how the season is running. On properties with heavy pressure, additional treatments through the peak keep the population suppressed. The residual doesn't last forever, which is why a program is a series of timed treatments rather than a single application, and why the landscaping work that makes the habitat inhospitable extends the benefit between visits.

It's also worth being honest about scope: a yard program manages *your* property. It doesn't protect you on a hike, at a park, or in the deer woods, which is where personal protection and tick checks carry the load.

## Personal protection: what to do on your own body

Yard treatment lowers the population where you live; personal habits protect you everywhere. The public-health guidance is consistent and worth following:

- **Repellent on skin** — EPA-registered products with DEET, picaridin, or oil of lemon eucalyptus, applied per the label.
- **Permethrin on clothing and gear** — permethrin-treated clothing, boots, and pants actually kills ticks on contact and holds up through washings; it's the single most effective personal measure for people who spend real time in tick habitat.
- **Dress to keep them out** — light-colored clothing to spot ticks, long pants tucked into socks, shirts tucked in, when moving through brush or tall grass.
- **Stay in the center of trails** and out of the brushy margins where ticks quest.
- **Shower within a couple hours** of coming in, and run clothes through a hot dryer, which kills ticks that hitched a ride.

## Common tick myths worth clearing up

A few beliefs get people bitten:

- *"Ticks fall out of trees."* They don't. Ticks quest by climbing low vegetation and grabbing a passing host at ground-to-knee level. They get on your head because they climbed up from your legs.
- *"If there's no bull's-eye rash, I'm fine."* Roughly 20–30% of Lyme cases never show the classic rash, and the other tick-borne diseases don't cause it at all. Watch for fever, fatigue, headache, and joint or muscle aches after any bite.
- *"Winter kills the ticks."* Adults are active any time it's above freezing and the ground isn't snow-covered, including mild winter days. Cold slows them; it doesn't reliably wipe them out.
- *"Only deep-woods hikers get Lyme."* Most people are bitten in their own yards and gardens, in the edge habitat right around the house.

## Pets are part of the equation

Dogs are tick magnets, and on a rural property a dog running the woods and field edges will find ticks you never would. Two problems follow. First, dogs get tick-borne disease themselves — canine Lyme and anaplasmosis are real and common here. Second, a dog is a delivery vehicle, carrying ticks off its coat and into the house and onto the furniture, where they can find a human host. The playbook: a **veterinary tick preventive** (talk to your vet — there are good options), a thorough **check after every outing** with attention to the ears, neck, armpits, and between the toes, and a **treated, short-mowed yard** that lowers the population the dog is exposed to in the first place. A yard program protects the pets and the people together.

## Tick checks and safe removal: the last line of defense

No yard program, no landscaping, and no repellent is a substitute for the tick check. Because Lyme transmission generally requires **24 to 36 hours of attachment**, finding and removing a blacklegged tick within the first day is the most powerful single thing you can do. After time outdoors, check thoroughly — and remember you're looking for something poppy-seed small. Pay special attention to the scalp and hairline, behind the ears, the back of the neck, the armpits, the belly button, the waistline, behind the knees, and the groin. Shower within a couple hours of coming inside; it helps wash off unattached ticks and gives you a reason to do a full check.

If you find an attached tick, remove it correctly:

1. Use **fine-tipped tweezers** and grasp the tick as close to the skin as you can.
2. Pull **straight up with steady, even pressure.** Don't twist, jerk, or squeeze the body.
3. **Skip the folklore** — no matches, no nail polish, no petroleum jelly. Those can make the tick regurgitate into the bite.
4. **Clean** the bite and your hands.
5. **Note the date**, and watch for a rash or flu-like symptoms over the following weeks. DHS notes that in Wisconsin areas where Lyme is common, a single preventive dose of antibiotics is sometimes appropriate after a bite — ask your health-care provider.

Not every property needs the same intensity, and it's worth being straight about where a program pays off most. The highest-value candidates are **rural and wooded lots**, **lake cabins**, **homes that back onto woods, fields, or wetland**, and any property with **kids and dogs spending real time outside** through the warm months. Those are the situations where edge habitat, host animals, and human exposure all line up. A tight village lot mowed to the curb with no woods nearby carries genuinely lower tick risk, and it's fine to say so. What tips a property from "watch it" to "treat it" is the presence of that lawn-meets-woods edge, brush and leaf litter, and the mice and deer that feed the tick population — the exact features that describe most rural properties in the country we serve.
