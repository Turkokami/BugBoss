## Something's in the attic — and what it is changes everything

The call usually starts the same way: "I'm hearing something up in the attic." What comes next depends entirely on *what* it is, because a bat, a squirrel, and a raccoon are three completely different problems with three different solutions, three different health risks, and — for bats — a legal calendar you're not allowed to ignore. Guess wrong, and you either waste money on the wrong fix or, worse, seal a nursing animal and its young inside your walls to die.

Rural Wisconsin is prime territory for all of this. The older farmhouses, village homes, and lake cabins out here have decades of gaps, rotted trim, and open framing for wildlife to exploit, and they sit surrounded by the trees, fields, and outbuildings that produce the wildlife in the first place. ## Step one: figure out what's up there

You often can't see the animal, so you diagnose it by its habits. The three big tells are **when it's active, what it sounds like, and what it leaves behind.**

| Animal | Active when | Sounds | Droppings | Entry size |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Squirrel** | Daytime, peaks at dawn/dusk | Fast scurrying, rolling/gnawing, light | Small, scattered, rice-to-raisin sized | ~1.5–2 in. hole, often chewed |
| **Raccoon** | Night | Heavy thumping, dragging, chittering; young chirp | Large, tubular, in a communal latrine | Large; torn soffit/vent |
| **Bat** | Exits at dusk, returns before dawn | Nearly silent; faint scratching/squeaking | Peppery pellets piled under roost | Tiny — as small as 3/8 in. |
| **Mouse** | Mostly night, constant | Light scratching, scampering in walls | Tiny dark grains, widespread | ~1/4 in. (dime) |

### If it's active in the daytime — think squirrel

[Squirrels](/pest-library/squirrels/) are day-shift animals, and the giveaway is fast, light scurrying and rolling sounds concentrated at dawn and dusk, when they're leaving and returning. You may hear gnawing — that's the sound that should worry you most, and we'll come back to why. Squirrels typically enter through a chewed hole an inch and a half to two inches across, often at a roof edge, soffit corner, gable vent, or where a lower roof meets a wall.

### If it's active at night and it's heavy — think raccoon

If the attic goes quiet during the day and comes alive at night with **heavy** thumping, dragging, and vocal chittering — sounds distinctly larger than a mouse or squirrel — you're likely dealing with a [raccoon](/pest-library/raccoons/). In spring, add chirping or crying sounds, which mean a female has denned in your attic to raise a litter. Raccoons are strong and destructive; their entry is usually a soffit, vent, or roof section physically torn open rather than neatly chewed.

### If you never hear much but bugs and dusk activity add up — think bat

[Bats](/pest-library/bats/) are the quiet ones. You may hear only faint scratching or high squeaking on hot days, but the real evidence is behavioral and physical: bats streaming out of a gap under the eaves or fascia at dusk, a smell of ammonia developing over time, and a pile of small, dark, peppery droppings (guano) accumulating directly under the roost — often on insulation, in a wall, or on the ground below an exit. Guano crumbles to dust and glitters faintly with insect-wing fragments, which distinguishes it from mouse droppings. Bats need almost nothing to get in — a gap about three-eighths of an inch.

### If it's light, constant, and everywhere — think mouse

Not everything in the attic is big. Light, scratchy, scampering sounds in the walls and ceiling, especially in fall, usually mean [mice](/pest-library/mice/), which enter through gaps as small as a dime and are a different (and simpler) job. If you're unsure, the droppings and the size of the entry hole settle it.

## How they get in: entry points on rural homes

Wildlife doesn't break into a house so much as find the weakness that's already there, and older rural Wisconsin homes offer plenty. The usual suspects:

- **Soffit and fascia gaps** — where the roof overhang meets the wall; the most common attic entry of all, especially where soffits have loosened or rotted.
- **Roof-edge and eave junctions**, and the spots where a lower roof ties into an upper wall.
- **Gable, ridge, and roof vents** — squirrels chew them, raccoons tear them open, bats slip through the gaps around them.
- **Chimneys** — a classic raccoon den site, and a way in for other animals.
- **Construction gaps and rotted or loose trim**, plus the openings where old homes have simply settled and separated over a century.

Three things make older **farmhouses** especially prone. First, **age**: decades of settlement open gaps, and trim and soffits rot and pull away. Second, **construction**: older framing — including balloon framing — leaves open cavities running from the foundation straight up to the attic, so an animal or insect that gets in low can end up high. Third, **location**: a farmhouse sits in the middle of the habitat, ringed by mature trees that act as a highway to the roofline and by fields and outbuildings full of animals looking for a better place to live. Lake cabins share the wooded-setting problem and add the vulnerability of sitting empty and undisturbed for long stretches.

Finding *every* entry point — not just the obvious one — is the heart of a professional inspection, because sealing four of five holes just funnels the animal through the fifth.

## The seasons of attic wildlife

Attic wildlife runs on a calendar:

- **Spring** is raccoon season. Females seek a safe, warm, defensible place to have and raise their young, and an attic is ideal. A raccoon in your attic in April almost certainly means a litter, which is why spring raccoon jobs have to account for babies before anything gets sealed.
- **Summer** is bat maternity season. Bats form colonies in warm attics to raise flightless pups — and this is precisely the window Wisconsin law protects, June 1 through July 31.
- **Fall** is squirrel and rodent season, as animals look for warm winter quarters ahead of the cold.
- **Late winter** brings a second squirrel push and the start of raccoon denning as the breeding season begins.

## Which squirrel, which bat — the species matter

"Squirrel" covers a few different animals in rural Wisconsin, and they behave differently in an attic. **Gray and fox squirrels** are the big daytime chewers that open two-inch holes at roof edges and vents. **Red squirrels** are smaller and, pound for pound, some of the most destructive — they hoard food and can pack an attic with cached nuts and cones and shredded insulation. **Flying squirrels** are the surprise: nocturnal, gliding, and social, they can move into an attic in numbers and are often mistaken for a nighttime rodent because you never see them by day. The nocturnal activity can point you toward raccoon or mouse when it's actually flying squirrels, which is one more reason a correct diagnosis matters before anyone starts sealing.

Bats in Wisconsin attics are usually the **big brown bat** or the **little brown bat**, both of which form maternity colonies in warm building attics in summer. That distinction isn't academic: little brown bats are among the state-threatened species hit hardest by white-nose syndrome, which is part of why Wisconsin's bat protections are as strict as they are. Correctly identifying a bat colony — rather than treating it like a nuisance pest — is what keeps an exclusion both legal and humane.

## Skunks, and the wildlife that doesn't climb

Not all the wildlife around a rural home ends up in the attic. [Skunks](/pest-library/skunks/) den at ground level — under decks, porches, sheds, and concrete stoops — and while they don't climb into your ceiling, they're part of the same rural-wildlife picture and often turn up on the same property. The concern with skunks is denning under structures (again a spring-litter issue), the spray, and the digging, and the fix is again exclusion: sealing off the under-structure spaces once the animal is confirmed out, ideally with buried barriers they can't dig under. Raccoons that don't make the roof will do the same thing under a deck or in a crawlspace, and the approach is identical — evict, exclude, clean up. The point is that a real wildlife company handles the whole property, not just the attic.

## Why exclusion beats removal every time

Here's the concept that separates real wildlife work from a guy with a trap: **removal takes out the animal that's there now; exclusion seals the building so nothing can get back in.** Removal alone is a revolving door. Trap and release a squirrel and leave its two-inch hole open, and within days another squirrel — smelling the old nest and feeling the draw of a warm cavity — moves right in. The scent of a former occupant actively attracts the next one.

Exclusion is the permanent fix: sealing every entry point with materials the animals can't chew or tear through — heavy-gauge hardware cloth, metal flashing, proper vent covers, solid repairs — after the animals are confirmed out. It's more work than setting a trap, and it's the only thing that actually ends the problem. This is why a pest-only company that won't do exclusion, and a trapper who won't do the sealing and cleanup, both leave you with a problem that comes back.

## The bat exception: Wisconsin's legal timeline

Bats get their own rules, and they're not optional. Wisconsin protects its bats — four cave-bat species are state-threatened, including the northern long-eared bat, which is also federally endangered, and white-nose syndrome has hammered their populations since it reached the state in 2014. As a result, the **Wisconsin DNR prohibits sealing bats out during the maternity season: one-way exits must stay open June 1 through July 31**, because that's when flightless pups are in the roost. Seal the building then and you trap the pups inside to die — inhumane, illegal, and a far worse problem as they decompose in your walls.

Legal bat exclusion is done *outside* that window — spring before June 1, or August onward — and it follows a set process: find the exit points at dusk, install one-way doors that let bats leave but not return, wait at least a week for the entire colony to clear, then seal. You never seal bats in. ## The health risks are real (and why cleanup matters)

Wildlife in the attic isn't just noise and chewed wood. Each animal brings specific hazards.

**Bat guano** accumulates under a roost and can grow *Histoplasma*, a fungus whose spores cause **histoplasmosis**, a respiratory illness, when disturbed guano is inhaled. There's also the small but serious risk of **rabies** from direct contact — never handle a bat bare-handed, and if a bat is found in a room with a sleeping person, a child, or a pet, treat it as a possible exposure and contact your local health department, because a bat's bite can be too small to notice.

**Raccoon latrines** carry **raccoon roundworm** (*Baylisascaris procyonis*). Raccoons defecate in communal latrines, often right in the attic, and the eggs shed in their droppings become infectious and are extremely hardy — they can persist in the environment for years and resist ordinary cleaning. In the rare cases where the eggs are ingested, usually by a young child, the larvae can migrate through the body and cause severe, sometimes fatal, neurological damage. This is exactly why raccoon cleanup is a job for professionals with proper respiratory and contact protection, not a homeowner with a shop vac.

**Squirrels' constant gnawing** is the sleeper hazard. Their teeth grow continuously, so they chew to wear them down, and attic **wiring is a frequent target.** Gnawed, exposed wiring surrounded by dry insulation and old wood is a genuine **fire risk** — rodent-gnawed wiring is a documented cause of house fires. Add the droppings, urine-soaked and torn insulation, nesting debris, and parasites (fleas, mites, ticks) that all three animals bring, and the case for thorough **cleanup and decontamination** is clear. Beyond the health angle, cleanup removes the scent cues that draw the next animal to the same spot. A finished job restores the insulation and leaves the space clean, not just empty.

## Why poison and repellents are the wrong tools

It's worth saying plainly: **do not try to poison or repel attic wildlife.** Poison leaves you with an animal dead inside an inaccessible wall or attic cavity — meaning weeks of severe odor and a blowfly and maggot problem — and it does nothing to close the hole the animal came through. Ultrasonic gadgets, mothballs, and chemical repellents are unreliable and, again, don't address the access that's the actual problem. And with protected species like bats in the mix, the wrong approach isn't just ineffective, it's illegal and can harm pets and non-target animals. There is no shortcut around the real work: get the animals out humanely, seal the building, clean up.

## Chimneys, birds, and the other ways in

The attic isn't the only cavity animals occupy. **Chimneys** are a favorite raccoon den site and a trap for other animals, and an uncapped or damaged chimney is an open invitation; a proper chimney cap is one of the highest-value exclusion pieces on an older home. **Birds** — sparrows, starlings, and in the case of chimneys, chimney swifts — nest in vents, eaves, and flues, fouling them with droppings and nesting material and, once the nest is abandoned, leaving behind the mites and beetles that lived in it. Bird exclusion follows the same logic as everything else here: screen and cap the openings so they can't nest there, after making sure nothing is currently trapped. Dryer, bathroom, and stove vents that have lost their covers are common, overlooked entries for birds and small mammals alike, and they're an easy fix once they're found.

## The cost of waiting

Attic wildlife is one of those problems that gets more expensive every week it's ignored, and the reasons are concrete. A squirrel that's been gnawing for a month has done more wiring and structural damage than one caught in the first days. A raccoon female left alone through spring raises a litter, and now you have four or five animals and a full latrine instead of one animal and a clean attic. Bats left through a full summer deposit far more guano — and if you miss the pre-June window, the law requires you to wait until August, meaning another month or more of colony growth and mess. Insulation soaked with urine loses its R-value and has to be replaced, turning a wildlife bill into an energy-and-insulation bill. And the longer the scent and the entry point persist, the more the property advertises itself to the next animal. Acting early is almost always the cheaper path.

## What a real inspection looks like

The single most important part of a wildlife job happens before any animal is touched: the inspection. A thorough exterior inspection walks the entire roofline, every soffit and fascia run, all the vents, the chimney, and every roof-to-wall intersection, looking for the rub marks, chew damage, torn screening, droppings, and grease trails that mark an active entry. Inside, the attic inspection maps where the animals are living, where the droppings and nests are, whether there are young, and what damage has already been done to insulation and wiring. For bats specifically, a **dusk watch** — observing where bats stream out at sundown — pinpoints the active exits so the one-way doors go in the right places. Skipping or rushing this step is how companies seal four of five holes and leave the customer with a problem that never ends. Finding every entry point is not a formality; it's the whole job.

## Cleanup and restoration, done right

Once the animals are confirmed out and the building is sealed, the last phase is putting the attic right. That means removing droppings and contaminated, urine-soaked, and torn insulation; addressing the parasites — fleas, mites, ticks — the animals brought in; treating and deodorizing the space to kill pathogens and remove the scent that draws repeat invaders; and restoring insulation so the attic performs the way it should. For bat guano and raccoon latrines this work is done with proper respiratory and contact protection because of the histoplasmosis and roundworm risks described above. A job that stops at "the animal's gone" leaves the health hazard and the scent behind; a finished job leaves the attic clean, sealed, and insulated.
