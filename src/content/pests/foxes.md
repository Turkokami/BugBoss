## Usually the answer is not what people expect

Somebody rings up in late April. There is a fox den under the shed, the kits are out playing in the mornings, and they want it gone.

Very often the honest answer is: **wait about six weeks and it will be gone on its own** — and then we seal the void so it does not happen again next spring.

That is not a way of avoiding the work. It is the answer that fits the biology. Fox dens under structures are overwhelmingly a spring event with a built-in end date. The vixen picks a sheltered void in March, the kits are born, they emerge and play through May, and by six to eight weeks they are mobile and moving out with the adults. The family abandons the natal den on its own. If the void is then closed properly, the problem is over permanently, and nothing needed removing.

There are real conflicts that do need intervention — poultry losses, a den under an occupied building, a business that cannot have kits in the car park, an animal behaving abnormally — and this page covers those too. But the default response to a spring den is patience followed by exclusion, and any operator who leads with trapping is not giving you the full picture.

## What we have here

Two species, both native and both common.

**Red fox** (*Vulpes vulpes*) is the one people see. Rusty red above, white below, with black stockings on the legs, very large pointed ears, and a long bushy tail with a **white tip** — that white tip is the single most reliable field mark. Adults weigh only about ten to fifteen pounds; most of the apparent size is coat and tail. Red foxes do well in mixed country: farmland, field edges, woodlots, and the margins of villages.

**Grey fox** (*Urocyon cinereoargenteus*) is less often seen. Grizzled grey with rusty flanks, a **black-tipped** tail, and — unusually among canids — the ability to climb trees, using them for escape and occasionally for denning in hollows. Greys prefer heavier woodland and brushier ground.

Both are largely crepuscular, most active at dawn and dusk, and both hunt in daylight without anything being wrong. Both are opportunistic omnivores: mice, voles, rabbits, birds, insects, carrion, fruit, and whatever else presents itself.

## Fox, coyote, or dog?

Worth settling, because the animals need different responses.

**Red fox**: small, ten to fifteen pounds, slender, big pointed ears, black legs, bushy **white-tipped** tail, rusty coat. Runs with the tail out level.

**Coyote**: two to three times heavier, greyer or more tawny, proportionally larger muzzle and head, **black-tipped** tail carried low when running. Much more substantial in the body. See [coyotes](/pest-library/coyotes/).

**Grey fox**: fox-sized but grizzled grey, black-tipped tail, climbs trees.

**Domestic dog**: variable, but generally a heavier build, shorter muzzle, tail carried high, and moves differently — dogs bound and range about, foxes trot with a purposeful, low, direct gait.

## The den, and its timetable

Naturally, foxes den in bank burrows, enlarged woodchuck holes, rock crevices, and hollow logs. Around buildings, the void under a **shed, deck, porch, barn floor, mobile home, or old foundation** is warmer, drier, and safer from predators than anything they could dig.

An active den is easy to identify:

- An entrance around **eight inches** across, often with a second bolt hole nearby
- A **fan of excavated soil** spread outside the entrance
- A scatter of **feathers, bones, fur, and food remains** in the surrounding area
- A distinctive **strong musky odour** most people notice before they see the hole
- **Well-worn paths** radiating out from the entrance
- From about April, **kits playing outside** in the early morning and evening

The timetable is the useful part:

**Mid to late winter** — breeding.
**March to April** — kits born, typically four to six, and they stay underground for the first month.
**April to May** — kits emerge and start playing at the entrance. This is when almost everybody discovers they have foxes.
**Six to eight weeks** — kits mobile, beginning to travel with the adults.
**Late spring into early summer** — the family abandons the natal den.
**Autumn** — young disperse to find their own territories.

An important behavioural detail: a vixen almost always maintains **several alternate dens** and will move a litter if a site stops feeling safe. That is what makes mild harassment an effective and humane tool when waiting is not an option — you are not evicting a family into nothing, you are prompting a move to a site she already has.

## Where the conflicts happen around here

**Poultry.** The main genuine conflict, and it is common across the rural corridor. Foxes take birds during the day as well as at night, usually **one at a time, carried off**, leaving scattered feathers. This is quite different from a weasel, which kills many and leaves them.

**Rabbits and small caged livestock.** Same exposure.

**Dens under outbuildings.** Sheds, decks, porches, barn floors, mobile homes, and old foundations across every farmstead and village property in the area.

**Rodent-rich properties.** Barns, feed storage, and grain spillage support the mice and voles that bring foxes in. Bird feeders do the same thing at one remove.

**Accessible food.** Pet food left out, unsecured rubbish, fallen fruit, compost with food waste.

**Village edges.** Foxes adapt well to human proximity, so dens under sheds in Clintonville, Marion, and Manawa are as likely as anything out on a farm.

## Signs

- A den entrance about eight inches across with excavated soil fanned outside, under a structure
- Strong musky odour near the entrance
- Kits playing outside at dawn or dusk from April onward
- Feathers, bones, and food debris scattered near the den
- Poultry taken one at a time, often in daylight, with scattered feathers
- Small dog-like tracks in a direct, purposeful line rather than a wandering pattern
- Twisted, tapered droppings containing fur, bone fragments, or seeds, often left on a raised spot
- Screaming or barking calls at night, especially in winter breeding season
- Well-worn trails between the den and hunting ground

## Risk

**To people: very low.** Foxes are small, wary, and avoid confrontation. Attacks on people are extraordinarily rare, and a fox watching you from across the yard is curious, not dangerous.

The exception worth knowing is **abnormal behaviour** — staggering, circling, disorientation, unprovoked aggression, complete absence of fear, or paralysis. Those signs warrant a call and no approach. But simply being **out in daylight is not abnormal**, and this misunderstanding causes a great deal of unnecessary worry every spring. A vixen with a litter to feed hunts whenever she can.

**To pets: low but not zero.** Very small pets, kittens, and small dogs left out unattended at night carry some risk. Most adult cats are close enough in size that encounters are stand-offs rather than predation.

**To poultry: real.** This is the conflict that matters, and it needs a physical solution.

**Disease:** foxes can carry rabies, mange, and parasites as any wild canid can. Mange is the one you are most likely to see, producing a patchy, thin, unwell-looking animal — worth reporting rather than approaching.

And the **benefit** deserves stating: a fox family eats a very large number of rodents. A vixen feeding a litter hunts almost constantly, and mice and voles make up the bulk of what she brings home. On a rural property that is genuine, valuable rodent suppression.

## Why DIY usually fails

**Sealing the den with kits inside.** The most serious mistake, and it happens. The young starve under the structure, which is both inhumane and produces an odour and insect problem you will be dealing with for weeks.

**Removing the adult and leaving the litter.** Same outcome by a different route.

**Trapping without exclusion.** Foxes hold territories. Remove the resident and a new one moves in within a season, into the same convenient void under the same shed.

**Fencing that ignores digging.** Foxes are persistent and capable diggers. A tall fence with an unprotected base stops nothing; the bottom eighteen inches matter more than the top three feet.

**Chicken wire.** Stops neither a determined fox nor anything smaller. Half-inch hardware cloth, screwed and skirted.

**Repellents and deterrent products.** Commercial fox repellents perform poorly against a vixen with a litter established in a good site. Mild sustained harassment works far better and costs nothing.

**Panicking about daytime sightings.** A lot of removal requests trace back to a normal fox doing a normal thing.

## The BugBoss process

**1. Identify the animal and the situation.** Fox, coyote, or dog; den or passing through; kits present or not; what the actual conflict is. That last question matters, because "there is a fox" and "I am losing birds" call for different responses.

**2. Give you the honest recommendation.** For a spring den with no active conflict, that is usually to wait a few weeks and then seal the void — and we will say so rather than sell a removal. It is a better outcome and it costs less.

**3. Where intervention is needed, start with harassment.** Increased activity, light, and sound around the entrance will typically prompt a vixen to move the litter to one of her alternate dens within a few days. Humane, effective, and it avoids separating a family.

**4. Handle the animal where that is genuinely required**, within the regulations — repeated poultry losses, a den under an occupied structure, or an animal behaving abnormally. Foxes are native furbearers and the rules governing trapping and handling matter; we manage that side rather than leaving it to the property owner.

**5. Seal the void permanently.** Hardware cloth, properly fastened, with the base skirted outward or buried. This is the step that makes it a one-time job instead of an annual event, and it is the step most often skipped.

**6. Secure the poultry.** Covered run, hardware cloth rather than chicken wire, base skirted or buried against digging, positive latches, birds shut in at night. Get this right and it also handles weasels, raccoons, and most of what else is out there.

**7. Remove the attractants.** Pet food indoors, rubbish secured, fallen fruit cleared, and rodent control in the barn and feed storage — because the rodents are usually the reason the fox is on your property at all.

**8. Close the other voids.** Where there is one accessible space under a structure there are usually more. Sealing one and leaving three open just relocates the den.

## Prevention

- **Close voids under structures before anything moves in.** Sheds, decks, porches, barn floors, and mobile homes. Hardware cloth, skirted outward eighteen inches or buried. Cheap now, tiresome later.
- **Build the coop properly.** Half-inch hardware cloth, covered run, base skirted or buried, positive latches, birds shut in at night.
- **Bring pet food in** and do not feed pets outdoors.
- **Secure rubbish** and keep compost free of food waste.
- **Clear fallen fruit** in autumn.
- **Control rodents** around barns and feed storage — the fox is following the mice.
- **Reconsider bird feeders** if you have persistent pressure; they feed rodents, and rodents feed foxes.
- **Supervise small pets** after dark on rural properties.
- **Check for early denning activity** in March, before kits are born, when a void can still be closed without stranding anything.
