## What we are dealing with, and how to identify it

Chipmunks are the small, striped ground squirrels everybody recognizes and most people underestimate. Around Clintonville they are a fixture of yards, gardens, woodlots, and rock walls, and in ones and twos they are harmless enough — but the reason they end up on a pest-control call is not the animal, it is the **burrowing**. Chipmunks dig extensive tunnel systems, and they preferentially dig them along foundations, under patios, steps, and porches, and beneath retaining walls — the exact structures that tunneling can undermine. What looks like a charming backyard critter becomes a problem when its burrow network starts washing out the soil that supports your slab or your wall.

The **eastern chipmunk** is the species here: a small, reddish-brown ground squirrel with the distinctive dark and light stripes running down its back and sides, a lighter belly, and a moderately bushy tail it carries upright when it runs. It is smaller than a tree squirrel and lives on and under the ground rather than in trees, though it climbs readily to raid feeders. You will usually see chipmunks in **daylight**, darting along foundations, walls, and woodpiles, and hear their sharp repeated "chip" call.

The clearest identification, though, is the **burrow entrance**, and it has a signature that separates it from other diggers: a clean, round hole about golf-ball to two inches across, **with no mound of excavated soil** around it. Chipmunks carry the dirt away in their cheek pouches and scatter it, keeping the entrance inconspicuous, so you get a neat hole in the ground rather than a spoil pile. That is the tell that distinguishes a chipmunk burrow from a [rat](/pest-library/rats/) burrow (larger, with a fan of soil), a mole (raised ridges and cone-shaped mounds), or other diggers. Reading the burrow correctly is the first step, because it points to the right fix.

## Wisconsin biology and lifecycle

Chipmunks are ground squirrels adapted to the woodland edges and yards of the region, and their whole life revolves around the burrow and the food cache. A chipmunk digs an extensive system of tunnels and chambers — often surprisingly large for such a small animal, running many feet with multiple entrances, nesting chambers, and storage chambers for cached food. That burrow is where it sleeps, raises young, stores winter provisions, and shelters from predators, and it is why chipmunks favor the protected edges of foundations, walls, and hardscapes: the structure shields the burrow and hides the entrances.

They breed once or twice a year — typically a spring litter and sometimes a second in mid to late summer — with several young per litter. The young grow quickly and disperse within a few months to dig their own burrows, so a local population expands as juveniles set up nearby. This is slower reproduction than [mice](/pest-library/mice/) or [voles](/pest-library/voles/), but on a property that offers abundant food and good cover, and after a mild winter with high survival, the numbers build noticeably over a couple of seasons.

Chipmunks are driven by **food storage**. They are omnivores that eat seeds, nuts, berries, fruit, bulbs, insects, and more, but their defining behavior is caching — carrying food in their cheek pouches back to the burrow to stockpile for winter. In late summer and fall this drive goes into overdrive, which is when chipmunks are most visible and most destructive, raiding gardens, digging up bulbs and plantings, and working bird feeders relentlessly. Unlike true hibernators, chipmunks do not put on heavy fat and sleep straight through; instead they go **relatively dormant in the burrow over winter**, waking periodically to feed on their stored cache. That is why activity drops sharply once cold sets in and resumes in spring — and why the fall caching season is the peak of the problem.

The scale of the burrow system is what makes chipmunks a structural concern rather than a mere nuisance, so it is worth picturing what is actually under the ground. A single chipmunk's burrow is not a simple hole; it is a network that can run many feet in total length, a foot or more deep, with a main nesting chamber, one or more storage chambers packed with cached seeds and nuts, side tunnels, and several entrances — including "working" entrances the animal keeps hidden. When that network is dug into the fill beside a foundation or behind a retaining wall, it displaces and loosens the compacted soil those structures rely on, and it creates channels that let water move through and wash fines out. Now multiply that by the several chipmunks a well-fed, well-covered yard supports, all tunneling into the same run of foundation or the same wall over successive seasons, and the cumulative excavation is far larger than the tidy little entrance holes suggest. That gap between how minor the surface sign looks and how extensive the underground network is, is exactly why people underestimate chipmunks until a slab settles or a wall starts to lean.

## The chipmunk season in Northeast Wisconsin

Chipmunk pressure is a warm-season affair here, bracketed by winter dormancy, and it has two peaks.

Through **spring**, chipmunks emerge from winter dormancy hungry and active, and the first burst of the year begins — feeding, re-establishing and extending burrows, and breeding. This is when new burrow entrances appear along foundations and walls and when the spring litter is on the way. It is a good time to catch burrowing early, before the summer and fall population builds on it.

Through **summer**, chipmunks are busy raising young, feeding, and extending their burrow systems, and the juveniles from the spring litter disperse to dig their own. Garden and bulb damage runs through this season, and the burrow networks along foundations and hardscapes grow. The population is building toward its yearly peak.

**Fall** is the peak of chipmunk activity and damage. The caching drive kicks in hard as chipmunks race to stockpile seeds and nuts for winter, so they are everywhere — raiding feeders, digging up and hauling off bulbs and seed, and expanding burrows and storage chambers. This is when the burrowing along foundations, patios, and retaining walls is most active and when homeowners most often notice a real chipmunk problem. It is the prime window to address both the population and the burrow access before they settle in for winter.

Through **winter**, chipmunks retreat to their burrows and go relatively dormant, living off the cache and waking only periodically. Surface activity nearly stops, and the problem seems to disappear — but the burrow systems along your foundations and walls are still there, occupied and ready to reactivate in spring. Addressing the burrows and the food and cover before or after the dormant season is what keeps the cycle from resuming.

## Where chipmunks come from: the setting around Clintonville

Chipmunks are animals of the woodland edge, and the yards, woodlots, and landscaped properties around Clintonville give them exactly the mix of food, cover, and burrow-friendly structure they thrive on. The properties with the worst chipmunk problems are the ones that combine abundant food with good cover right next to the foundations and hardscapes chipmunks like to dig into.

**Foundations, patios, steps, and porches** are the primary burrow sites. Chipmunks dig along the base of these structures because the structure protects and conceals the burrow, and because the compacted fill and soil there are easy to tunnel through — which is precisely what makes the burrowing a structural concern. **Retaining walls and rock walls** are chipmunk favorites: the voids and the loose fill behind them are ideal burrow habitat, and the tunneling can wash out the backfill and destabilize the wall over time.

**Bird feeders** are a major driver. Spilled and scattered seed under a feeder is a concentrated, reliable food source that supports a large local chipmunk population and anchors them right where the feeder is — often against the house. **Dense landscaping, woodpiles, rock features, and ground cover** give chipmunks the cover they need to move and burrow safely, and yards with lots of these near the foundation carry more chipmunks. **Gardens and bulb beds** offer food to dig up and cache. And **woodlots and wooded edges** feed chipmunks in from the surrounding habitat, so a property backing onto woods gets steady pressure. Cutting the food and cover next to the vulnerable structures is the foundation of controlling them.

## Signs of an infestation

The signs of a chipmunk problem are mostly outdoors and mostly about the burrows. **Burrow entrances** — clean, round holes about golf-ball to two inches across, with no soil mound — are the defining sign, typically clustered along foundations, patio and step edges, retaining walls, and beside woodpiles and landscape features. **Chipmunks running the routes in daylight** along foundations, walls, and woodpiles, and their sharp repeated chip call, confirm an active population.

You will often see **damage to gardens and plantings** — dug-up bulbs and seed, uprooted seedlings, raided fruit and vegetables — and heavy activity around **bird feeders**, with seed hauled off and cached. **Small holes and digging** in flower beds, containers, and mulch, and worn paths along foundations and walls, mark their travel and foraging. Over time, **structural signs** may appear where burrowing has been extensive: settling or cracking near a patio or slab edge, a retaining wall beginning to tilt or bulge, soil washing out along a foundation, or steps and pavers shifting. Occasionally the sign is a chipmunk that has gotten **into the basement, crawlspace, or garage** through a low gap. Because chipmunks are daytime-active and vocal, they are usually easy to confirm once you connect the burrows to the animals.

## Damage and risk

The chipmunk problem is chiefly **structural and horticultural**, not a health-in-the-home issue. The structural risk comes from the burrowing. Chipmunks dig extensive tunnel systems, and when those run under and along foundations, slabs, patios, steps, porches, and retaining walls, the tunneling removes and channels the soil and fill that support those structures. Over time — this is a cumulative problem, not an overnight one — that can lead to **settling, cracking, shifting slabs and steps, and tilting or failing retaining walls**, and burrows against a foundation can also channel water where it does not belong, compounding the damage. A single chipmunk rarely undermines a wall, but a colony working the same foundation or hardscape season after season can, which is why active burrows against a structure are worth addressing before the network grows.

The horticultural damage is real too: chipmunks dig up and eat bulbs, seeds, and newly planted seed, raid gardens and fruit, uproot plantings, and are relentless at bird feeders. A chipmunk-heavy yard is hard on gardens, bulb beds, and landscaping.

It is worth being honest about the timeline, because it shapes how seriously to take a given situation. A single chipmunk with one burrow beside a walkway is not going to undermine your house, and it would be an overreaction to treat it as if it would. The concern is the **cumulative** case — several chipmunks working the same foundation, patio edge, or retaining wall over multiple seasons, in a yard that feeds and shelters a growing population. That is when the excavation reaches the scale that causes settling and structural movement, and because it builds gradually and out of sight, the practical signal to act is not a lone chipmunk but a **cluster of active burrows against a structure that matters** — a retaining wall holding back a slope, a patio or steps, the foundation of the house or an addition. Catching that pattern while it is a handful of burrows, rather than after a wall has shifted, is the difference between a straightforward exclusion job and a repair bill.

On the **health** side, chipmunks are a lower concern than the building-invading [mice](/pest-library/mice/) and [deer mice](/pest-library/deer-mice/) because they stay outdoors, away from food storage and living space. Like other rodents they can carry parasites — including ticks and fleas — and pathogens, and a chipmunk that gets indoors brings that in with it, but indoor contamination is not the primary reason to control them. The burrowing and the garden damage are.

## Why DIY usually fails

The common homeowner approach — a live trap or two, or scattering some repellent — knocks back the chipmunks that are present but rarely solves the problem, because it ignores the two things that actually drive a chipmunk population: **food and cover**. As long as the bird feeder is dropping seed, the garden and bulbs are available, and the dense landscaping, woodpiles, and rock walls give chipmunks safe places to burrow, the surrounding population simply refills the vacancy. On a well-fed, well-covered property there is always another chipmunk to take an open burrow, so trapping a few becomes an endless exercise.

The second gap is **the burrows themselves**. Trapping the animals does nothing about the tunnel network already dug along the foundation, patio, or retaining wall — the structural risk is the tunnels, and those stay open and available for the next occupant. Backfilling and screening active burrows and hardening the vulnerable structure edges is the part that actually protects the foundation and wall, and it is the part DIY usually skips. Buried hardware cloth along a patio or wall edge, done right, is skilled work; a shovelful of dirt in the hole is not.

The third issue is that repellents and home remedies — the mothballs, the sprays, the ultrasonic gadgets — are largely ineffective against a food-motivated animal with an established burrow next to a reliable food source. People cycle through these and conclude chipmunks are impossible. They are not; the effort just has to target the food, the cover, and the burrow access together, not the animal alone.

## Prevention and exclusion

Chipmunk prevention comes down to removing the food, cutting the cover, and hardening the structures they burrow into. On **food**, the biggest single factor is usually the **bird feeder**: spilled and scattered seed under a feeder feeds and anchors a large chipmunk population right against the house, so moving feeders well away from the foundation, using catch trays, and cleaning up spillage removes a major draw. Protect **bulbs and garden plantings** with buried hardware cloth where chipmunk digging is a problem, and do not leave pet food or other accessible food outdoors.

On **cover**, keep the areas next to foundations, patios, and retaining walls open and low: reduce dense landscaping, ground cover, and mulch right against the structures, move woodpiles and debris away from the walls, and keep a clear zone at the base of the structures chipmunks like to dig under. Cover is what lets chipmunks burrow safely; take it away next to the vulnerable structures and the burrows become far more exposed and less attractive.

On the **structures**, harden the edges chipmunks exploit. Seal and screen the gaps along the base of patios, steps, porches, and retaining walls — buried hardware cloth run down along the edge is the standard fix — and backfill and screen active burrows so they cannot simply be reopened. Seal the low, ground-level gaps into basements, crawlspaces, and garages — foundation cracks and utility penetrations — the same way you would to keep mice out. On a wooded lot with steady pressure from the surrounding habitat the realiztic goal is protected foundations and hardscapes plus a yard that does not feed and shelter a colony, not a chipmunk-free property.
