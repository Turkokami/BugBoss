## What you're dealing with

The opossum is the odd one out among Northeast Wisconsin's nuisance wildlife, and knowing what it actually is takes a lot of the alarm out of a sighting while keeping the real concerns in focus. The **Virginia opossum** (*Didelphis virginiana*) is North America's only marsupial — the females carry their young in a pouch — a cat-sized, grayish-white animal with a long, pointed pink nose, thin naked ears, a naked prehensile tail, and a mouthful of fifty teeth it bares in a dramatic hiss when cornered. That display, and the famous "playing dead," are the whole of its defense. Opossums are not aggressive, they're slow and nearsighted, they rarely stand and fight, and they're a genuinely low rabies risk. What they are is opportunistic: a wandering scavenger that will move into any ready-made den near an easy food source and stay as long as both hold out.

Around Clintonville, opossums are near the **cold northern edge of their range**, and that matters more than it sounds. They don't hibernate and they don't handle hard winters well — their naked ears and tail are prone to frostbite — so in our climate a sheltered, slightly-warm den is a survival requirement, not a luxury. That's exactly why they crowd in under decks, porches, sheds, and outbuildings, and into barns and crawl spaces, especially as the cold sets in. An opossum under your deck isn't there to menace anyone. It's there because your structure is warm and something nearby is feeding it.

Because BugBoss does **both pest control and wildlife removal**, we handle opossums as a complete job — the animal, the den, the food draw, and the parasite-and-droppings cleanup — rather than trapping the animal and leaving an open, scented crawl space and a coop still standing wide open. With opossums the follow-through is the whole point, because the animal itself is easy to remove; keeping the *next* one from taking the same den is the part that takes doing.

## Wisconsin biology and behavior

Opossums live fast, wander widely, and improvise, and their habits around homes follow directly from that.

They are **nocturnal omnivores and scavengers** with a famously indiscriminate diet: insects, slugs, worms, carrion, small animals, eggs, fruit, and — around people — garbage, pet food, compost, birdseed, and whatever a chicken coop offers. They don't establish a fixed territory the way many mammals do; a young opossum in particular drifts from den to den, using a cavity for a few nights and moving on, which is why opossum problems can seem to come and go. But a den with reliable food beside it will hold an opossum for the season.

Crucially, opossums **rarely dig their own dens**. They take over an existing cavity — very often an **abandoned skunk or woodchuck burrow** under a deck, shed, or slab, or a hollow, a brush pile, a woodpile, or a gap into a barn or crawl space. This is a direct link to the region's other ground-denning wildlife: clear a [groundhog](/pest-library/groundhogs/) from under your shed and leave the burrow open, and an opossum (or a [skunk](/pest-library/skunks/)) may move straight into the vacancy. It's one more reason closing a burrow matters as much as removing the animal that dug it.

Reproduction is pure marsupial. After a very short gestation the tiny, undeveloped young crawl into the mother's **pouch**, where they nurse and develop for weeks before emerging to **ride on her back** as she forages. A female in Wisconsin may raise young from spring into late summer, sometimes two litters in a good year. The practical upshot is the same as with the region's other spring-and-summer denning wildlife: a female removed carelessly can leave dependent young behind, so the timing and the handling matter.

Opossums **do not hibernate** and stay active all winter, which is a hard life this far north. In the coldest stretches they hole up in the most sheltered den they can find and live off fat reserves, emerging to forage on milder nights, and they suffer frostbite damage to ears and tail in severe cold. That drive for winter shelter is what pushes them under heated structures and into barns and crawl spaces from late fall onward. Their lifespan is short — often just two to three years in the wild — and their populations turn over fast, so the individual under your deck this winter is unlikely to be the same one next winter, but the *den* will keep drawing new arrivals until it's closed.

## The season in Northeast Wisconsin

**Spring** brings breeding and the first pouch-young, and dispersing yearlings looking for dens and food after winter. A female denning under a deck in spring may be carrying young, which changes how removal has to be timed.

**Summer** is peak foraging and the season when mothers travel with young riding on their backs. Gardens, compost, fallen fruit, pet food, garbage, and coops all pull opossums in close, and a property with easy food will host them steadily.

**Fall** is when the search for winter shelter intensifies. As nights cool, opossums start settling into the dens they'll try to overwinter in — under decks, sheds, porches, and into barns and crawl spaces — so this is a common time for one to move in under a structure.

**Winter** is the hard season and the one that drives opossums hardest toward your buildings. Unable to hibernate and vulnerable to frostbite, an opossum will hole up in the warmest sheltered den it can find and ride out the cold, emerging on mild nights. A crawl space, a barn, or the void under a heated part of the house is prime, which is why winter den complaints are common here despite the cold.

Around Clintonville and the surrounding rural towns, the vulnerability is the same mix that draws skunks and woodchucks: abundant ground-level cavities under decks, porches, sheds, and outbuildings; abandoned burrows waiting to be adopted; and easy food from gardens, compost, garbage, feed, and coops. The cold-edge climate simply sharpens the animal's need for shelter, concentrating it around structures.

## Local den sites

Opossums den wherever there's a ready cavity and food nearby, and on properties here the same spots recur. The **crawl space under a deck, porch, or stoop** is the classic, especially when a skunk or woodchuck dug the entrance first. **Sheds and outbuildings on skids or blocks**, and on farms the **barn, pole barn, granary, corn crib, and machine shed**, all offer sheltered dens — and opossums readily den *inside* barns and outbuildings, up in stored hay or equipment, not just underneath. **Abandoned skunk and woodchuck burrows** under slabs, stoops, and structures get adopted wholesale. **Woodpiles, brush piles, and junk piles** in the yard or at field edges make natural dens. And **chicken coops** are both a den option and a food source in one, which is why coops draw opossums so reliably on rural properties.

The tell is usually an **existing hole** under a structure that an opossum has taken over — you may not see fresh digging, because it likely didn't dig it — along with droppings and a musky (not skunk-strong) odor near the entrance.

## Signs of an opossum

Opossums leave a scattered set of signs. **Droppings** are one of the clearer ones — large, dog-like, often found near feeding spots — and their presence in a barn or feed room is a specific concern on horse properties (see below). Look for **raided garbage and pet-food dishes, disturbed compost, tipped or emptied bird feeders, and coop damage** — missing or broken eggs, killed birds, cleaned-up spilled feed. A **den entrance under a deck, shed, porch, or slab**, frequently an adopted burrow rather than fresh digging, marks where it's sheltering.

**Tracks** are distinctive: the hind foot shows an opposable, thumb-like toe set off at an angle, unlike any rodent or canine print. And you may simply **see the animal** at night — cat-sized, grizzled grayish-white, with a pointed pink nose, thin naked ears, and a long, scaly, naked tail, moving slowly and deliberately. If cornered it'll hiss with its mouth wide open showing all those teeth, or it may keel over and "play dead" — both are defensive bluffs, not signs of aggression or, in the case of playing dead, of a sick animal. On rural properties, a persistent [flea](/pest-library/fleas/) problem that keeps resurging despite treatment can itself be a sign of a wildlife den under a structure.

## Health and property risk

Opossums carry a **lighter health load than raccoons or skunks**, and it's worth being accurate about the parts that matter.

**Rabies is a low risk.** An opossum's body temperature runs lower than most mammals', which makes it a poor host for the rabies virus, so rabies in opossums is genuinely uncommon — not impossible, so an animal that's staggering, disoriented, or unnaturally tame still deserves distance and a call, but you should not assume a rabies threat from an opossum the way you would from a skunk or raccoon.

**Fleas and ticks are the routine concern.** Opossums carry fleas, ticks, and mites, and they transport them right into your yard and under your deck. There's a popular belief that opossums are a tick-control ally because they groom off and eat the ticks that latch onto them — and they do eat some — but recent field research has walked back the dramatic numbers from an early lab study; wild opossums eat far fewer ticks than that figure suggested, and examined opossum stomachs rarely contain ticks at all. The honest bottom line is that an opossum denning by your house is a **net source** of fleas and ticks, not a solution to them, and in a region with the [tick and Lyme pressure](/guides/tick-lyme-yard-program/) this one carries, that's not trivial.

**Droppings carry bacteria.** Opossum feces can harbor **leptospirosis** (spread through contaminated soil and water) and **salmonella**, so den debris and droppings are handled with gloves and sense, not swept up bare-handed.

**Horses face a specific risk.** Opossums are the definitive host of *Sarcocystis neurona*, the parasite that causes **equine protozoal myeloencephalitis (EPM)** — a serious neurological disease horses can develop after eating feed or drinking water contaminated with opossum droppings. On horse properties, keeping opossums out of the barn, feed room, and hay storage, and cleaning up their droppings, is a real herd-health measure, not just tidiness. This is a big reason opossum work on rural and equestrian properties emphasizes securing feed and outbuildings.

**Property damage** is modest compared with a woodchuck or raccoon. Opossums don't excavate their own burrows and don't tear up structures the way a raccoon does; the harm is mostly the **coop raiding, garbage and garden mess, droppings, and the parasite load**, plus occasional damage getting into a barn, crawl space, or feed store. The indirect problem is the same as with the other ground-denners: an opossum keeping a crawl-space den open and scented helps keep that den attractive to whatever comes next.

## Why DIY usually fails

Homeowners try the familiar remedies on opossums, and they fail for the same structural reasons they fail on skunks and woodchucks.

**Repellents and harassment** — mothballs, ammonia rags, predator-urine granules, lights, radios — are unreliable and short-lived. They might annoy a drifting opossum into leaving for a night, but they do nothing about the open den or the food that drew it, so the animal or the next wanderer returns. Mothballs scattered under a deck are also a pesticide being misused, and they contaminate soil for no lasting benefit.

**Sealing the void without removing the animal** traps whatever's under there — and in spring or summer, a female with pouch or back young, which then die in an inaccessible space and become an odor-and-cleanup problem. The den has to be confirmed empty, and young accounted for, before it's closed.

**Trapping alone** ignores the two things that actually solve it: closing the ready-made den and cutting the food. Trap the opossum and leave the burrow open and the coop and garbage accessible, and you've cleared one drifter while leaving a furnished, stocked den for the next — often within weeks, since opossums move den to den. And there are Wisconsin handling and relocation rules to follow even for a lightly protected animal; hauling one off to dump elsewhere isn't the clean fix people imagine.

**Leaving the coop and feed open** is the rural version of the mistake. As long as there's an easy meal — eggs, chicks, spilled feed, pet food, compost, fallen fruit — a property will keep drawing opossums no matter how many you remove.

Finally, the **cleanup** — flea-and-tick-laden bedding, droppings carrying leptospirosis and salmonella, and, in a barn, the EPM concern for horses — is work to do with protection and know-how, not a broom and a bag.

## Prevention and exclusion

Keeping opossums out is the same two-front job as the region's other ground-denners: close the cavities and cut the food.

**Structures and dens.** Skirt every deck, porch, shed, and slab with a **buried, dig-proof barrier**, and close the gaps into barns, crawl spaces, and outbuildings. Backfill and block abandoned skunk and woodchuck **burrows** so an opossum can't adopt one. Keep **woodpiles, brush piles, and junk piles** up off the ground and away from the house, since they make natural dens.

**Food.** Keep **garbage in secured, latching cans**, bring **pet food indoors** at night, manage **compost** so it isn't an open buffet, and clean up **spilled birdseed and fallen fruit**. On rural properties, keep **feed in metal containers**, keep the **feed room and hay storage** closed up, and — the big one — **secure the chicken coop** tightly at ground level and overhead, because a coop is both a food source and a den in one.

None of this evicts an opossum already denning under a structure — exclusion and food control are post-removal and preventive measures, not a way to get a resident animal out. And the same buried-barrier work that opossum-proofs a deck also shuts out [skunks](/pest-problems/skunk-under-deck/) and [groundhogs](/pest-library/groundhogs/), so the effort closes the whole ground-level wildlife problem at once. If [bats](/pest-library/bats/) are part of the same project, they're protected in Wisconsin and can't be excluded during the summer maternity season, so we sequence the work so nothing gets sealed at the wrong time.
