## What cluster flies are, and how to know you have them

If you live anywhere in the Clintonville area and you find yourself vacuuming slow, dark flies off your upstairs windows on a sunny day in January, you have cluster flies. They are the single most common overwintering insect we deal with across rural Waupaca, Shawano, and Outagamie counties, and they are also the most misunderstood. People assume flies in the house mean something rotting, something unsanitary, a failure of housekeeping. Cluster flies are none of that. They are a soil insect that happens to use your walls as a winter hotel.

The fly itself is *Pollenia*, most often *Pollenia rudis*. It is larger than a common house fly, noticeably darker, and much slower — a cluster fly bumbles where a house fly darts. The giveaway is a patch of short, crinkly golden or yellowish hairs on the thorax, the section just behind the head, which catches the light and looks almost dusted with gold. At rest the wings overlap one another rather than sitting spread apart. When you crush one, and you will crush a few by accident, it leaves a faint sweetish smell rather than the sharp reek of a filth fly. And true to the name, they cluster — you rarely find one. You find a dozen on a windowsill, a knot of them in a light fixture, a slow-moving mass on the warm siding outside.

That clustering behavior, combined with the timing, is what tells you what you are looking at. A few fast flies in July around the kitchen are house flies or fruit flies. A slow crowd of big dark flies at the windows in October, or on the first thaw in February, is almost always cluster flies. If you are unsure, that is exactly the kind of identification the [team at BugBoss](/residential-pest-control/) sorts out before recommending anything — because the fix for cluster flies is completely different from the fix for a fly problem tied to a dead animal in a wall.

## Wisconsin biology and lifecycle: the earthworm connection

Here is the fact that changes how you think about cluster flies. Their larvae are parasites of earthworms. The adult female lays her eggs in cracks in the soil. When the tiny larvae hatch, they seek out an earthworm, burrow in, and develop inside the living worm, eventually killing it. They pupate in the soil and emerge as adults. In our climate that cycle runs through several generations over the course of the warm season, from spring into early fall.

Sit with what that means. The cluster flies that will invade your walls this fall were raised in the earthworms living in the soil around your house right now. Every acre of lawn, pasture, hayfield, old orchard, and woodlot in Northeast Wisconsin is essentially a cluster-fly nursery, because all of that ground is rich in earthworms. This is precisely why the pest is a rural and small-town problem and not a downtown-apartment problem. A farmhouse ringed by hayfields sits at the center of an enormous worm population, so it produces an enormous fly population, no matter how immaculate the kitchen is. There is nothing you can do to your house that changes the earthworm density in the field next door.

It also explains why the problem is so stubborn and so predictable. You cannot bait cluster flies the way you bait ants, because they are not coming indoors to feed — they never eat anything in your house. You cannot eliminate the breeding source, because the breeding source is the soil for hundreds of yards in every direction and it belongs to the worms. What you can do is intercept the adults in the narrow window when they leave the soil and try to get into the building. Everything effective about cluster fly control hinges on that timing.

## The cluster fly season in Northeast Wisconsin

The calendar for cluster flies in our region is tight and reliable, and understanding it is most of the battle.

Through spring and summer, the flies are outdoors and largely invisible to homeowners, quietly cycling through the soil and the earthworm population across several generations. You are not thinking about them, and you have no reason to.

Then, as the nights start to cool in late August and through September, the final generation of adults begins looking for a protected place to spend the winter. On warm, sunny afternoons they gather in growing numbers on the sun-warmed south and west walls of buildings, drawn to the heat the siding radiates as the air cools. This is the single most important period, and most people miss it because the flies are still outside and not yet a visible indoor nuisance. Late August into September is the window when an exterior treatment does virtually all of its work.

By October the flies are actively working into the structure — through soffit and fascia gaps, around window and door frames, at the roofline, through gable and ridge vents, and any crack the warm wall offers. They filter into wall voids, attics, and unused upper rooms and settle into dormancy.

Through the depths of winter they stay hidden and quiet. Then comes the part homeowners actually notice: any warm, sunny day, even in January or February, heats the wall voids and the attic, rouses the dormant flies, and they crawl toward the light. They end up on the inside of your windows, in light fixtures, in the corners of upstairs rooms. People assume they are breeding in the house or coming in fresh. They are not. It is the same population that entered in September, waking up on a warm day. When spring truly arrives, the survivors try to work their way back outside to start the cycle over in the soil.

## Where cluster flies hit hardest around Clintonville

Not every building gets cluster flies equally, and knowing which ones do explains a lot about the local pattern. The worst cases are the homes and cabins that sit surrounded by the most worm-rich soil with the warmest walls and the most gaps — and Northeast Wisconsin is full of them.

**Older farmhouses** are the classic heavy case. They sit in the middle of open fields and pasture, so they draw from an enormous surrounding earthworm population. Their two-story south and west walls collect the whole afternoon's sun, giving the flies a big, warm gathering surface. And their construction — decades of settlement, unsealed soffit and fascia gaps, and especially balloon framing that leaves the wall cavities open from sill plate all the way to the attic — gives the flies both easy entry and vast, undisturbed voids to overwinter in. A fly that enters a balloon-framed wall at a soffit gap can end up dormant two floors down, which is why people find them in first-floor windows they cannot explain. These homes reward the thorough roofline exclusion we do more than any other type.

**Seasonal cabins** around Iola, the Waupaca Chain O' Lakes, and the Wolf and Little Wolf River corridors are the other prime target, for a specific reason: they are quiet and empty exactly when the flies want a still, undisturbed overwintering site. Nobody is home in October to notice the flies gathering, nobody vacuums them off the sills all winter, and the population builds unchecked until the owner opens up in spring to find windowsills and light fixtures full of them. Timing treatment to the cabin's closing schedule, so the place is protected while it sits shut, is the whole game — it is a core part of our [cabin seasonal pest control](/guides/cabin-seasonal-pest-control/) approach.

**Village homes** in Clintonville proper get them too, though often in smaller numbers, since they draw from smaller yards and shared lot lines rather than open acreage. Tighter mid-century construction gives the flies fewer voids to exploit, but soffit gaps, dryer and utility penetrations, and old window frames still let them in. And **light-colored or masonry walls** anywhere that bake in the afternoon sun act as magnets regardless of the home's age, because it is the warmth of the wall, not the address, that the flies are answering.

Cluster flies also turn up in **commercial and agricultural buildings** — offices, warehouses, and the pole barns and outbuildings that dot every rural property — for the same reasons. Any large, warm, south-facing wall over worm-rich ground is a candidate, which is why we address them on [commercial properties](/commercial-pest-control/) as well as homes.

## How do you know you have cluster flies?

The signs are straightforward once you know the pattern. In fall, look for masses of large, sluggish flies on the sunny exterior walls, especially in the afternoon. Indoors, the classic sign is groups of dead and dying flies on windowsills, particularly upstairs, and in light fixtures and ceiling corners. Live flies appearing at the windows on warm winter and early-spring days are the clearest indicator of an established overwintering population in the structure. In heavy cases, a large mass of flies dormant in a wall void or attic can give off a faint, sweetish, slightly greasy odor. And where dead flies accumulate over years, you may find carpet beetles and other scavengers that have moved in to feed on the carcasses — a secondary problem the flies quietly created.

## Are cluster flies dangerous?

The honest answer is that cluster flies are almost entirely a nuisance pest, and it is worth saying so plainly rather than overselling the threat. They do not bite. They do not sting. They do not transmit disease to people, because unlike house flies and blow flies they never contact garbage, manure, or carrion — they develop inside earthworms in clean soil. They do not lay eggs in your food or breed inside the house. They do not chew wood, wiring, or insulation, so there is no structural damage.

What they cost you is comfort and a bit of cleanup. Large overwintering populations mean repeated vacuuming through the winter, stained sills and walls where flies die and their bodies break down, and the odor a big dormant mass can produce. The one genuine secondary concern is that accumulations of dead flies attract scavenging insects like carpet beetles, which can then move on to fabrics and stored goods. So while cluster flies themselves are harmless, letting a heavy population build in the walls year after year is not consequence-free. It is a manageable nuisance that becomes a persistent one if ignored.

## Why doesn't DIY work on cluster flies?

Almost every homeowner starts the same way: they wait until the flies show up at the windows, then they buy a can of flying-insect spray and go after the ones they can see. It never solves the problem, and the reason is timing and location. By the time flies are inside on the windowsill, they are already deep in the wall voids and attic — the ones in view are a tiny fraction of the population, and killing them changes nothing about the hundreds still hidden. Spraying the interior also puts pesticide where you live, breathe, and sleep, to no real benefit. Vacuuming is cleaner and just as effective for the visible flies.

The other common mistake is treating too late. People call in October or November wanting the flies gone, but by then the insects have moved inside and the moment to intercept them on the exterior walls has passed. The effective work happens in late August and September, on the outside of the building, before the flies enter — and most homeowners simply are not thinking about flies while it is still warm out.

Exclusion is the other half, and it is genuinely hard to do well on the kinds of houses we have here. The soffit and fascia gaps, the roofline junctions, the balloon-framed walls of older farmhouses that run open from sill to attic — these are the entry points, and finding and sealing them takes knowing where to look and being willing to work at the roofline. A homeowner caulking around the obvious windows misses the routes that matter most. This is the same envelope-sealing skill that underlies our [farmhouse rodent exclusion](/guides/farmhouse-rodent-exclusion/) work, and it is not a weekend job on a two-story farmhouse.

## How do you keep cluster flies out?

The prevention story for cluster flies is the exclusion story, because you cannot touch the breeding source. Every opening you close before fall is one the flies cannot use. The high-value targets are the roofline and upper structure: seal and screen gable, ridge, and soffit vents; close the gaps where soffit meets fascia and where the roof meets the walls; and repair any lifted or damaged flashing. Around the walls, caulk gaps around window and door frames, seal where utility lines and pipes penetrate the siding, and repair torn screens. On older homes, pay attention to the places where different materials meet and where decades of settling have opened seams.

Do this work in summer, before the late-August gathering begins, so the building is buttoned up before the flies arrive. Light-colored siding and shaded walls attract fewer flies than dark, sun-baked ones, though that is rarely a practical thing to change. The realistic goal is not zero flies — the land guarantees a population — but keeping that population out of the wall voids and living space, which exclusion plus well-timed exterior treatment reliably does.

It is also worth managing the secondary problem. Where cluster flies have overwintered in a wall or attic for years, dead flies accumulate, and those carcasses feed scavengers like carpet beetles and, occasionally, the larder beetles that then move on to fabrics and stored goods. Cleaning out heavy accumulations of dead flies from attics and voids, as part of the exclusion work, removes that food source and heads off a fabric-pest problem before it starts. On cabins that have gone years without attention, this cleanup is often as valuable as the treatment itself.
