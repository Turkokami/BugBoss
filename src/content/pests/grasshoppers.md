Grasshoppers are the pest where the most useful thing we can tell you is where the limits are.

## This is a field problem that reaches your garden

Wisconsin has several hundred grasshopper species and only a handful do the damage: the **differential**, **redlegged**, **twostriped** and **migratory** grasshoppers account for nearly all of it.

None of them are household insects. They do not breed in buildings, they cannot feed on anything in a house, and an individual indoors in August came through an open door by accident and will be dead within days.

What they are is an **edge** problem. Damage starts on the side of the property facing pasture, hay ground, a ditch bank or a roadside, and works inward.

## Why some years are so much worse

Grasshopper numbers are normally held down by **fungal pathogens**, and those pathogens need humidity to spread.

A hot, dry summer removes the brake. Populations that would have crashed instead climb, and two dry summers in a row produce the outbreak years people remember for a decade. A wet June does more for grasshopper control than anything that can be sprayed.

This is worth internalising because it reframes the problem: an outbreak year is weather, not something you failed to prevent, and it will end when the weather changes.

## Where they actually come from

Females lay **egg pods in undisturbed soil** in late summer — ground that will not be tilled or disturbed before spring:

- Ditch banks and roadside verges
- Fence rows
- Field margins and headlands
- Pasture
- The unmown strip at the back of the lot

Eggs overwinter there and hatch the following spring. On a rural property the single most useful thing to establish is **which margin they are hatching off**, because that determines whether anything can be done and who would have to do it.

## What treatment can and cannot do

Here is the honest division.

**It can** protect a specific planting, knock down a heavy edge, and make a difference if applied to the margin band **early**, while they are still nymphs and before they can fly well.

**It cannot** give you a grasshopper-free lawn in an outbreak year on an exposed property. Adults are strong fliers and reinvade continuously from surrounding land. A treated yard ringed by hay and roadside is being restocked daily, and no product changes that.

If somebody quotes you a yard treatment for an outbreak year without mentioning reinvasion, they are selling you a repeat visit.

## What actually works better

**Treat early, not late.** Nymphs are far more susceptible than adults. An outbreak dealt with in early summer goes much better than the same one in August.

**Treat the margin, not the lawn.** If the hatch ground is yours, an early band treatment along it is the highest-value application available. If it belongs to somebody else, that is the limit and it should be said out loud before any money changes hands.

**Manage the egg-laying ground you own.** Mowing and disturbing those margins reduces next spring's hatch on your land.

**Use row cover on what matters.** For a vegetable bed or a young planting, physical exclusion is more reliable than chemistry and cheaper.

## And if it is actually crickets

Easy to confuse and a completely different job. **[Crickets](/pest-library/crickets/)** have long thread-like antennae, are mostly nocturnal, and genuinely do move into basements, crawlspaces and garages every fall. If what you have is indoors, chirping, and arriving in September, start there instead.
