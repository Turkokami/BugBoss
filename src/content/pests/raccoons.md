## What you're dealing with

A raccoon in the attic or chimney is a different kind of problem from an insect infestation, and it's worth being clear-eyed about why. The **raccoon** (*Procyon lotor*) is intelligent, strong, dexterous, and nocturnal, with hands capable of prying, pulling, and tearing. It carries diseases and parasites that pose a genuine risk to your family, and in Northeast Wisconsin it very often shows up as a **mother raising a litter** inside your home. That combination — a smart, powerful animal, a real health hazard, and, frequently, dependent young — is why raccoon work is a specialized job, not a matter of setting a trap and hoping.

Raccoons are thoroughly at home in the mix of woodlots, farmland, river corridors, and older buildings that define the Clintonville area. The Pigeon River and the region's other waterways give them travel routes and habitat, mature trees give them access to rooflines, and the area's older farmhouses, cabins, and outbuildings give them den sites full of the gaps and weak edges they exploit. A raccoon that dens in your chimney didn't wander in by accident; it chose the safest, warmest cavity in its territory, and to a raccoon your attic is a hollow tree that happens to hold heat.

Because BugBoss does **both pest control and wildlife removal**, we handle raccoons as a complete job — the animal, the entry points, and the contamination — rather than passing you between a trapper, a chimney company, and a cleanup crew. That matters more with raccoons than with almost any other nuisance, because a raccoon problem is really three problems stacked together.

## Wisconsin biology and the denning cycle

Raccoon behavior around homes is driven almost entirely by the breeding calendar, and once you know it, the pattern of calls makes sense.

Raccoons **breed in late winter**, roughly January through March. After a gestation of about nine weeks, **kits are born from about March into May**, usually two to five per litter. The pregnant female's search for a safe, insulated, defensible den site is what drives her into human structures — and an attic or chimney checks every box: warm, dry, high, protected, and hard for predators to reach. This is why spring is raccoon season for us, and why so many spring calls turn out to involve not one animal but a mother and a hidden litter of kits that can't yet climb or walk out on their own.

The kits stay in the den for their first several weeks, nursing and growing, and the mother is fiercely committed to them — she will not abandon them easily, which is exactly why "just wait for it to leave" and home-remedy eviction rarely work in spring. By midsummer the kits are mobile and begin foraging with the mother, and through **summer and fall** the family's activity and range expand as the young mature. Raccoons don't truly hibernate in Wisconsin, but they den up and go largely dormant during the coldest stretches of winter, living off fat reserves and emerging on milder days.

Raccoons are **nocturnal**, opportunistic omnivores — they'll eat almost anything, which is what makes garbage, pet food, birdseed, and gardens such reliable draws. That adaptability is also why removing an animal without sealing the entry solves nothing: the food and shelter that attracted the first raccoon are still there for the next one.

It's worth understanding just how capable these animals are, because it explains why they defeat casual fixes. A raccoon's front paws are nearly as dexterous as hands — they can turn knobs, work latches, pry, and manipulate objects — and an adult is strong enough to tear apart soffit panels, bend aluminum vent covers, lift loose shingles, and rip back fascia. They're also persistent and have excellent spatial memory, returning night after night to a food source or a den they've claimed. A gap that looks far too small to matter is often just where a raccoon starts; it works the edge with those hands until the opening is raccoon-sized. This is why "raccoon-proof" has to mean genuinely reinforced materials, not a piece of screen or a loosely fitted cover that a determined animal peels away in a night.

The **chimney** case deserves its own note because it's so common and so misunderstood. A masonry chimney is, to a raccoon, an ideal hollow tree: vertical, protected, and warm. A female will climb down and settle on the **smoke shelf** just above the damper to raise her litter, which means the animals are sitting directly above your fireplace. This is exactly why you never light a fire to "smoke out" a chimney raccoon — you'll kill the kits, which can't climb out, and leave carcasses on the shelf. A chimney raccoon is a different job from an attic raccoon, and it almost always involves young.

## The season in Northeast Wisconsin

**Late winter and spring** are the peak. February and March bring denning females; March through May brings the kits and the heaviest attic and chimney activity. This is the most important window to get right, because a job done in spring almost always involves young that must be accounted for — done carelessly, you orphan kits that then die and rot inside a wall or flue, turning a wildlife problem into a serious odor-and-decontamination problem.

**Summer** brings the growing family's noise and damage as kits get large and active, plus dispersing young raccoons looking for their own territory and den sites. **Fall** sees animals seeking winter shelter, so attic and outbuilding entries tick up again. **Winter** is quieter, but a raccoon that denned in fall will hunker down in your attic or chimney through the cold — and a chimney raccoon in winter is a real hazard the first time someone tries to light a fire.

Around Clintonville specifically, the older housing stock is the vulnerability. Century-old farmhouses and village homes with aging soffits, unscreened vents, open or poorly capped chimneys, and weather-loosened roof edges give raccoons the pry points they need. Add the barns, sheds, and pole barns on rural properties — prime alternative den sites — and you have an area where raccoon work is a regular part of the calendar, not an oddity.

## Signs of a raccoon

The signs are usually loud and physical. **Heavy nighttime noise** — thumping, dragging, and deliberate movement — in the attic or chimney, starting around dusk, is the classic tell; raccoons are big enough that the sound is unmistakably heavier than a [squirrel's](/pest-library/squirrels/) daytime scampering. Squirrels are light and busy and active in daylight; raccoons are heavy, deliberate, and nocturnal, and the difference in the sound is one of the fastest ways to guess what you're dealing with before anyone goes up to look. In spring you may hear **chittering, crying, or purring sounds from kits**. On the exterior, look for **torn soffits, bent or pried-open vents, and damaged shingles or fascia** at the entry point, often with muddy smudges and hair around the opening. Inside, you'll find flattened and shredded insulation, and a concentrated **latrine** — raccoons return to the same spot to defecate, so droppings pile up in one area. Outdoors, tipped and raided garbage cans, raided pet food, and rolled-back sod (from foraging for grubs) round out the picture.

## Health and structural risk

Raccoons carry the heaviest health load of the common attic wildlife, and it's worth spelling out.

**Raccoon roundworm** (*Baylisascaris procyonis*) is the headline concern. Its eggs are shed in raccoon droppings, and **about half of Wisconsin's raccoons carry the parasite**. People — most often young children, who are likeliest to put contaminated hands or objects in their mouths — become infected by ingesting eggs from soil, droppings, or contaminated surfaces. Once inside a person, the larvae can migrate through the body to the **brain, eyes, and organs**, causing severe and sometimes permanent neurological damage. The eggs aren't infectious the moment they're passed — they take two to four weeks to develop — but once infectious they are **extraordinarily tough**, resisting weather and common cleaners and surviving in the environment for years. That resistance is precisely why a raccoon latrine can't just be scooped out; it requires real decontamination.

Beyond roundworm, raccoons can carry **rabies** and **canine distemper**, and their den debris can harbor other parasites and pathogens. Any raccoon acting sick, disoriented, or unusually bold, or active in broad daylight and staggering, should be treated as a possible rabies case and reported, not approached. A raccoon calmly foraging at dusk is normal; one wandering in circles at noon, showing no fear, or making unusual noises is not, and that's the animal to keep your family and pets well away from.

There's a household angle to the roundworm risk that's easy to underestimate. Because raccoons return to the same **latrine** — often a corner of an attic, a flat spot on a roof, a deck, a woodpile, or the base of a tree — the eggs concentrate in one place over time, and that place is frequently somewhere a curious child or a dog can reach. An attic latrine also sends contamination down into insulation and, through gaps, into the living space's air handling. This is why the cleanup isn't cosmetic: it's removing a durable, concentrated source of an infectious parasite from your home. Standard household cleaners don't reliably kill the eggs, which is part of why this is professional work rather than a mop-and-bucket job.

Structurally, raccoons are among the most destructive animals that get into a house. They tear and compact insulation, **chew and displace electrical wiring** (a documented fire risk), rip into ductwork and vent pipes, and soak insulation, drywall, and framing with latrine waste. A single denning season can produce insulation loss, contamination, and repairs that run well past the cost of prompt removal.

## Why DIY usually fails

Homeowners try a predictable set of do-it-yourself fixes on raccoons, and they fail for consistent reasons.

**Repellents and eviction gimmicks** — ammonia, mothballs, bright lights, blaring radios, commercial "eviction fluid" — are unreliable against a determined animal and especially against a mother defending kits, who has every incentive to stay. Even when they push an adult out temporarily, they do nothing about the open entry, so the next raccoon simply moves in.

**Lighting a fire** to drive a chimney raccoon out is dangerous and often deadly to the animals — it can kill kits that can't escape, leaving you with carcasses lodged in the flue and a decomposition-and-decontamination problem far worse than what you started with.

**Trapping alone** ignores the two things that actually solve the problem: sealing the entry and cleaning up the contamination. Trap out a mother and leave the kits, and they die in the wall. Trap the adult and leave the hole open, and you've bought a few weeks. And **Wisconsin regulates nuisance wildlife** — how animals are trapped, handled, and relocated or dispatched — so a well-meaning homeowner can run into legal problems on top of the practical ones.

Finally, the **contamination cleanup** is genuinely hazardous work given the roundworm risk, requiring proper protective equipment and decontamination, not a shop vac and a garbage bag.

## Prevention and exclusion

Keeping raccoons out comes down to closing the building and removing the draws. **Cap the chimney** with a sturdy, animal-resistant cap — an open chimney is the single most common raccoon den entry. **Screen and reinforce attic, gable, and roof vents** with heavy-gauge material, since standard louvered covers are easily pried. **Repair and secure soffits, fascia, and roof edges**, especially the weather-loosened spots on older homes, and check the roof-return junctions where dormers and additions meet the main roof. **Trim tree branches** that overhang or touch the roof, since they're the raccoon's ladder to the roofline.

Then cut the attractants: keep **garbage in secured, latching cans**, bring **pet food indoors** at night, clean up spilled birdseed and fallen fruit, and don't leave accessible food or water out. On rural properties, screen and secure the gaps in barns, sheds, and pole barns the same way, and keep feed in metal containers. None of this helps once an animal is already denning inside — exclusion is a post-removal and preventive measure, not a way to evict a resident raccoon.

[Squirrels](/pest-library/squirrels/), [bats](/pest-library/bats/), and [skunks](/pest-library/skunks/) exploit many of the same weak points, so the exclusion work that raccoon-proofs your home tends to close out the rest of the attic-and-crawlspace wildlife too. There's an important timing difference to be aware of, though: **bats are legally protected in Wisconsin** and can't be excluded during the summer maternity season, so if bats turn up alongside a raccoon problem, the bat portion has to follow the DNR timeline even while the raccoon work proceeds.
