## What are squirrels, and how do you identify them?

Squirrels are the wildlife call we get most across the wooded country around Clintonville, and they show up in the attic and soffit far more often than most homeowners expect. The tip-off is almost always sound: heavy, confident scampering across the ceiling, a rolling noise as they move stored nuts, and gnawing — all of it in broad daylight, concentrated around dawn and again in the hour before dark. That daytime activity is the fastest way to separate squirrels from the other things that get into attics. Mice are lighter and work at night; [bats](/pest-library/bats/) are nearly silent and swarm at dusk; [raccoons](/pest-library/raccoons/) are heavier, slower, and often nocturnal. When the noise is brisk, busy, and daylit, it is usually a squirrel.

Two species account for nearly all of it here, and telling them apart matters because they behave differently. The **eastern gray squirrel** is the large, familiar one — gray-bodied with a big plume of a tail, at home in village yards, mature oak and hickory woodlots, and any neighborhood with old shade trees. Grays are bold, they cache food widely, and they readily den in attics of homes with tree cover. The **red squirrel**, which a lot of locals just call a pine squirrel, is a different animal: roughly half the size, rusty red-brown along the back, with a white eye ring and a sharp, scolding chatter you will hear before you see it. Reds are tied to conifers and mixed northern woods, they are territorial and pugnacious out of all proportion to their size, and out in the woodlots, farm groves, and cabin country they are the more frequent attic invader. Because a red is smaller, it exploits smaller gaps and is genuinely harder to keep out than a gray.

A third animal, the **flying squirrel**, is present in the region's mature hardwoods and occasionally turns up in attics too. It is small, nocturnal, and social, so if the attic noise is at night and involves several animals gliding and skittering, it can be flyers rather than mice. The identification shapes the plan, so pinning down which squirrel you have is one of the first things we do on an inspection.

## Wisconsin biology and lifecycle: why fall and late winter are the pinch points

The reason squirrel entry clusters into two predictable windows comes straight out of their breeding cycle. In Wisconsin, gray squirrels breed twice a year — once in the depths of winter, roughly December through February, and again in early summer, around June and July. That timing produces two litters: young born in late winter and early spring, and a second batch born in late summer. Each litter is a handful of pups, and the female raises them in a den — naturally a tree cavity, but an attic serves the purpose better than any hollow oak. Red squirrels follow a broadly similar two-season pattern, adjusted for the northern climate.

Layer the two breeding seasons onto the calendar and the attic-invasion windows fall out cleanly. **Late summer into fall**, the second litter disperses and every young squirrel of the year is looking for its own winter den right as the nights turn cold — a surge of animals shopping for shelter. **Late winter**, pregnant females are hunting for a warm, safe, defensible place to have the first litter of the year, and a heated attic is exactly that. Those two pushes are when the phone rings, and they are why "I hear something in the attic" tends to arrive in October and again in February.

Squirrels are built to get into buildings. Like all rodents, their incisors grow continuously and must be worn down by constant gnawing, which means a squirrel does not need a ready-made hole — it can manufacture one. A soft spot in a fascia board, a gap at a soffit return, the corner of a gable vent: a squirrel will enlarge any of these into a usable entry in short order, leaving a rim of fresh, pale wood shavings as the giveaway. They are also superb climbers and jumpers, running up brick and siding and launching from branches, so the whole roofline is within reach. And a squirrel that has denned successfully in an attic remembers it and scent-marks it, so the site stays attractive to that animal and to others season after season unless it is sealed.

## The squirrel season in Northeast Wisconsin

Squirrels are active and present year-round here — they do not hibernate — but the pressure on your building rises and falls with the seasons.

Through **spring**, the late-winter litters are being raised, often in attics that the females moved into weeks earlier. This is when a homeowner who ignored a little scratching in January suddenly hears a lot more activity, because the young are now mobile and noisy. It is also the season when the damage from a winter den — soiled insulation, chewed wiring, a gnawed-open hole — becomes obvious.

Through **summer**, most squirrels are living outdoors in leaf nests (dreys) up in the trees and denning in natural cavities; attic pressure eases somewhat. Reds are busy defending territory and caching cones. But the second breeding season in June and July is already setting up the next wave.

**Fall** is the first big invasion window. As the late-summer litter disperses and the weather turns, young squirrels flood the landscape looking for winter quarters, and buildings near woodlots get probed hard — soffits, vents, and rooflines tested for any weakness. Homes with branches overhanging the roof are the easiest hits. This is the season to have the roofline sealed before the animals find the gap.

**Winter** drives the animals that got in to stay put, and by late winter the cycle restarts with females seeking den sites for the first litter. A squirrel denning in your attic in January is warm, safe, and about to raise young — which is why a small noise in deep winter is worth acting on before spring turns it into a full family. Sealing the building outside these windows, ideally in the calmer stretches of late spring or summer, keeps the fall and late-winter pushes from finding a way in.

## Where squirrels come from: the setting around Clintonville

Squirrels do not wander in randomly; they come off the trees, and the wooded, glacial terrain around here is squirrel country. Knowing what surrounds a building tells you how much pressure it is under and where to look.

**Homes near woodlots and farm groves** are the classic case. Oak and hickory woodlots feed heavy gray populations; conifer stands and mixed woods carry the reds. A house sitting at the edge of the woods, or with a mature yard of big shade trees, is within an easy jump or climb of the roof. The single biggest factor we see is **overhanging branches** — any limb within roughly six to eight feet of the roofline is a launch pad, and cutting those branches back is often the highest-value thing a homeowner can do.

**Older farmhouses** offer a lot of roofline to exploit. Decades of weather leave fascia and soffit boards soft and gapped, dormers and additions create roofline joints that never sealed tightly, and open gable vents are an easy chew-through. The tall, complex rooflines of these homes give squirrels many candidate entry points, and the balloon-framed ones let an animal that gets into a wall or soffit travel farther inside than the homeowner expects.

**Seasonal cabins** around Iola and the Chain O' Lakes are prime red-squirrel targets. They sit quiet and empty in the woods through the exact months squirrels are denning, offering warmth and zero disturbance. An owner who closes up in October and returns in spring can find a winter's worth of chewing, nesting, and soiled insulation. **Village homes** in Clintonville proper, with their mature tree-lined lots, get plenty of gray squirrel pressure at the soffit returns, gable vents, and rooflines. And **pole barns and outbuildings** draw reds especially, which cache cones and nest in stored materials; the gaps at the eaves and gable ends of a pole building are easy access.

## How do you know you have squirrels?

Squirrels tend to be obvious once you know what to listen and look for. **Daytime noise** is the leading sign — brisk scampering, rolling, and gnawing in the attic or soffit, strongest at dawn and again before dusk. A distinct **rolling or dropping sound** is often squirrels moving cached nuts or acorns across the attic floor. **A gnawed entry hole**, roughly golf-ball to fist-sized with a rim of fresh, light-colored wood shavings, is the physical proof; look up high, at soffit returns, fascia edges, gable and ridge vents, and roofline joints. **Chewing damage** shows on fascia, trim, vent screening, and — the dangerous one — electrical wiring in the attic.

Inside the attic you will find **nesting material** — shredded insulation, leaves, and debris gathered into a den — along with **droppings and urine staining** in the nesting area, and **soiled, matted, displaced insulation** that has lost its value. Outside, look for **worn, greasy travel routes** up downspouts, corners, and along branches, and for the squirrels themselves running the roofline. A **drey** — a bulky leaf nest wedged in a tree fork near the house — signals a resident population that may test the building. And in late winter and spring, more noise and more voices usually means a litter of young has arrived.

## Are squirrels dangerous?

The costs of an attic squirrel problem fall into two buckets, and both are real. On the **structural and fire** side, the gnawing is the core issue: squirrels chew wiring, and stripped or damaged wiring in an attic full of dry nesting material is a documented cause of house fires. They chew structural wood, fascia, soffit, and vent screening, and a female enlarging a hole to move young in turns a small gap into a big one. They shred and compact **insulation**, which loses R-value where they nest and travel, and heavy contamination means the insulation has to be removed and replaced. Water intrusion through a gnawed-open roofline adds rot and mold over time. None of this is instant, but across a winter it compounds into real repair bills.

On the **health** side, squirrels are wildlife, and their droppings and urine can carry parasites and pathogens; accumulated waste in attic insulation is not something to disturb casually. They bring in **fleas, ticks, and mites** that can move down into the living space. And a cornered or handled squirrel — especially a mother defending young — will bite and scratch, which is one more reason removal is a job for someone equipped for it rather than a homeowner reaching into an attic. ## Why doesn't DIY work on squirrels?

The typical homeowner response to attic squirrels is a live trap from the farm store and, if that seems to work, plugging the hole. It almost never solves the problem, and it frequently makes things worse. The first issue is **sequence and timing**. If you seal the hole without confirming the attic is empty, you either trap the animal inside — where it dies in a wall void and stinks for weeks, or does frantic damage trying to escape — or, worse, you seal a mother out and leave a litter of young to starve in the insulation, which is both inhumane and a serious odor and contamination problem. Squirrels have young in the den during both of their main invasion windows, so this is not a rare edge case; it is the norm.

The second issue is that **trapping treats the animal, not the access**. Northeast Wisconsin has no shortage of squirrels, and a gnawed, scent-marked hole in your roofline is a flagged invitation. Remove one squirrel and leave the hole, and the next one — drawn by the scent and the obvious opening — moves in within days or weeks. People trap the "same" squirrel over and over and conclude squirrels are impossible. They are not; the access simply was never closed.

The third issue is **the repair itself**. Squirrels gnaw, so sealing a roofline against them is not a matter of stuffing a hole with foam or screen — they chew right back through soft materials. It takes heavy-gauge metal flashing, hardware cloth, and proper structural repair of the often rotted, weather-damaged wood around the entry, installed to the standard that a determined rodent cannot defeat. And it is usually **high, awkward work** — a soffit return two stories up, a gable vent at the peak, a roofline joint over a dormer — which is dangerous for a homeowner on a ladder and is exactly where the amateur repair fails first. There is also the regulatory piece: squirrels are a game species in Wisconsin with rules around trapping and relocation, which a licensed operator handles as a matter of course.

## How do you keep squirrels out?

Keeping squirrels off a building is mostly about closing the roofline and cutting the routes onto the roof. On the **structure**, the priorities are the high, vulnerable spots: seal and reinforce soffit returns and fascia gaps, screen or cap gable and ridge vents with heavy metal mesh, close the roofline joints where dormers and additions meet the main roof, and repair any soft, rotted wood before a squirrel finds it. Use materials a rodent cannot gnaw — heavy-gauge hardware cloth and metal flashing, not foam, plastic, or light screen. Because our older farmhouses and complex rooflines offer so many candidate openings, a methodical inspection of the whole roofline beats patching the one hole you happened to notice.

Cut the **routes onto the roof** as well. Trim back any branches within roughly six to eight feet of the roofline — this single step removes the launch pads squirrels rely on and is often the highest-value prevention on a wooded lot. Keep firewood, trellises, and anything climbable away from the walls, and consider guards on downspouts squirrels are using as ladders. Around **cabins and outbuildings**, close the eave and gable gaps that reds exploit, and store any seed, feed, or cones-attracting material in sealed metal.

Deny them a reason to stay, too. Bird feeders right against the house concentrate squirrels at the building and give them a food-anchored reason to probe the roofline; moving feeders well away from the structure lowers the pressure. On a wooded rural lot the squirrels own the landscape and always will — the realistic goal is a sealed, sound building plus the branch clearance that keeps them off the roof, not a squirrel-free property.
