## What are shrews, and how do you identify them?

Shrews are one of the more misunderstood animals we get called about, mostly because they are small and mouse-shaped and get lumped in with rodents — which they are not. A shrew is an **insectivore**, more closely related to a mole than to a [mouse](/pest-library/mice/), and everything about how it behaves and how you deal with it flows from that. Where a mouse comes indoors seeking your food and your warmth, a shrew comes in **hunting** — following insects, spiders, worms, and other small prey — and that single difference is the key to controlling it. Around Clintonville shrews turn up in damp basements, crawlspaces, garages, and outbuildings, usually one at a time, and more often startle a homeowner than do any real harm.

Telling a shrew from the small rodents is straightforward once you know what to look at. A shrew has a **long, pointed, constantly moving snout**, tiny eyes almost lost in the fur, ears hidden in dense velvety fur that is usually dark gray to nearly black, and small sharp teeth suited to eating insects rather than the chisel-shaped gnawing incisors of a rodent. Compare that to a mouse — blunter nose, prominent eyes, large ears — or a [vole](/pest-library/voles/), which is chunky with a blunt nose and short tail. Shrews also move at a **frantic, twitchy pace**, driven by an extreme metabolism, and, tellingly, they are very often found **dead**: a shrew burns energy so fast it can starve within hours of not eating, so people frequently discover a dead shrew on the basement floor rather than catching a live one.

The common shrew here, and the one that matters most, is the **northern short-tailed shrew** — a relatively large, stocky, dark, short-tailed shrew found across the region's woods, fields, wetlands, and yards. It is the one most likely to come indoors, and it is the one with the venomous bite. Smaller species like the masked shrew live in the surrounding fields and woods but rarely become a household concern. For control, they all behave the same way: tiny insectivores following prey through low gaps.

## How do shrews live and breed in Wisconsin?

The defining fact about a shrew is its **metabolism**, which runs at an extraordinary pace. A shrew's heart rate and energy burn are so high that it must eat almost constantly — many shrews consume their own body weight or more in prey every day — and it can **starve to death within a few hours** of being unable to feed. This drives everything: shrews are active around the clock in short bursts of frantic hunting and rest, they do not hibernate, and they cannot afford to wander far from food. It is also why finding dead shrews indoors is so common — one that gets into a basement or garage and cannot quickly find enough insects simply does not survive the outing.

Shrews are relentless **predators** for their size, eating insects, spiders, earthworms, snails, other invertebrates, and occasionally small vertebrates. The **northern short-tailed shrew** takes this a step further: its saliva contains a **toxin** it uses to subdue prey, making it one of the very few venomous mammals in North America. The venom lets it overpower and even cache live prey. For a person, a short-tailed shrew bite is not medically dangerous, but it can be genuinely and disproportionately painful, with local swelling and irritation — a good reason never to pick one up or corner it barehanded.

Shrews live mostly in the runways, burrows, and litter of moist, cover-rich habitat, often using the tunnels of [voles](/pest-library/voles/) and moles. They breed through the warm months, producing one or more litters a year with several young, though their fast-paced lives are short. They possess **scent glands** that give them a strong musky odor, which makes them unpalatable to many predators — cats and dogs often catch a shrew and then leave it uneaten. On the landscape they are a natural part of the fauna, and useful ones, eating large numbers of insects; they only become a "pest" when they follow prey indoors and cross paths with people.

That metabolic pace is worth dwelling on because it explains nearly everything odd about how shrews behave around a house. An animal that must eat every few hours cannot ration or wait out a bad situation the way a mouse can; it hunts more or less constantly and dies quickly when hunting fails. This is why a shrew indoors is almost never a settled, breeding infestation the way a mouse population becomes — it is a lone hunter passing through, and one of two things happens: it finds enough prey to keep going and moves on, or it does not and starves within hours. It is also why shrews do not hoard your food, do not gnaw their way through the house, and do not build the nests and colonies that rodents do. Reading a shrew correctly — as a transient, prey-driven insectivore rather than a nesting rodent — is what steers the response toward the two things that actually matter: the low gaps and the insects. A homeowner who instead treats it as a mouse infestation, setting bait and expecting a colony, is solving a problem the shrew does not represent while missing the two it does.

## The shrew season in Northeast Wisconsin

Shrews are active year-round — the extreme metabolism does not allow for hibernation — so they are a possibility in any season, but the indoor encounters have some rhythm to them.

Through **spring and summer**, shrews are hunting in the moist, prey-rich cover outdoors — leaf litter, mulch, ground cover, tall grass, and the runways of other small mammals — where insects and invertebrates are abundant. Populations build over the warm months. Indoor sightings in this period usually trace to a damp, buggy basement, crawlspace, or garage that offers the same hunting conditions, or to a shrew that fell into a window well.

**Fall** tends to bring more indoor encounters, for the same reason so many pests come in then: as the weather cools and outdoor insect activity begins to wind down, shrews follow prey toward the warmer, still-active insect populations in and around buildings, and they slip through the same low gaps that let in mice during the fall rodent push. A basement or outbuilding that has an insect population and open ground-level gaps is a natural draw.

Through **winter**, shrews remain active and still must feed constantly, hunting whatever invertebrate prey survives in sheltered spots — including the insects overwintering in and around a heated building. A shrew that gets into a basement or crawlspace in winter is hunting the bugs sheltering there. Because they cannot slow down, a shrew that cannot find enough prey indoors in any season quickly dies, which is why the winter sign is so often a dead shrew rather than an established one. The through-line across all seasons is the same: shrews go where the prey is, and they get in through low gaps.

## Where shrews come from: the setting around Clintonville

Shrews are woodland-and-field animals of the moist, cover-rich habitat that surrounds nearly every property around here, and they come indoors from that habitat when prey and an opening line up. Understanding what draws them points straight at the fix, because with a shrew the draw is **insects**, not your pantry.

**Damp, insect-rich basements, crawlspaces, and garages** are the classic indoor setting. A basement with moisture and a healthy population of spiders, ground beetles, and other invertebrates is a hunting ground, and a shrew that finds a way in will work it. **Outbuildings** — sheds, pole barns, and the like — with damp corners, stored materials, and insect populations offer the same. The common thread is prey plus a way in.

Outdoors, shrews concentrate in **leaf litter, mulch beds, ground cover, tall grass, woodpiles, and debris** against and near the foundation — the moist, sheltered spots their invertebrate prey favors. Heavy mulch and dense plantings against the house both feed a shrew population and put it right at the building's low gaps. **Window wells** are a frequent trap: shrews fall in hunting the insects that collect there and cannot climb back out, which is why they turn up dead in wells and in the basements the wells open into.

They enter through the same **ground-level openings** that admit [mice](/pest-library/mice/): foundation cracks, gaps at the sill plate, utility and pipe penetrations, gaps under doors, and openings around basement windows. Because a shrew comes in low and following prey, the fix is the same low-gap sealing we do for rodents, combined with knocking down the insects that made the space worth hunting in the first place.

## How do you know you have shrews?

Shrew sign is subtle, and the most common sign is the animal itself — often dead. **A tiny, dark, pointed-snouted animal**, alive and moving frantically or, more often, found dead on a basement, garage, or outbuilding floor, is the usual first sign. Because shrews starve so fast indoors, discovering a dead one is genuinely common and is a meaningful clue: it tells you there is a ground-level entry point and, often, an insect population that drew it.

Other signs are minor. **Small droppings**, often found near where insects gather, may turn up, though they are easy to miss. You will not see the gnaw damage, greasy rub marks, and large droppings of a rodent, because shrews are not rodents and do not gnaw or travel fixed greasy runways the way mice and rats do. You may notice a faint **musky odor** from their scent glands, and pets may catch or show interest in shrews near the foundation, window wells, and damp corners. The best "sign" to act on, honestly, is the combination the shrew points to: **low entry gaps plus an indoor insect population** — the two underlying conditions that let a shrew in and gave it a reason to stay. Reading a shrew as a symptom of those two things is more useful than treating it as an infestation in its own right.

## Are shrews dangerous?

Shrews are a low-risk animal overall, and it helps to be clear about that so the response is proportionate. On the **structural** side, there is essentially nothing to worry about: shrews are not gnawers like rodents, so they do not chew wiring, enlarge holes, or shred insulation the way mice and [squirrels](/pest-library/squirrels/) do. A shrew will not damage your house.

On the **health** side, the concerns are minor and mostly about the bite. The **northern short-tailed shrew's venomous bite** is the standout feature, but in a person it is painful rather than dangerous — local pain, swelling, and irritation, out of proportion to the animal's size, but not medically serious. The venom evolved to subdue insects, worms, and other small prey, not to defend against something the size of a person, so a bite is a defensive reaction to being cornered or grabbed rather than anything a shrew seeks out. The practical takeaway is simple: do not handle a shrew or corner it barehanded, and keep curious children and pets from trying to. Shrews are not significant disease vectors the way house mice and especially [deer mice](/pest-library/deer-mice/) are — they do not carry the hantavirus concern that makes deer-mouse cleanup a careful business — though, like any small mammal, they can carry some parasites, and a pet that catches one can pick those up. Their **musky odor** is a nuisance, and a dead shrew left in place will smell. In short, a shrew is more startling than hazardous; the reason to deal with it is comfort and the two underlying conditions it points to, not damage or serious disease.

## Why doesn't DIY work on shrews?

The instinct with a shrew is to treat it like a mouse — set a trap, maybe put out bait — and it usually falls short, because a shrew is not a mouse and the two things that brought it in are left untouched. **Bait aimed at rodents does not work well on a shrew**, which is after prey, not grain or seed — it wants insects, not the food a mouse bait is built around. So the standard rodent bait approach often does nothing.

More fundamentally, **a shrew indoors is a symptom, and trapping treats only the symptom**. The two underlying conditions are open ground-level gaps and an indoor insect population, and neither is fixed by removing one shrew. Leave the gaps and the bugs, and another shrew can follow the same prey through the same openings. People catch a shrew, think it is handled, and are puzzled when another appears — because the hunting ground and the door are both still there. Lasting control means **sealing the low entry points** and **reducing the insect prey** so the space stops being worth hunting in, with removal of any shrews present as one part of the job, not the whole of it.

There is also the matter of the **bite**. A cornered short-tailed shrew will bite, and the venom makes that bite painful, so trying to grab or corner one barehanded is a mistake. And because shrews die so fast indoors, a DIY effort often just means finding dead shrews repeatedly while the gaps and the insects go unaddressed. The effective approach is the one that closes the two doors — the physical gaps and the prey supply — rather than removing shrews one at a time.

## How do you keep shrews out?

Preventing shrews means closing the low gaps and taking away the prey and the damp cover that draw them. On the **building**, seal the ground-level openings a shrew slips through — foundation cracks, gaps at the sill plate, utility and pipe penetrations, gaps under doors, and openings around basement windows — using the same approach and materials as rodent exclusion. Cover or screen **window wells**, which are a frequent trap that funnels shrews (and the insects they hunt) into basements. Because shrews enter low and small, the same methodical sealing of ground-level gaps that keeps mice out keeps shrews out.

Cut the **prey supply and the cover** that make a space worth hunting. Reduce the indoor insect populations in damp basements, crawlspaces, garages, and outbuildings through general pest control and by addressing **moisture** — a drier basement supports fewer of the invertebrates a shrew is after. Outdoors, pull heavy **mulch, leaf litter, dense ground cover, tall grass, woodpiles, and debris** back from the foundation, since that damp cover feeds both the shrew's prey and the shrew, and puts them right at the building's low gaps. Keeping a drier, more open, lower-cover strip around the foundation lowers the pressure from both the bugs and the shrews hunting them.

The reframe worth keeping in mind: with a shrew, you are not really defending against the shrew, you are removing its reason to come in. Seal the low gaps, dry out and de-bug the basement or garage, and clear the damp cover at the foundation, and the space stops being a hunting ground and a shrew has neither a door nor a dinner.
