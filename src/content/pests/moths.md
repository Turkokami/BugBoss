## What indoor moths are, and how to know which one you have

When people call about moths in the house, they are almost always dealing with one of two very different problems that happen to share the word "moth." One kind is after your food. The other is after your clothes. Telling them apart is the first and most important step, because the source, the damage, and the fix are entirely different — and around Clintonville and the rural stretches of Waupaca, Shawano, and Outagamie counties, we see plenty of both.

The **pantry moths**, also called stored-product moths, are led by the **Indian meal moth** (*Plodia interpunctella*), far and away the most common one in our kitchens. The adult is small, about a half-inch across the wings, and unmistakable once you know it: the forewings are two-toned, pale gray or cream on the half nearest the body and a coppery, reddish-bronze on the outer half. That copper-tipped wing is the tell. You usually notice the adults first, fluttering in a weak, aimless way around the kitchen in the evening, drawn toward lights.

The **clothes moths** are a different animal. The two that matter, the **webbing clothes moth** (*Tineola bisselliella*) and the **casemaking clothes moth** (*Tinea pellionella*), are small, plain buff or golden moths with no bold pattern. Crucially, they behave the opposite of pantry moths: they are weak fliers that **avoid light** and dart for the nearest dark cover when disturbed, so you rarely see them out in the open. If moths are flying around your lamps, they are almost certainly pantry moths; clothes moths stay hidden in the closet.

For both kinds, the single fact that changes everything is this: the **adult moth does no damage**. Adult pantry moths do not eat your food, and adult clothes moths do not eat anything at all — the clothes moth adult does not even have working mouthparts. The adults exist only to mate and lay eggs. All of the destruction is done by the **larvae**, small caterpillar-like grubs feeding out of sight in your food or your fabric. This is why swatting the moths you see accomplishes nothing about the actual problem, and it is the first thing the [team at BugBoss](/residential-pest-control/) explains when the calls come in.

## Wisconsin biology and lifecycle

Both groups run the same basic cycle — egg, larva, pupa, adult — but on different food, and the details of each explain why they are so persistent.

**Pantry moths** lay eggs directly on or near stored food. The larvae hatch and feed on dry plant products: flour, cornmeal, cereal, grains, pasta, dried fruit, nuts, seeds, spices, chocolate, birdseed, and pet food. As they feed they spin **silk webbing** through the food, matting it into clumps and leaving a telltale gauzy mess. When a larva is full-grown, it does something that trips up almost everyone: it **crawls away from the food to pupate**, often traveling a surprising distance and climbing up walls to spin its cocoon high in a corner or where the wall meets the ceiling. That wandering is why you find little cocoons and crawling larvae in rooms far from the infested package. Indoors, with warmth and food, pantry moths breed continuously, running several overlapping generations a year, so a small infestation compounds quickly if the source is not found.

**Clothes moths** lay their eggs on animal fibers, and the larvae feed on **keratin** — the protein in wool, fur, feathers, silk, felt, and hair — exactly the same food that draws carpet beetles. The webbing clothes moth larva spins silk as it feeds, leaving webbing and silken feeding tubes on the fabric; the casemaking clothes moth larva builds and carries a small portable case made of silk and fiber fragments, dragging it along as it grazes. Either way, the larvae favor dark, undisturbed, and often soiled spots — the folds of a stored blanket, under a collar, the shaded underside of a rug — and can feed for months. Adults live only a short time, just long enough to mate and lay the next round of eggs on suitable material.

The shared lesson is that these are **larval problems fed by a hidden food source**. The moths you see are the tail end of the cycle, not the cause, and until the material the larvae are feeding on is found and dealt with, the cycle simply repeats.

It is also worth knowing that the Indian meal moth rarely travels alone. The same stored dry goods that feed it — flour, grain, cornmeal, birdseed, pet food — also feed a group of small **stored-product beetles**, including flour beetles, sawtoothed grain beetles, and drugstore and cigarette beetles, which infest the same packages and often turn up in the same cupboard. So a pantry problem that starts as "moths" sometimes proves to be a mixed infestation, and clearing it means checking for the beetles too. This is one more reason a thorough search beats reacting to whatever insect happens to be flying that day, and it is why we treat the pantry as a whole rather than chasing a single species.

## The moth season in Northeast Wisconsin

Both kinds of moths are, like carpet beetles, essentially **year-round indoor pests**, because a heated Wisconsin home stays warm enough for the larvae to keep feeding and breeding straight through winter. There is no cold season that clears them out the way frost ends the outdoor insects.

That said, people notice **pantry moths** most in the warmer months and whenever stored food has had time to sit — late summer through fall is common, as pantries fill and bulk birdseed and deer corn come into the house and outbuildings ahead of hunting and feeding season. Warmth speeds up their generations, so a package that would slowly develop an infestation over months in a cool room can boil over in a warm one.

**Clothes moths** follow the rhythm of stored clothing. They quietly feed on packed-away woolens through the seasons those items sit unused — the winter coats and wool blankets stored all summer, the summer items packed away all winter — and damage is often discovered when something is pulled out of storage for the change of season and found full of holes. In a cabin or a seldom-used closet, the larvae can work undisturbed for a very long time.

There is a Wisconsin-specific wrinkle to the timing, too. Fall is when a lot of the raw material for both moths arrives at once. Hunting season brings home hides, wool clothing, and fresh taxidermy work; bird-feeding season fills garages and porches with bags of seed and suet; and the pantry gets stocked with baking supplies and bulk staples heading into winter. A quiet moth problem that had been simmering along on a small source can find a great deal of new food in a single season, which is why some households notice the first real flush of moths a month or two after everything gets brought in and put away for the cold months.

## Local drivers: why rural homes and cabins get both

A few features of life in Northeast Wisconsin quietly stack the deck for indoor moths, and knowing them tells you where to look.

For **pantry moths**, the driver is **bulk dry-goods storage**, which is simply how rural households run. Homes here keep large quantities of flour and baking supplies, buy pet food and especially **birdseed** in big bags, store **deer corn** and other wildlife feed, and often keep home-dried foods, nuts, and grains on hand. Every one of those is a potential pantry moth source, and the bigger and longer-stored the supply, the more likely it is to harbor an infestation that then spreads to the rest of the cupboard. Birdseed and pet food kept in a garage, mudroom, or porch are especially common starting points because they sit undisturbed and are rarely inspected. You can read more about the specific problem of [pantry pests in the cupboard](/pest-problems/pantry-pests-in-cupboard/), which covers moths and stored-product beetles together.

For **clothes moths**, the driver is the same stock of animal-fiber goods that feeds carpet beetles: wool blankets and wool hunting clothes, down and feather bedding, **taxidermy mounts** and tanned hides in hunting country, felt, furs, and wool rugs. A single stored wool item or an old mount in a back closet can seed an infestation, and because clothes moths and [carpet beetles](/pest-library/carpet-beetles/) eat the same material and hide in the same dark closets, the two frequently turn up together — which is exactly why identifying which one you have, by looking for webbing versus cast skins, matters before treating.

**Seasonal cabins** combine both risks with the worst possible condition: no one there to catch it early. Bulk food and birdseed left in a closed cabin draw pantry moths, and wool bedding, hides, and taxidermy draw clothes moths, all developing undisturbed for months. ## Signs of an infestation

For **pantry moths**, the signs are the weak-flying, copper-tipped adults around the kitchen in the evening; **silk webbing, clumping, and matting** inside packages of flour, cereal, birdseed, pet food, or other dry goods; small caterpillar-like larvae in or near the food; and — the sign that surprises people — larvae crawling up walls and small **cocoons in high corners and where wall meets ceiling**, sometimes rooms away from the food. A musty or off smell in a heavily infested package can accompany the rest.

For **clothes moths**, the signs are **irregular holes** in wool, fur, silk, or felt items, especially in hidden or soiled areas like folds, cuffs, and the undersides of rugs; **silken webbing, tubes, or small cases** on the fabric, along with gritty debris; and, occasionally, the small plain moths themselves flitting out of a closet when disturbed. Because clothes moth larvae work in the dark and quiet, the damage is usually found long before the insect is, often when a stored item is pulled out and discovered ruined.

One caution on identification: not every moth indoors is a stored-food or fabric pest. In summer and early fall, ordinary outdoor moths wander in through open doors and torn screens, drawn to lights, and they have no interest in your food or clothing — they are just passing through and will die off or leave. The way to tell a real infestation from a stray is the pattern: a genuine pantry or clothes moth problem comes with the evidence of larvae, webbing, cocoons, or damage at a source inside the house, not just a moth or two at the window on a warm night. If there is no source and no damage, a lone moth is usually nothing to worry about.

## Health and property risk

Neither pantry nor clothes moths bite, sting, or transmit disease, so there is **no direct health threat**. Accidentally eating a few pantry moth eggs or larvae in contaminated food is not harmful — unappetizing, certainly, but not dangerous. The real cost is elsewhere. Pantry moths force you to throw out contaminated food, sometimes a good deal of it, and the contamination and cleanup are a genuine nuisance. Clothes moths destroy belongings, and often irreplaceable ones — heirloom woolens, hunting trophies, furs, antique rugs — quietly and thoroughly before they are caught. As with carpet beetles, the value at stake with clothes moths is the property, which is what makes catching them early worthwhile even though they pose no risk to your health or your home's structure.

## Why DIY usually fails

The classic pantry moth mistake is going after the adults — spraying the kitchen, hanging strips, swatting the moths at the light — while never finding the infested food that is producing them. Since the adults do no damage and the larvae are hidden in a package or a spill, killing the visible moths changes nothing, and they keep reappearing. The second, related failure is not searching thoroughly enough: people clean out the one obviously infested box and stop, missing the package pushed to the back, the flour spilled in a crack, the larvae that crawled off to pupate in another room, or the second source entirely — the bag of birdseed in the garage, the deer corn in the shed, the pet food on the porch. Pantry moths are persistent for exactly this reason, and one overlooked source restarts the whole cycle.

Pheromone traps are another point of confusion. The sticky pantry-moth traps sold in stores use a lure that attracts male Indian meal moths, and they are useful — but as a **monitoring tool**, to confirm you have them and gauge whether a problem is ongoing, not as a control. A trap catches a fraction of the males and does nothing about the females, the eggs, or the feeding larvae, so hanging a trap and considering the job done leaves the source untouched and the infestation running. Used to tell you whether you have cleared the source, the traps earn their keep; relied on to fix the problem, they disappoint.

Clothes moths fail DIY efforts for a different reason: they are simply hard to find. The adults hide, the larvae feed in the dark on stored items, and the damage is discovered late. Treating one visible garment while larvae continue in an adjacent stored item, or in a rug edge under the furniture, gets nowhere. And because clothes moths and carpet beetles look like the same problem to a homeowner, people frequently treat for the wrong one. Getting the identification right and, more importantly, locating **every** source is what the job actually requires, and it is more work than it first appears.

## Prevention and keeping them out

Prevention for both kinds of moths comes down to denying the larvae their food, and it is genuinely effective once you commit to it.

For **pantry moths**: transfer dry goods out of their original paper and cardboard and into **sealed glass, metal, or hard plastic containers**, which both traps any hidden infestation and keeps new moths out; buy flour, grains, and other staples in amounts you will actually use before they sit for months; and store **birdseed, pet food, and animal feed sealed and away from the living space**, since those bulk items are such frequent starting points here. Check new packages when they come home, use older stock first, and clean up spills in cupboards promptly, because a little spilled flour or seed in a crack is enough to sustain a population.

For **clothes moths**: clean wool, fur, and silk items before long-term storage, since larvae are drawn to sweat, food, and soiling; store woolens, furs, and down in **sealed containers or garment bags** rather than open closets or cardboard; and rotate, inspect, and air out stored items periodically so nothing feeds undisturbed for a year. Vacuum closets, under furniture, and rug edges regularly, and deal with any keratin reservoirs in the house — old nests, dead-insect accumulations, forgotten hides or mounts — that could be feeding the moths. As with carpet beetles, in a rural home the durable fix often means addressing the same closets, attics, and stored goods that harbor the whole fabric-pest complex.
