## Two very different cockroaches, two different problems

When people say "cockroaches," they usually picture one generic bug, but in Northeast Wisconsin two distinct species drive nearly all the calls, and confusing them is the fastest way to fail at control. The **German cockroach** is a small, indoor-breeding species that lives its whole life inside warm buildings — the roach of kitchens, apartments, restaurants, and food service. The **American cockroach** is a big, moisture-loving species that lives in drains, sewers, sump pits, and damp basements and comes up from below. They look different, they live in different places, they get in by different routes, and they call for different control. So the first thing BugBoss does on any roach call is confirm which one you actually have.

Both share one thing, though, and it is central to how they are treated: cockroaches are controlled with **targeted baiting and sanitation, not with the sprays most people reach for first.** With German cockroaches especially, spraying is not just ineffective — it is actively counterproductive, scattering the population and spreading it through the building. That single fact separates modern, successful cockroach work from the spray-and-pray approach that leaves people fighting the same infestation for months. This page explains both roaches, why they are a real health and allergy problem, and why the baiting-and-source approach is the one that works.

## Identification: German versus American

Telling the two apart is easy once you know what to look for, and it tells you almost everything about how to proceed.

The **German cockroach** (*Blattella germanica*) is small — about half an inch long as an adult — light tan to brown, with two dark parallel stripes running lengthwise on the shield just behind the head. It has wings but rarely flies. You find it in the kitchen and around food and moisture: near the stove, the dishwasher, the refrigerator, the coffee maker, under the sink, in cabinet seams. If you see small brown roaches in the kitchen, particularly several of them or ones out during the day, it is almost certainly German cockroaches, and that means an indoor breeding population — because German cockroaches do not live outdoors here at all.

The **American cockroach** (*Periplaneta americana*) is much larger — well over an inch, often close to an inch and a half — and reddish-brown, with a pale yellowish margin around the shield behind the head. Despite the name it is not native; people sometimes call it a "water bug" or, in the South, a "palmetto bug." You find it in wet, dark places: floor drains, sump pits, sewers, crawlspaces, and damp basement corners, often coming up from the drain lines or moving in from outdoors through pipe penetrations. If you find a big reddish roach in the basement or by a drain, that is an American cockroach, and it points to a moisture and drain problem rather than a kitchen breeding problem.

There are a couple of others you might encounter — the **Oriental cockroach**, another large, dark, moisture-loving species found in cool damp basements and drains and treated much like the American, and the occasional **brown-banded cockroach** in a warm building — but German and American are the two that account for the overwhelming majority of real infestations in this area, and they are the two worth understanding in depth.

## Wisconsin biology and life cycle

Cockroaches develop by gradual metamorphosis: egg, then a series of nymph stages that look like small wingless adults, then the adult. There is no maggot or grub stage. The details of the German cockroach's biology in particular are what make it such a formidable indoor pest.

A female German cockroach produces an **egg case (ootheca)** and, unusually, carries it protruding from her abdomen until it is nearly ready to hatch, protecting the developing eggs the whole time. Each case holds roughly **30 to 40 eggs**, and a female produces **several cases over her life** — so one female can be responsible for well over a hundred offspring. Because she carries the case until just before hatch, the eggs are shielded from most surface treatments, which is exactly why control has to account for the next generation and cannot be a one-shot event.

The nymphs mature quickly in the warm, food-rich environment of a heated building — a matter of a couple of months from hatch to breeding adult, faster in ideal conditions. Generations overlap, and with each female producing dozens of offspring per case, the population compounds. A German cockroach infestation that starts from a few individuals hitchhiking in on a cardboard box can become a serious problem within a couple of months. And because they are a **tropical species that cannot survive outdoors in Wisconsin**, they live entirely indoors, breeding year-round wherever it stays warm — the season outside is irrelevant to them.

The **American cockroach** develops more slowly and lives longer — its life cycle can run many months to over a year. Its egg case holds fewer eggs (around 14 to 16), and it deposits the case in a sheltered spot rather than carrying it. American cockroaches favor warm, humid places and can persist in sewers, heated utility rooms, boiler rooms, and chronically damp basements. They breed more slowly than German cockroaches, so they rarely build the explosive indoor populations German roaches do, but a persistent moisture source and an open drain route will keep supplying them.

## The season here: indoors, roaches have no off-season

It is tempting to think a Wisconsin winter takes care of a cockroach problem. It does not — and understanding why matters.

German cockroaches live exclusively inside heated structures, so from their point of view it is summer all year. A kitchen, an apartment, a restaurant line, or a care facility stays warm and humid twelve months a year, and the roaches breed continuously through the depths of January just as they do in July. The one seasonal pattern worth noting is a human one: people crank up the heat, spend more time cooking indoors, and move deliveries and boxes around during the holidays and the cold months, all of which can help an infestation along. But there is no natural winter die-off indoors. This is why "waiting it out" never works with German cockroaches.

American cockroaches have a slight seasonal edge outdoors — they are more active and more likely to wander in during warm, wet weather, and a rainy summer can push more of them up out of sewers and drains. But the ones established in a warm, damp basement, a sump area, or a utility room persist year-round too. In either case, the Wisconsin climate is not doing you any favors; the warmth of the building is the roach's whole world, and it stays hospitable regardless of the calendar.

## Local sources: how cockroaches get in and where they thrive

**German cockroaches almost always arrive by hitchhiking.** They ride in on cardboard boxes and grocery bags, in deliveries and shipments, in used appliances — a secondhand refrigerator, microwave, or dishwasher is a classic carrier — in secondhand furniture and electronics, and in personal belongings moved from an infested location. In multi-unit housing, apartments, and duplexes, they also spread between units through shared walls, plumbing chases, and utility penetrations, so a neighbor's infestation can become yours. Once inside, they settle wherever there is warmth, moisture, food, and a tight crack to hide in — which is why the kitchen and, secondarily, the bathroom are ground zero. Restaurants, taverns, bakeries, food processing, grocery back rooms, and institutional kitchens are especially prone because they offer roaches everything they need on an industrial scale, and because the constant flow of deliveries keeps reintroducing them.

**American cockroaches come from the wet infrastructure below and around a building.** They travel through sewer and drain lines and can come up through floor drains, especially ones that have dried out and lost the water seal in their trap. They move in from outdoors through gaps around pipes, utility penetrations, and foundation cracks, drawn to moisture and warmth. They thrive in sump pits, damp crawlspaces, chronically wet basements, and around leaking plumbing, and in commercial and institutional buildings they haunt boiler rooms, steam tunnels, and utility spaces. Their presence is a strong indicator of a moisture problem and an open route from the drains or exterior — so controlling them is heavily about moisture management and exclusion.

Around Clintonville, Shawano, and Waupaca, the practical picture is this: German cockroaches show up in village apartments and rentals, in restaurants and food businesses, and occasionally in homes after a used appliance or an infested box comes in; American cockroaches show up in damp older basements, around sump and drain areas, and in commercial utility spaces. Older village housing stock, with its aging plumbing and stone or block basements, gives both species more of the cracks and moisture they exploit.

## How do you know you have cockroaches?

The most direct sign is **seeing the roaches**, but the details matter. Because cockroaches are nocturnal and secretive, spending the day jammed into tight cracks, **daytime sightings out in the open usually mean the population is already large** — the harborage is crowded and roaches are being pushed out to find food and space. So catching one in the beam of a flashlight when you flip on the kitchen light at night is an earlier warning than seeing them stroll across the floor at noon.

Beyond live roaches, look for **droppings**: German cockroach droppings look like coarse black pepper or coffee grounds, often smeared along cabinet edges, in drawers, and around harborage; American cockroach droppings are larger, blunt-ended, and can be mistaken for mouse droppings. **Egg cases** — small, brown, capsule-shaped — near harborage confirm breeding. **Shed skins** accumulate where nymphs are maturing. A heavy infestation produces a distinctive **musty, oily odor**. And **smear marks** or dark staining in the cracks where roaches travel show established runways. For American cockroaches specifically, recurring large roaches by a floor drain or sump are the tell. Finding and reading these signs is how we gauge the size and location of the problem before treating.

## Health and allergy risk

Cockroaches are a real health concern on two fronts, and the second is underappreciated.

The first is **contamination**. Cockroaches crawl through drains, sewers, garbage, and filth, then travel across food-preparation surfaces, dishes, and food itself, mechanically carrying pathogens including **Salmonella, E. coli, and other bacteria**, and they can contaminate food and surfaces with these organisms and with their droppings. In a home kitchen this is a hygiene problem; in a restaurant, bakery, grocery, or care facility it is a serious food-safety and regulatory hazard, and a visible cockroach problem is exactly what shuts a food operation down in an inspection.

The second is **allergens and asthma**. Cockroach droppings, shed skins, saliva, and body fragments accumulate as fine particulate that becomes a potent indoor allergen. Sustained exposure — especially in homes and apartments with heavy German cockroach populations — is a well-documented trigger for allergic reactions and, importantly, for **asthma**, with children in infested housing at particular risk. This means a cockroach infestation is not just unpleasant; it is degrading the indoor air and can be making people, especially kids, genuinely sick. Getting the roaches out and cleaning up the residue is an air-quality intervention as much as a pest one. It is one of the strongest reasons not to let a German cockroach problem linger.

## Why DIY (and spraying) usually fails

Cockroaches, and German cockroaches in particular, are where the instinct to grab a can of bug spray backfires the most, and it is worth understanding exactly why.

**Repellent sprays scatter them.** Most over-the-counter cockroach sprays are repellent — the roaches detect the treated surface and avoid it, which sounds fine until you realize what that means in practice: instead of dying, they flee the treated area, dispersing deeper into wall voids, into adjoining cabinets and rooms, and in an apartment building into neighboring units. Spraying a German cockroach infestation frequently spreads it. The population you thought you were hitting simply relocates and keeps breeding.

**Sprays miss the eggs and hit resistant roaches.** Because the female carries the egg case until nearly hatching, the next generation is protected inside her and inside sheltered cases, untouched by surface spray. On top of that, German cockroaches have widespread, well-documented **resistance** to many common spray active ingredients after decades of exposure, so even the roaches that contact the spray may shrug it off. You can empty can after can and select for an ever-hardier population.

**Baiting mistakes and quitting early.** Even people who use bait — the right tool — often undermine it: they place it in the wrong spots (out in the open instead of tucked into the harborage cracks where roaches actually live), they use stale or unattractive bait the roaches ignore, or they simultaneously spray repellents that drive roaches away from the bait. And most critically, they stop as soon as they stop seeing roaches, before the protected egg cases have hatched — so a few weeks later the next generation emerges and the infestation rebounds. Cockroach control is a multi-week, multi-visit process precisely because of those egg cases, and skipping the follow-up is the single most common reason DIY fails.

## How do you keep cockroaches out?

Preventing cockroaches is about denying them the three things they need — food, water, and harborage — and cutting off how they get in.

**Deny food and water.** Keep food in sealed containers, clean up grease and crumbs (paying attention to the spots people forget — under and behind the stove and refrigerator, the toaster tray, the dishwasher gasket), manage garbage in sealed cans, don't leave pet food or dirty dishes out overnight, and fix leaks and drips. A dry, clean kitchen is a far less hospitable place for a German cockroach to establish, and it makes any bait treatment work faster.

**Deny harborage and entry.** Seal the cracks and gaps around appliances, cabinets, baseboards, plumbing, and wall penetrations where roaches hide and travel. For American cockroaches, keep floor drains functional and their traps filled with water (a dried-out trap is an open sewer door), cap unused drains, seal pipe penetrations and foundation gaps, and deal with basement and crawlspace moisture with drainage, dehumidification, and leak repair. Address the routes between units in multi-family housing.

**Stop the hitchhikers.** Because German cockroaches so often arrive on cardboard, in used appliances, and in secondhand items, inspect and break down incoming boxes promptly (don't let cardboard pile up), be cautious with used appliances and furniture, and — for food businesses — inspect deliveries. This is the most overlooked prevention step and one of the most important, since it addresses the actual point of introduction.
