## Bed bugs are hitchhikers, and this is not about how clean you are

Two things make bed bugs different from almost every other pest, and getting both straight from the start saves people a lot of unnecessary shame and a lot of wasted effort. First: **bed bugs have nothing to do with cleanliness.** They do not feed on filth, crumbs, or garbage — they feed on blood, human blood, and a spotless home offers them everything they need just as readily as a messy one. Immaculate houses, brand-new apartments, tidy farmhouses, and four-star hotels all get bed bugs. If you have them, it is not a judgment on your housekeeping, and treating it as a dirty secret only delays getting help.

Second: **bed bugs spread by hitchhiking.** They do not fly, they do not jump, and they do not come up out of the ground or in from the yard. They travel by riding along — in luggage after a night away, in a used couch or mattress brought home, in secondhand clothing, furniture, and electronics, in the bags and belongings of visitors, and, in apartments and duplexes, through the wall voids and utility runs between units. That means the risk factors are ordinary life: traveling, buying used, having guests, living next to an infested unit. Anyone can get them, which is exactly why they have made such a comeback.

Those two facts drive everything else on this page. Because bed bugs hitchhike, prevention is about vigilance with luggage and used items, not about cleaning harder. And because they hide deep and resist common chemistry, do-it-yourself efforts and foggers reliably fail, and getting rid of them takes a professional process built around careful inspection, thorough treatment, and a follow-up visit. ## Identification: what bed bugs look like and how to tell

Adult bed bugs are about the size and color of an apple seed — roughly a quarter-inch long, flat, oval, and reddish-brown, turning darker and more swollen after a blood meal. They are visible to the naked eye, but their flatness lets them wedge into cracks so thin you would never think to look, and they are active mostly at night, so you often find the evidence before you find the bug. The **nymphs** are smaller and paler — nearly translucent when unfed — and the **eggs** are tiny, white, and about a millimeter long, cemented into cracks and seams in small clusters.

Because they hide and feed at night, identification usually rests on a combination of signs rather than a single sighting. **Fecal spots** are among the most reliable: small dark brown or black dots, like marker or ink, often in clusters along mattress seams, on the box spring, on sheets, and in the cracks where bugs harbor; they smear if wiped. **Shed skins** — the pale, empty cast husks nymphs leave as they grow through five stages — accumulate near harborage. **Eggs and eggshells** appear in the same seams and cracks. In heavier infestations there can be a distinctive musty, sweetish odor. And of course **live bugs** tucked into seams, folds, and crevices are definitive.

A word on **bites**, because they are what usually prompts the first worry: bites often show up as itchy red welts on skin that was exposed during sleep, sometimes in a rough line or cluster, but reactions vary enormously — some people welt up dramatically, others show no reaction at all, and bites can be confused with those of fleas or mosquitoes. This is why you cannot diagnose bed bugs from bites alone, and why finding the physical evidence, or having a trained inspection, is what actually confirms an infestation.

## Wisconsin biology and life cycle

Bed bugs develop by gradual metamorphosis: egg, five nymph stages, then adult — and, critically, **every stage from first-instar nymph to adult needs a blood meal to develop and reproduce.** That biology shapes both how they behave and how they must be treated.

A mated female lays eggs a few at a time, cementing them into cracks and seams, and can lay a few hundred over her life. The eggs hatch in about a week to ten days under warm indoor conditions into tiny nymphs, which immediately seek a blood meal. Each of the five nymph stages must feed and then molt to advance, and under favorable warm conditions the whole path from egg to breeding adult takes roughly a month to a month and a half. Because indoor temperatures stay warm year-round, bed bugs breed continuously indoors regardless of the Wisconsin season outside — the calendar outdoors is irrelevant to a bug living in a heated bedroom.

Two features of the biology make them especially tenacious. First, the **eggs are well protected** and are not reliably killed by many treatments — which is precisely why a follow-up is essential, to catch the nymphs that hatch from eggs that survived the first pass. Second, bed bugs can **survive a remarkably long time without feeding** — many months under the right conditions — so you cannot starve them out by leaving a room or a mattress unused; they simply wait for a host to return. Combined with their ability to hide in nearly invisible cracks and, across the country, their **widespread resistance to common pyrethroid insecticides**, this longevity and egg protection are the reasons bed bugs are so hard to eliminate and so unforgiving of a half-measure.

There is one weakness worth knowing, because it underpins part of the treatment and prevention advice: bed bugs and their eggs are **vulnerable to sustained high heat.** This is exactly why running infested bedding and clothing through a hot dryer kills any bugs and eggs riding along in the fabric, and it is the principle behind professional heat approaches. It is also why the flip side — trying to freeze or starve them out at home — is unreliable, since it depends on conditions household settings rarely sustain. Heat is the lever that works; the deep-hiding, resistant, patient rest of their biology is what makes everything else difficult.

One more behavioral note shapes how infestations play out. Bed bugs tend to **aggregate** — they cluster together in shared harborage rather than scattering evenly, drawn to the traces left by other bed bugs — which is why fecal spotting and cast skins pile up in specific seams and cracks rather than everywhere. That clustering is useful for inspection, because it concentrates the evidence, but it also means an infestation has a spatial structure that has to be found and treated in full. Miss an aggregation tucked behind a headboard or in a baseboard crack across the room, and it repopulates the bed within weeks.

## The season here: indoors, there's no off-season

People sometimes assume a Wisconsin winter will help with bed bugs. Indoors, it does nothing — bed bugs live in heated homes, apartments, and lodging where it is warm every month of the year, and they breed straight through January. There is no seasonal die-off in a bedroom.

The one seasonal pattern worth noting is a human one, tied to how bed bugs travel. Movement spreads bed bugs, so periods of heavy travel, lodging turnover, and moving — summer trips and vacations, the holiday travel season, college and rental move-in and move-out — tend to be when new infestations get introduced. A family returns from a summer road trip with a stowaway in the luggage; a used couch comes home; a tenant moves in with an infested mattress. So while the bugs themselves ignore the seasons, the human activity that seeds infestations does have rhythms, and staying alert around travel and used-item acquisition is worthwhile year-round but especially during those busy stretches.

## Local sources: how bed bugs arrive around Clintonville

Bed bugs get into homes and buildings around here the same way they do everywhere — by hitchhiking — but a few local avenues are worth naming.

**Travel and lodging** are the classic route. A stay in an infested hotel, motel, cabin rental, or someone else's infested home, and bed bugs ride home in luggage, bags, and clothing. This is the most common way a single-family home first gets them, and it is why unpacking and laundry habits after travel matter.

**Used and secondhand furniture** is a major local source, and rural Wisconsin's healthy culture of rummage sales, auctions, thrift, marketplace finds, and hand-me-downs raises the exposure. A used mattress, box spring, couch, recliner, or dresser can carry a hidden infestation home, as can secondhand clothing and electronics. Curbside "free" furniture is a particular gamble.

**Multi-unit and shared housing** — apartments, duplexes, senior and care facilities, and rentals — face the added route of spread between units through wall voids, shared plumbing and electrical chases, and shared laundry. An infestation in one unit can migrate to neighbors, which is why bed bugs in rental and institutional settings are a building-level problem, not just a single-unit one, and why they are a serious [commercial pest control](/commercial-pest-control/) concern for lodging and facilities.

**Visitors, caregivers, and returning family** can bring them in on their belongings — a guest's bag, a returning student's luggage, an item from an infested household. None of this involves any failing on the resident's part; it is simply how a hitchhiking insect moves through ordinary human life. The takeaway is that the sources are travel and used items and shared walls, not dirt — which is exactly why "cleaning harder" never prevents or cures bed bugs.

## Signs of an infestation

Catching bed bugs early makes them dramatically easier to eliminate, so it pays to know the signs. **Unexplained itchy welts** appearing overnight, especially on skin exposed during sleep and sometimes in lines or clusters, are often the first prompt — but remember bites alone aren't proof. The physical evidence is what confirms it: **dark fecal spots** (like smearing marker dots) along mattress seams and piping, on the box spring, on sheets, and in nearby cracks; **pale shed skins** near harborage; tiny **white eggs** cemented in seams and crevices; a **musty, sweetish odor** in heavier infestations; and **live bugs** — flat, reddish-brown, apple-seed-sized — tucked into the seams of the mattress and box spring, the joints of the frame and headboard, and the cracks around the bed. The concentration of evidence around the sleeping area, where they feed, is characteristic. If you find these signs, the right move is to stop and get a professional inspection rather than start spraying or hauling out furniture, both of which can make things worse.

## Health risk

Bed bugs occupy an unusual spot among pests: they are **not known to transmit disease to humans**, which genuinely sets them apart from mosquitoes, ticks, and filth flies. You are not going to catch an illness from a bed bug bite the way you might from those vectors. But it would be wrong to conclude they are harmless, because the harm they do is real and takes a different form. The **bites** themselves itch, can become secondarily infected from scratching, and in some people provoke notable allergic reactions. Heavy infestations mean many bites night after night. And the effect that most defines bed bugs is **psychological**: the anxiety, insomnia, and distress of being bitten in your own bed, of not feeling safe where you sleep, is a substantial toll, and it is one of the reasons an infestation deserves prompt, thorough treatment rather than being toughed out. Sleep loss and stress are health issues too. So the honest summary is that bed bugs are not a disease threat but are far from trivial, and the sooner they are eliminated the smaller the physical and mental cost.

## Why DIY usually fails

Bed bugs are, along with German cockroaches, the pest where do-it-yourself efforts fail most reliably and most expensively — and understanding why saves people from making the infestation worse.

**Resistance.** Bed bug populations across the country are widely resistant to the **pyrethroid** insecticides that dominate over-the-counter sprays and foggers. So the products most people reach for often simply don't kill the bugs, or kill only a fraction, while giving the impression that something was done.

**Foggers scatter them.** "Bug bombs" are actively counterproductive. The fog settles on exposed surfaces but cannot penetrate the deep seams, cracks, and voids where bed bugs actually hide, so it misses the population. Worse, the repellent action can drive bugs to disperse — deeper into walls, into adjoining rooms, and in an apartment into neighboring units — turning a contained infestation into a spread one. Foggers also don't kill the protected eggs.

**Deep hiding and the eggs.** Bed bugs are flat enough to wedge into nearly invisible cracks, and thorough treatment means reaching all of those harborage points — a skill and a completeness that spot-spraying a mattress doesn't achieve. And because the **eggs survive** many treatments and hatch days to weeks later, any approach without a properly timed follow-up leaves the next generation to rebuild.

**Panic mistakes.** Finally, the instinctive reactions often backfire: dragging an infested mattress or couch through the house and out to the curb spreads bugs along the route and into other rooms; moving to sleep on the couch or in another bedroom just spreads the infestation to a new area as the bugs follow the host; and blanketing everything in store-bought spray can drive bugs into new harborage and interfere with subsequent professional treatment. Bed bugs punish improvisation, which is why a disciplined professional process is the reliable path.

## Prevention and vigilance

Because bed bugs hitchhike, prevention is about vigilance rather than cleaning, and a few habits meaningfully lower your risk.

**When you travel**, keep luggage off the bed and floor (a hard-surfaced luggage rack or the bathroom is safer), and give the mattress seams and headboard area of a room a quick look for fecal spots and live bugs before settling in. On returning home, unpack away from the bedroom and run travel clothing through a hot dryer, which kills any stowaways.

**With used items**, inspect any secondhand furniture, mattress, or clothing carefully before bringing it inside, and be especially wary of curbside "free" furniture. A used bed frame or upholstered piece is a classic carrier. When in doubt, don't bring it in, or launder and inspect thoroughly first.

**At home**, reducing clutter around the bed gives bed bugs fewer places to hide and makes any early infestation easier to spot and treat — this is not about cleanliness for its own sake but about denying harborage. A **bed bug-proof mattress and box spring encasement** is a strong preventive and diagnostic tool: it traps any bugs inside where they can't feed, keeps new ones from harboring in the mattress, and makes inspection far easier. And catching an introduction early — acting the moment you see signs rather than waiting — is itself the best prevention against a small problem becoming a large one. In shared and rental housing, prompt reporting and a building-level response prevent spread between units.
