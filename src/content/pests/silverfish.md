## What silverfish are

The little silvery, teardrop-shaped insect that wriggles across the bathroom floor at night — or that you find stranded in the bottom of the tub in the morning — is a **silverfish**, *Lepisma saccharina*. The name captures it well: the body is covered in fine silvery-gray scales, it tapers from a broad front to a narrow rear like a fish or a carrot, and it moves with a quick, side-to-side, fish-like wriggle. It's one of the oldest kinds of insects on earth, essentially unchanged for hundreds of millions of years, and it has thrived alongside humans because our buildings supply the two things it needs: **humidity and starch**.

Those two requirements are the whole key to silverfish. They are wingless, they never developed the ability to conserve water well, and they can't survive or reproduce without high humidity — so they live in the dampest parts of a house. And they feed on **carbohydrates**, specifically the starch and cellulose in paper, cardboard, book bindings, wallpaper paste, and stored dry goods. Put those together and you have a pest perfectly adapted to a damp Northeast Wisconsin basement full of stored cardboard boxes, old books, and paper — which is exactly where we find them most.

The important reframe is that a silverfish problem is a **moisture problem with an appetite for your belongings**. Unlike a lot of pests, silverfish do real damage — they eat holes in and graze the surface off paper, documents, photographs, books, and wallpaper, and they get into stored food. But they can only do it where it's humid enough to support them. That's why every effective approach to silverfish comes back to the same lever: take away the humidity, and you take away the silverfish.

## How do you identify silverfish?

Silverfish are distinctive once you know the marks. Adults are about a **half inch to three-quarters of an inch long**, not counting the appendages, with a flattened, tapering, teardrop or carrot shape covered in metallic silvery-gray scales that rub off easily. The two features that clinch the identification are at the ends: **two long antennae at the front**, and **three long, thin tail-like appendages** (called cerci and a central filament) fanning out at the rear. That combination — silvery scaled body, tapered shape, three tails, and a wriggling fish-like motion — is unique among common household insects.

They have no wings and can't fly or jump; they get around by running and wriggling, quickly, and they head for cover the instant they're exposed to light. Immature silverfish look like smaller, sometimes paler versions of the adults and gain their full silvery scaling as they mature.

The main look-alike is the **firebrat**, a close relative that's mottled brown-and-gray rather than silvery and prefers hot spots — near furnaces, water heaters, and hot pipes — rather than the cool, damp areas silverfish favor. Control is the same for both, but in Northeast Wisconsin basements the cool-loving silverfish is much more common. Don't confuse silverfish with young [centipedes](/pest-library/centipedes/) (which have many legs and are predators) or with small beetles; the three tails and the silvery, scaled, tapered body make silverfish easy to pick out.

## How do silverfish live and breed in Wisconsin?

The silverfish life cycle is unusual in ways that directly shape how a problem develops and how you have to fight it. Two traits stand out: they're **slow to develop and long-lived**, and they **molt throughout their entire lives**, even as adults — a rarity among insects.

A female lays eggs in small batches in cracks, crevices, and protected damp spots. The young hatch looking like miniature adults and molt repeatedly as they grow. Development is slow, especially in less-than-ideal conditions — it can take up to two or three years to reach maturity — but silverfish compensate by living a long time, several years or more. Because they keep molting as adults, you'll find shed scales and skins near their harborage. This slow, long-lived pattern is why silverfish problems build quietly: a population establishes over a couple of years and is often well entrenched by the time anyone notices the damage.

The non-negotiable requirement is **humidity**. Silverfish thrive at relative humidity above roughly 75 percent and struggle to survive, and especially to reproduce, in dry conditions. This is the single most important fact about them for control purposes. They also prefer moderate temperatures — cool basements suit them, which is why they, not the heat-loving firebrats, dominate in our region. And they can survive long periods with little food, so starving them out isn't practical; the food is everywhere in a home anyway, in the form of paper and starch.

Because silverfish live entirely indoors in the controlled climate of a heated home, they have **no seasonal die-off** — they're a year-round, self-sustaining population rather than an invader that comes and goes with the weather. That's a key difference from most Wisconsin pests. Outdoors they can live under bark, in leaf litter, and in similar damp spots, but the silverfish causing you trouble are living inside, in your basement and walls, breeding on their own schedule regardless of the season outside.

A few more survival traits are worth spelling out, because they explain why silverfish problems are so persistent and so easy to underestimate. Silverfish are extraordinarily **hardy feeders** — they can survive for months, in some cases up to a year, with no food at all, drawing on stored reserves, which means you can't starve them out and a population can ride out lean stretches you'd assume had killed it off. They're intensely **nocturnal and secretive**, hiding deep in cracks, voids, and stored materials by day and emerging only in the dark, so the handful you see is almost always a small fraction of the true population. And they're fast and flat, able to squeeze into the thinnest cracks in a foundation wall or a bookshelf. Put those traits together — long-lived, slow-breeding, drought-of-food-tolerant, hidden, and crack-dwelling — and you have an insect that establishes quietly, hides most of itself, and hangs on tenaciously. It's why a silverfish problem almost never announces itself early, and almost never resolves with a single quick fix or one round of treatment.

## When are silverfish active in Northeast Wisconsin?

Silverfish are a **year-round** presence in a home that suits them, but there's still a seasonal rhythm to when Northeast Wisconsin homeowners notice them and to how much humidity is available to fuel them.

**Spring** brings snowmelt and saturated ground, and in the block-and-fieldstone basements common in this region, that means rising humidity in the lower level — good conditions for silverfish coming out of a quieter winter. **Summer** is the peak. Warm, muggy Wisconsin summers push basement and crawlspace humidity to its highest, and without a dehumidifier a lot of local basements sit well above the threshold silverfish need. This is when populations grow fastest and when people report the most sightings and the most fresh paper damage. **Fall** stays favorable as long as the dampness holds, and it's also when people start using basements and closets more and moving stored boxes, which is often how they discover a problem that's been building all summer.

**Winter** is the interesting one. You'd think cold would help, but the opposite can be true for the humidity picture: a tightly closed, heated home can actually trap moisture, and areas near water heaters, laundry, and poorly ventilated bathrooms stay humid all winter. Silverfish in the heated envelope don't slow down the way an outdoor pest would. So a silverfish sighting in January is, like a winter centipede, a clear sign that the conditions indoors — not the season — are what's sustaining them.

The pattern to take away: silverfish track indoor humidity, which peaks with spring melt and summer mugginess but never fully goes away in a damp home. A basement that stays humid year-round gives you silverfish year-round.

## What conditions bring silverfish to a property here?

This is where the Clintonville area's housing stock and climate do the work, because Northeast and Central Wisconsin homes offer silverfish nearly ideal conditions.

**Block and fieldstone foundations** — standard in the region's older farmhouses and village homes — wick and hold moisture and keep basements humid, and they're full of cracks and voids for silverfish to shelter and lay eggs in. **Crawlspaces with bare dirt floors** under older additions and cabins are constantly humid from ground moisture, another prime silverfish habitat. **Poor drainage, high water tables** near the river drainages, and the region's damp climate keep the lower levels of a lot of local homes above the humidity silverfish need for much of the year.

Then there's the storage pattern, which is really the second half of the equation. People use basements, closets, and attics to store exactly what silverfish eat: **cardboard boxes, old books, documents, photographs, newspapers, and paper keepsakes**, along with dry pantry goods. A damp basement full of stored cardboard is a silverfish buffet and breeding ground combined. The starch in book bindings, the paste behind old wallpaper, the sizing in stored fabrics — all of it feeds them. On older homes, original wallpaper and its starch paste can be a food source built into the walls.

Seasonal **lake cabins** around the Waupaca Chain O' Lakes deserve special mention. A cabin closed up cold and unventilated over the winter and spring takes on ground and lake moisture with no heat or airflow to drive it off, so it can hold high humidity for months — and cabins are full of stored paper, books, and cardboard. Owners frequently open up in spring or summer to find silverfish established in the damp, stored belongings. It's one of the most consistent silverfish situations we see, and a big reason we recommend an inspection at cabin opening.

## How do you know you have silverfish?

Silverfish leave clearer evidence than many pests, because they damage what they feed on. The signs:

- **The insects themselves**, silvery and wriggling, seen at night or trapped in tubs, sinks, and basins in the morning (they fall onto smooth surfaces and can't climb out).
- **Feeding damage on paper and cardboard**: irregular holes, notched edges, and a grazed or etched surface where the top layer has been scraped away, on stored boxes, books, documents, wallpaper, and photographs.
- **Yellowish stains and shed scales** on paper, in boxes, and in drawers, along with the fine silvery scales that rub off their bodies.
- **Tiny black droppings** resembling ground pepper near the damage and in harborage areas.
- **Damage concentrated in damp, dark storage areas** — basements, closets, bathrooms, and under-sink cabinets — which points straight at the humidity driving the problem.

Because the damage is cumulative and the population builds slowly, finding grazed paper and shed scales usually means silverfish have been present a while.

## Are silverfish dangerous?

Silverfish are one of the pests where the risk is real but specific: they don't threaten your health, but they do **damage your belongings**. They don't bite, sting, spread disease, or endanger people or pets. What they do is eat and deface the things we store — and some of those things are irreplaceable.

The **property damage** is the main concern. Silverfish eat holes in and graze the surface off paper, books, documents, letters, photographs, and archival materials; they damage book bindings by eating the glue; they feed on wallpaper and its paste, sometimes leaving telltale grazed patches on the wall; and they get into stored dry goods like flour, cereal, and pet food, contaminating them. For anyone storing family records, old photos, books, or keepsakes in a basement, attic, or closet, silverfish are a genuine threat to those items, and because the damage accumulates slowly and quietly, a lot of harm can be done before it's noticed.

There's also a modest **indoor-air consideration** in heavy, long-standing infestations: the shed scales and skins silverfish leave behind can contribute to allergen load, and some sensitive people react to them. This is a minor issue compared with the property damage, but it's a reason not to let a large population persist.

So the case for control is protecting your belongings and, secondarily, keeping a damp, allergen-shedding population out of your living space. And as with the other moisture pests, a silverfish problem is often the first clear symptom of a **humidity problem** that will eventually cause mold, wood rot, and other pest issues if it isn't addressed — so they double as an early warning about the health of your home's lower level.

## Why doesn't DIY work on silverfish?

People tend to fight silverfish with sticky traps and sprays, and while those catch some individuals, they rarely resolve the problem, for reasons rooted in the biology.

The core issue is that DIY measures **don't touch the humidity**, which is the actual cause. You can trap and kill the silverfish crossing your bathroom floor, but the population is breeding in the wall voids, the cracks in the block foundation, and the damp cardboard boxes you can't easily reach — all in a space that stays humid enough to keep them going. Kill the visible ones and the hidden population, which is the bulk of it, simply replaces them.

Silverfish are also **slow-breeding and long-lived**, which fools people into thinking a treatment worked when the population is just rebuilding quietly over months. And they can survive long stretches with little food, so you can't starve them out, and there's starch everywhere in a home anyway.

The other DIY failure is treating the symptom instead of the system. Homeowners kill silverfish and never address the **moisture, the food storage, or the harborage** that make the space livable. A damp basement full of stored cardboard will keep producing silverfish no matter how many traps you set. Lasting control means lowering the humidity below what they need, removing and better-storing their food, decluttering their harborage, and treating the cracks and voids where they breed — a coordinated approach that a package of traps was never going to accomplish.

## How do you keep silverfish out?

Preventing silverfish is, first and last, about controlling humidity — plus denying them food and harborage and sealing them out of living spaces. The practical steps:

**Dry out the damp areas.** Run a properly sized dehumidifier in basements and crawlspaces and aim to hold relative humidity below about 50 percent — the single most effective anti-silverfish measure. Fix plumbing and water-heater leaks, insulate sweating pipes, ventilate bathrooms and laundry rooms, vent the dryer outdoors, and lay a vapor barrier over bare crawlspace dirt.

**Fix drainage outside.** Clean and extend gutters and downspouts to discharge away from the foundation and correct grading that slopes toward the house, so the basement wall stays drier from the outside in.

**Store food and paper properly.** Move books, documents, photographs, and keepsakes out of damp basements and attics, or into sealed plastic containers rather than cardboard boxes. Keep pantry dry goods in sealed containers. Reducing accessible starch shrinks what silverfish can live on and protects the items that matter.

**Declutter and seal.** Clear out cardboard, old paper, and clutter that gives silverfish harborage on damp floors. Caulk and seal cracks in foundations and around baseboards, pipes, and utility penetrations to cut harborage and slow movement between rooms.

Do those things and the space drops below the humidity silverfish must have, loses the food they graze, and offers fewer places to hide — and a slow-breeding, moisture-dependent insect can't sustain a population against all three.
