## What boxelder bugs are, and how to identify them

Boxelder bugs are one of the three insects that define fall in Northeast Wisconsin, alongside [cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/) and [Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/). If you have watched a knot of black-and-red bugs crawl slowly across the sunny side of your house on an October afternoon, or found them creeping across the inside of a window on a warm February day, you know them even if you did not know the name.

The insect is *Boisea trivittata*. The adult is about half an inch long, elongated and flat, and mostly charcoal to black, with three distinct red or orange stripes running lengthwise on the thorax behind the head and red lines edging the wings, which fold flat over the back in an X pattern. The overall impression is a dark bug with bright red trim. The immatures, called nymphs, are a very different look — small and bright red with darker heads, wingless, and getting darker and more adult-looking as they grow. Seeing bright red nymphs and black-and-red adults together on a wall in late summer is a dead giveaway.

People most often confuse them with brown marmorated stink bugs, the other overwintering nuisance moving into our region. The distinction is easy once you look: boxelder bugs are long, narrow, and boldly black-and-red; stink bugs are shield-shaped and a mottled, camouflage brown. Both are fall invaders, both pack into wall voids for winter, and BugBoss treats both the same way — but knowing which you have helps confirm the pattern. If you are unsure, identification is part of what the [BugBoss residential service](/residential-pest-control/) sorts out before recommending a plan.

## Wisconsin biology and lifecycle: tied to the seed trees

Boxelder bugs live and die by the seeds of the female boxelder tree, *Acer negundo*, a fast-growing, weedy maple relative that is common along fencerows, riverbanks, disturbed ground, and old yards throughout our region — you will find it all along the Pigeon, Wolf, and Embarrass river corridors and in nearly every rural windbreak. Boxelders are dioecious, meaning trees are either male or female, and only the female trees produce the winged seeds the bugs feed on. That single fact drives a lot of the pattern: a property with a big seed-bearing female boxelder can be swarmed while a neighbor with only male trees sees far fewer. The bugs will also feed to a lesser degree on maple and ash seeds, but boxelder is the mainstay, and they typically cannot complete their full life cycle on the alternate hosts.

The season runs in two generations in Wisconsin. Adults that overwintered emerge in spring, and after feeding the females lay clusters of small red eggs on host trees and nearby surfaces. Those eggs hatch in roughly ten days to two weeks into the bright red nymphs, which feed and molt through the summer and mature into a first generation of adults by mid-summer. Those adults produce a second generation, so by late summer the population on and around a good host tree can be enormous, with all life stages present at once. It is that late-summer peak population that turns into the fall invasion.

Because they depend on the seed trees outdoors, boxelder bugs cannot reproduce inside your house — there is nothing for them to feed on or breed on indoors. Every bug you see over winter is an adult that came in during the fall. That is genuinely good news, because it means the indoor population cannot grow on its own; it can only reflect how many got in before you closed the gaps.

## The boxelder bug season in Northeast Wisconsin

Through spring and summer, boxelder bugs are a tree insect, cycling through two generations on and around the boxelders, maples, and ash in the area. Most homeowners barely notice them in this phase unless a heavily infested tree sits right against the house.

The problem announces itself in fall. As nights cool through September and into October, the season's large adult population leaves the trees and seeks a protected place to overwinter. They gather in growing numbers on warm, sun-exposed surfaces — and the south and west walls of buildings, which hold the afternoon heat longest as the air cools, are irresistible. You get the classic sight of bugs massed on the sunny siding, sometimes hundreds of them, drifting slowly upward and probing every crack. This gathering period, roughly September into early October, is the critical window for control, because the bugs are exposed on the exterior walls and have not yet worked inside.

From those walls they push into the structure through gaps around windows and doors, cracks in siding and foundation, soffit and fascia junctions, and utility penetrations, settling into wall voids, attics, and other sheltered spaces to go dormant. Through the cold of winter they stay hidden and inactive. Then, exactly like cluster flies, they betray themselves on warm days: a sunny afternoon or the heat of the furnace rising into a wall void rouses them, and they crawl toward the light, ending up on the inside of windows and in the living space. Come spring, the survivors work back outside to feed and start the two-generation cycle over again.

## The fall-invader complex: how boxelder bugs fit in

Boxelder bugs almost never show up alone in Northeast Wisconsin. They are one member of a group of insects — the fall invaders, or "overwintering pests" — that all do the same thing at the same time: leave the outdoors as fall cools, gather on warm sunny walls, and push into buildings to spend the winter dormant, then reappear on warm days. Understanding the group matters, because the control approach is the same for all of them, and treating them one species at a time makes no sense when they are exploiting the same walls and the same gaps together.

The other regular members here are [cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-flies/), which come off the earthworms in the surrounding soil; [Asian lady beetles](/pest-library/asian-lady-beetles/), the orange, ladybug-like beetles that can nip and leave yellow stains; and, increasingly, the **brown marmorated stink bug**, the shield-shaped brown invader that has been spreading through Wisconsin. What separates boxelder bugs from the group is the food source: cluster flies are tied to worms, lady beetles to aphids, and boxelder bugs to the seeds of specific trees. But the fall behavior is identical, and so is the fix.

That is why, when we treat a home for boxelder bugs in September, we are almost always treating the whole complex in the same visit — one properly timed exterior application to the sunny walls, soffits, and roofline, plus one round of exclusion, addresses boxelder bugs, cluster flies, lady beetles, and stink bugs at once. A homeowner who chases only the black-and-red bugs while ignoring the beetles and flies on the same wall is doing a third of the job. The sensible unit of work is the wall and the season, not the single species.

## Where boxelder bugs hit hardest around Clintonville

The properties that get overrun are the ones with the food source and the warm walls close together. A home with a large, seed-bearing female boxelder in the yard — or a maple or ash — and a big south- or west-facing wall is the textbook heavy case, because the bugs feed all summer just yards from the surface they will overwinter on. Boxelders love disturbed ground, fencerows, and riverbanks, so properties along the Pigeon, Wolf, and Embarrass corridors, and rural yards with old windbreaks and volunteer trees, tend to carry more of them.

**Older farmhouses** get the worst of it for the same reasons they get cluster flies: big two-story sun-collecting walls, decades of settlement gaps, and balloon-framed cavities that let a bug entering at a soffit end up dormant behind a first-floor wall. **Seasonal cabins** around Iola and the Chain O' Lakes make ideal quiet overwintering sites, so an owner opening up in spring often finds bugs at every window. And **light-colored siding** anywhere is a stronger draw, because the bugs orient to the warmth the wall radiates — a pale, sun-baked south wall can be covered while a shaded north wall a few feet away has almost none.

## Signs of an infestation

The signs follow the season. In late summer and fall, the clearest sign is the bugs themselves massed on the sunny south and west walls, often with the bright red nymphs mixed among the black-and-red adults. Reddish or rust-colored fecal spotting on siding, especially light-colored siding, and on curtains, blinds, and walls near where they gather is a common and telling sign — those stains are hard to remove and are one of the real nuisances of a heavy population. Indoors, bugs appearing at windows and in living spaces on warm winter and early-spring days point to an established overwintering population in the walls. And if you have a large female boxelder tree on or near the property, especially one throwing heavy seed, treat that as a standing risk factor even before you see bugs on the house.

## Health and structural risk

Boxelder bugs are, like cluster flies, essentially a nuisance pest, and it is worth being straight about that. They do not bite people or pets — their mouthparts are made for piercing plant seeds, not skin. They do not sting, do not transmit disease, do not infest or contaminate food, and do not breed indoors. They do not chew or bore, so they cause no structural damage to wood, wiring, or insulation.

The genuine downsides are staining and sheer numbers. Their reddish fecal spots stain drapes, curtains, blinds, carpet, and light-colored walls and siding, and those marks can be difficult to clean. Crushing a bug — which happens constantly when there are many — leaves a stain and releases a mild, unpleasant odor, which is why vacuuming rather than squashing is the right way to handle the ones indoors. In very large numbers they are simply hard to live with over a long winter of repeated cleanup. So the case for control is not danger; it is comfort, cleanliness, and keeping a manageable nuisance from becoming a season-long one.

One thing worth knowing: a heavy boxelder bug population on a wall in fall is also a fair indicator that the same wall is letting in the rest of the fall-invader complex. The bugs are big and visible, so they are often the first thing a homeowner notices, but the gaps they are using are the same ones the cluster flies and lady beetles are quietly slipping through. In that sense the boxelder bugs are a useful early warning — if they are massing on your siding in September, that is the signal to treat and seal before the whole group is dug into the walls for winter.

## Why DIY usually fails

The typical homeowner approach fails for the same reasons it does with all the fall invaders: wrong timing, wrong location, and hidden numbers. People wait until bugs appear inside on a warm winter day and then spray them, but by then the insects are already deep in the wall voids, the ones in view are a fraction of the total, and spraying the interior puts pesticide in your living space for very little gain. Vacuuming handles the visible bugs more cleanly and does just as much.

The window that actually matters — the fall gathering on the exterior walls — comes and goes while most people are not thinking about bugs at all, and by the time they call, in late October or November, the insects have moved inside and the moment has passed. Exterior sprays sold at the hardware store also break down quickly in sun and weather and rarely have the residual staying power to cover the whole gathering period on a large wall.

Exclusion is the durable half of the answer, and it is the part homeowners most often underestimate. Sealing the actual entry points on an older farmhouse — the soffit and fascia gaps, the seams where materials meet, the gaps around window frames, the openings where a balloon-framed wall runs open into the attic — takes knowing where the bugs really get in and being willing to work the roofline and upper structure, not just the obvious ground-floor cracks. A partial seal leaves the routes that matter open. It is the same building-envelope work that underlies rodent and cluster fly exclusion, and it is not a quick caulk-gun afternoon on a two-story house.

## Prevention and exclusion

Prevention is mostly exclusion plus a bit of landscape awareness. On the building, seal in summer before the fall gathering: caulk gaps around window and door frames, seal where siding meets the foundation and where utility lines penetrate the walls, close soffit and fascia gaps, repair cracks in foundation and siding, and fix torn screens and worn door sweeps. Every gap closed before September is one the bugs cannot use.

On the landscape side, the one lever you have is the seed trees. If a large female boxelder sits right against the house and throws heavy seed every year, removing or replacing it can meaningfully cut the local population — but weigh that against the fact that the bugs fly in from neighbors' trees and from maples and ash, so tree removal is rarely a complete fix on its own and often is not worth taking down a healthy tree. Male boxelders, which do not produce the seeds, are far less of a draw. For most homes, the realistic and effective plan is not to fight the trees but to seal the building and treat the walls at the right time, accepting that the goal is keeping the bugs out of the walls and living space rather than eliminating them from the landscape.
